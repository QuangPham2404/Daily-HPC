## 0. Concise Summary

Today's issue is a bridge. The first half finishes GPU scheduling by looking **below the software API**: what actually happens between `cudaMemcpyAsync()` returning and data moving. The answer is a set of *hardware engines* — compute engines and copy/DMA engines — each with its own in-order queue, coordinated by semaphores. Understanding why same-direction copies serialize, why a blocked operation stalls its whole engine queue, and why copy engines have nothing to do with global-to-shared-memory movement explains most real-world stream behavior.

The second half pivots to the other half of every GPU server: the CPU. Module 6 starts at the core — out-of-order execution, branch prediction, and speculative execution, the machinery that makes a CPU *latency*-optimized where a GPU is *throughput*-optimized — then works outward through the cache hierarchy (L1/L2/L3, cache lines, MESI coherence, false sharing), the integrated memory controller and DRAM timing (how 16 channels of DDR5 turn into 1.6 TB/s), and finally sockets and NUMA, where memory access cost depends on which socket owns the page.

The two developments are the two poles of this story. AMD's EPYC "Venice" (Zen 6, announced July 2026, shipping Q4 2026) pushes the throughput philosophy to 256 cores, 16 DDR5 channels, and the first PCIe Gen 6 server CPU — with a DMA offload engine (SDXI) and packet-to-cache injection that are today's queue concepts reborn inside the CPU. NVIDIA's Vera CPU does the opposite: 88 latency-optimized Arm cores, LPDDR5X, and a coherent NVLink-C2C fabric that makes the CPU and GPU behave like one NUMA domain instead of two machines joined by PCIe. Between them, every concept in today's lesson appears in shipping silicon.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

Module 5 established what runs *on* the GPU: threads, warps, blocks, registers, shared memory, occupancy, and concurrent kernels. What we have not examined is the machinery *around* the SMs — how work gets from your program to the hardware, and why certain overlaps are possible while others are not. Today's first topic closes that gap. Then the curriculum deliberately turns to the CPU: a GPU server is not a GPU with accessories, it is a *CPU system* (cores, caches, memory controllers, sockets) with accelerators attached at specific points. You cannot reason about GPU data movement, bandwidth, or placement without understanding the host side it moves through.

### 1.1 Hardware queues and asynchronous engines (GPU-5.7)

**The problem.** When you call `cudaMemcpyAsync()` or launch a kernel into a stream, the call returns in microseconds, but the actual work — a DMA transfer across PCIe, or a grid of blocks executing — takes milliseconds. Something must hold that work between "issued" and "done," order it, track dependencies, and hand it to the right hardware unit. That something is a set of hardware work queues feeding specialized engines.

**The submission path.** The runtime (CUDA driver, or on AMD the ROCm runtime) builds command buffers in host memory. The GPU's *front end* — AMD calls it the Command Processor (CP) — fetches commands from these buffers and distributes them to the GPU's blocks. The Linux amdgpu driver documents the mechanism precisely: communication with the engines uses **ring buffers** operating as a producer-consumer system. Software (the driver, acting for your application) writes commands into the ring and advances a **write pointer (wptr)**; the engine reads commands and advances a **read pointer (rptr)**. When `rptr == wptr` the ring is idle. Because a command can point at an *indirect buffer* elsewhere in memory, the small ring can reference arbitrarily large command streams — the ring holds doorbells and pointers, not the commands themselves.

On AMD specifically, compute work flows through **AQL (Architected Queuing Language) queues** — rings of fixed 64-byte packets, one packet per kernel dispatch, created via the KFD driver interface — while bulk data movement flows through entirely separate **SDMA queues**. `hipMemcpyAsync`, peer-to-peer copies, and staging traffic are submitted to SDMA rings, not to compute queues; a kernel dispatch and a DMA copy ride different hardware units. That physical separation is the foundation of overlap.

**The CUDA mapping.** CUDA exposes this hardware through *streams*. A stream is a sequence of operations that execute in issue order; operations in *different* streams have no prescribed order and may run concurrently. Underneath, the driver dispatches each operation to an engine queue: kernels to the compute engine queue, transfers to copy engine queues. NVIDIA's Streams and Concurrency material states the dispatch rules directly, and they explain most observed behavior:

1. An operation is dispatched from its engine queue when all *preceding operations in the same stream* have completed,
2. all *preceding operations in the same engine queue* have been dispatched, and
3. the required resources (registers, shared memory, blocks) are available.

Two consequences follow. **Within an engine, execution is strictly in-order** — the engine does not reorder or skip ahead. **Between engines, dependencies are maintained** (by hardware semaphore signals), so a copy in stream 1 correctly waits for the kernel in stream 1 that produced its data, even though copy and kernel sit in different queues. The dark side is **head-of-line blocking**: a blocked operation blocks everything behind it in that engine queue, even operations from other streams that are ready to run.

**A short history of copy engines.** The oldest Tesla GPUs (C1060 class) had a single copy engine. Because the engine is in-order, an interleaved issue pattern (H2D for chunk 1, D2H for chunk 1, H2D for chunk 2, ...) forced the D2H of chunk 1 to wait behind the H2D of chunk 2 — no overlap with the kernel, because *the copy engine itself was the serialization point*. Batching all H2D copies before all D2H copies restored overlap. The C2050 (Fermi) added a **second copy engine, one per direction**, so H2D and D2H never block each other; NVIDIA's blog on overlapping data transfers walks through exactly this arithmetic (their 12 ms sequential example dropping to ~8.7 ms or ~7.5 ms depending on engine count and issue order). Kepler's **Hyper-Q** (compute capability 3.5) then widened the compute side from a single hardware queue to 32, eliminating launch-order sensitivity entirely.

Modern hardware: an H100 reports `asyncEngineCount = 3`, and NVIDIA engineers clarify that the driver assigns engines to *directions* — H2D, D2H, and peer-to-peer. Two caveats matter:

- **Same-direction transfers serialize, and this is by design.** PCIe is a fixed-bandwidth pipe; one engine already saturates it. Running three H2D copies "in parallel" would not move more bytes per second than running them back-to-back. Copy engines are built to saturate the interface, not to scale to device memory bandwidth.
- **Device-to-device copies do not use copy engines at all.** They are executed by internal kernels on the SMs. And copy engines have *nothing* to do with moving data from global memory to shared memory — that is done by your kernel's own load instructions, or on Ampere+ by the `cp.async` instruction, or on Hopper by the **TMA unit** (Tensor Memory Accelerator), a distinct on-chip engine that asynchronously moves whole multi-dimensional tensor tiles between HBM and shared memory with descriptor-based addressing. Three different "async copy" mechanisms, three different hardware units — a CUDA API call crossing PCIe, a kernel's own global loads, and TMA bulk tiles. Conflating them is the most common conceptual error in this area.

**What overlap requires.** For copy/compute overlap you need three things: a device with "concurrent copy and execution" (essentially everything modern), the kernel and the transfer in **different, non-default streams**, and **pinned (page-locked) host memory** — because the DMA engine needs physical addresses that cannot change mid-transfer, a requirement we will treat fully in GPU-7.4. The default ("null") stream is special: in its legacy form, it synchronizes with all other streams on the device, so issuing anything into it serializes the whole pipeline.

**The canonical HPC pattern.** Everything above converges on one picture, the chunked pipeline used by stencil codes, HPCG-style sparse kernels, and multi-batch GEMM streaming:

```text
stream i:   [ H2D chunk i ]──[ kernel chunk i ]──[ D2H chunk i ]

issue order:  H2D(0) K(0) D2H(0) H2D(1) K(1) D2H(1) H2D(2) ...

engine view:
  copy engine (H2D):   H2D(0)      H2D(1)        H2D(2) ...
  compute engine:            K(0)        K(1)        K(2) ...
  copy engine (D2H):               D2H(0)      D2H(1) ...

steady state:  H2D(i+1) ∥ K(i) ∥ D2H(i-1)   — three engines busy at once
```

With one engine per direction this actually achieves the three-way overlap: the H2D of chunk 2, the kernel of chunk 1, and the D2H of chunk 0 run simultaneously. Fail any of the three requirements (default stream, pageable memory, same-direction grouping on a one-engine device) and the timeline collapses back to serial. Practical wisdom from the tuning literature: a *few fat streams* with large chunks beat many tiny streams, because each chunk transfer has fixed overheads and the engine queues are in-order — and `cudaStreamQuery()` between kernels can force the signal flush that delayed kernel-completion signals would otherwise defer.

This topic completes the scheduling picture of module 5: occupancy governs what fits *on an SM*, concurrent kernels (GPU-5.6) govern what shares *the SM array*, and hardware queues and engines govern what the *whole device* can do at once — compute, plus one transfer in each direction, is the ceiling.

### 1.2 CPU core architecture in contrast to a GPU (GPU-6.1)

**The design inversion.** A CPU core and an SM answer the same question — "how do I keep execution units busy when memory is slow?" — in opposite ways. The GPU answers with *parallelism*: keep thousands of threads resident, and when a warp stalls on memory, switch to another warp (GPU-5.1, GPU-5.2). The CPU answers with *speculation and caching*: make one thread progress as fast as possible by predicting what it will need next — future instructions, future data, future branch directions — and being right almost all the time.

**Out-of-order execution.** A modern CPU core fetches and decodes instructions in program order, but executes them when their *operands* are ready, not when their turn comes. The machinery (descending from Tomasulo's reservation stations, 1967): instructions are **renamed** onto physical registers, removing false dependencies (two instructions writing the same architectural register no longer conflict); they wait in reservation stations until operands arrive on result buses; they execute on any free functional unit; and results are held in a **reorder buffer (ROB)** until the instruction reaches the head of the buffer, at which point it *commits* — irrevocably updating architectural state. In-order commit is what makes precise exceptions possible: when something goes wrong, there is a clean boundary between "executed" and "officially done."

**Branch prediction and speculation.** A deeply pipelined, wide superscalar core fetches 6–8 instructions per cycle, but roughly one in five is a branch. If the core had to wait for a branch to *resolve* (deep in the pipeline) before fetching past it, it would starve. So the front end **predicts** branch direction and target, fetches speculatively down the predicted path, and the out-of-order machinery executes that speculative work. Predictions are trained statistical models of past behavior (two-bit saturating counters, history-based predictors, modern TAGE-class designs), and accuracy on typical code is well above 95%, often above 99%. Intel's own measurements (Lin & Tarsa, *Branch Prediction Is Not a Solved Problem*) show the remaining mispredictions are concentrated in a small number of systematically hard-to-predict branches and rare branches — and that even growing a state-of-the-art predictor's storage eight-fold recovers only ~2.7% IPC, evidence of how mature the field is. A misprediction costs a pipeline flush: all speculative work after the branch is discarded (the ROB is cleared past it), costing on the order of 15–20 cycles on modern parts — and all the energy that speculative work consumed.

**Why the CPU looks like this.** Branch predictors, rename tables, reservation stations, ROBs, and schedulers are enormous consumers of silicon area and power relative to the arithmetic they enable. That is the trade: a CPU spends most of a core's budget on *control* so that a single thread runs fast; a GPU spends nearly all of it on *lanes* so that throughput per watt and per mm² is high, and tolerates individual threads being slow. This is also why the CPU carries large caches and aggressive hardware prefetchers — another latency-hiding device — while the GPU relies on resident warps instead (its caches exist mainly to *feed bandwidth*, e.g. coalescing in L1/L2, not to hide most latency).

**A reality check on the gap.** The famous "GPUs are 100× faster" claims were largely an artifact of untuned baselines. The ISCA 2010 study *Debunking the 100X GPU vs. CPU Myth* optimized throughput kernels for *both* a Core i7 and a GTX 280 and found the GPU ahead by about **2.5×**, not 100× — the CPU's caches, prefetchers, SIMD width, and multi-threading closed most of the gap on regular throughput kernels. The honest summary: for latency-bound, control-heavy, or irregular code the CPU wins outright; for regular data-parallel arithmetic the GPU wins decisively; for tuned regular kernels both are "in the same ballpark" with the GPU ahead.

**Why a node needs both.** This is not sentiment; it is a division of labor visible in every stack: the CPU runs the OS, the MPI layer, the CUDA runtime, launches kernels, packs and stages buffers, and executes every serial or irregular phase (sparse factorizations, adaptive meshing, graph algorithms, the panel logic of dense factorizations); the GPU executes the regular data-parallel kernels. The CPU's latency machinery is what makes the *rest of the system* responsive — including the dispatch path of section 1.1, which is ordinary CPU code plus ring-buffer writes.

### 1.3 CPU cache hierarchy (GPU-6.2)

**Why a hierarchy exists.** SRAM cells are fast but large, power-hungry, and expensive per bit; DRAM is dense but ~100× slower. No single physical memory can be both, so the hardware stages data through a pyramid, relying on **locality** — temporal (recently used data is used again) and spatial (nearby data is used soon) — to keep most accesses at the fast levels:

| Level | Typical size | Typical latency | Shared by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registers | ~1 KB visible | 1 cycle | one thread |
| L1 (split I/D) | 32–64 KB | ~4–5 cycles (~1 ns) | one core |
| L2 | 512 KB–2 MB | ~14 cycles (~4 ns) | one core |
| L3 / LLC | 32–1024 MB | ~45–50 cycles (~15 ns) | all cores in a socket |
| DRAM | 100s of GB | ~250 cycles (~80–100 ns) | system |

A thread that stalls on DRAM wastes the equivalent of several hundred instruction slots. Prefetchers partially hide this for *predictable* patterns: stride-1 streaming achieves a large fraction of peak DRAM bandwidth because the prefetcher runs ahead of demand; random gather/scatter collapses to a small fraction because every access is a new cache line and nothing is predictable.

**Mechanism: the cache line.** Data moves in fixed **64-byte lines** on all current x86 and Arm server parts (Apple uses 128 bytes — a legitimate divergence, not a constant of nature). One line holds exactly 8 doubles. Reading one double fetches all eight — amortize over all eight and the transfer is free; use one and discard seven and you paid 8× for your data. An address decomposes into tag, set index, and offset: a 32 KB 8-way L1 with 64 B lines has 512 lines in 64 sets, so bits 0–5 of the address select the byte in line, bits 6–11 select the set, and the rest is the tag compared against the 8 ways in parallel.

**Misses, classified** (Mark Hill's 3C taxonomy plus coherence): **compulsory** (first touch — fix with prefetch or warm-up), **capacity** (working set exceeds the cache — fix with blocking/tiling), **conflict** (hot lines fight for the same set — associativity or data layout), and **coherence** (another core invalidated your line). This taxonomy is *actionable*: `perf stat -e LLC-load-misses` and `perf c2c` attribute your misses, and the fix differs by kind. The single most important algorithmic response is **cache blocking**, which the HPL theme has already shown for GEMM: iterate over tiles small enough that A, B, C panels stay resident in L2/L3, so the O(n³) arithmetic reuses each O(n²) datum many times before it evicts. The same idea governs sparse kernels (register blocking, section reordering) and stencil sweeps.

**Coherence: MESI.** With many private caches over one DRAM, hardware maintains the invariant that a load returns a sane value given other cores' stores. Each line carries a state — **M**odified (dirty, exclusive), **E**xclusive (clean, exclusive), **S**hared (clean, replicated), **I**nvalid — and cores exchange invalidation and transfer messages on transitions (a read of a line another core holds in M forces a writeback and demotion to S; a store to an S line broadcasts invalidations). Intel adds an F state (MESIF: one shared copy is designated the responder so DRAM is not disturbed); AMD adds an O state (MOESI: a dirty line can be shared directly from the owner cache without writeback). Coherence operates at *cache-line granularity*, which produces **false sharing**: two threads incrementing two adjacent 8-byte counters on the same 64-byte line will invalidate each other on every store — observed slowdowns of 10–100×, cured by padding each counter to its own line. This is a *purely architectural* disease: the program has no logical sharing at all.

**LLC design and the chiplet twist.** An L3 can be **inclusive** (holds a superset of L1/L2 contents — simple snoop filtering, but each core's L2 effectively shrinks usable LLC), **exclusive** (AMD's classic choice — full combined capacity), or **non-inclusive non-exclusive (NINE**, Intel post-Skylake-X, with a separate snoop filter). On chiplet CPUs (AMD Zen), L3 lives *per CCD* — e.g. 32 MB per 8-core cluster on Zen 4, 128 MB per 32-core CCD on the new Zen 6c server dies — so "the L3" is really several coherence/locality domains under one heatspreader, which flows directly into today's last topic.

**The TLB.** Every memory access is translated virtual→physical through the **TLB**, a tiny fully-associative cache of page-table entries. With 4 KB pages, even a large TLB covers only tens of MB; a large-array sweep can miss the TLB on every page boundary, each miss costing a page-table walk of ~100+ cycles. **Huge pages** (2 MB, 1 GB) multiply coverage by 512×/262144× — routinely worth double-digit percent on large-footprint HPC codes, which is why transparent hugepages and `mmap(MAP_HUGETLB)` matter on compute nodes.

GPU connections, briefly: the GPU's L1/shared per SM and L2 per partition mirror the *bandwidth-feeding* role, but there is no MESI-style coherence between SMs' L1s, and — critically for module 7 — no coherence between CPU caches and GPU caches across the PCIe boundary, which is why CPU↔GPU sharing requires explicit transfers or managed migration with page-level visibility rules (GPU-7.8).

### 1.4 CPU memory controllers and DRAM (GPU-6.3)

**From northbridge to on-die.** In the 2000s the memory controller lived in a separate chipset chip ("northbridge"); every core access left the CPU package. AMD integrated the controller on-die with the K8, Intel with Nehalem — cutting a round-trip off every DRAM access and, more importantly for today's story, *binding each socket to its own memory*. The controller is now part of the CPU's uncore/IOD, alongside the PCIe root ports and the inter-socket links.

**DRAM mechanics.** A DDR channel is a 64-bit data bus (plus command/address) shared by the DIMMs plugged into it. Memory is organized as a tree: channel → DIMM → **ranks** (a rank = a full 64-bit-wide set of chips) → **banks** (independent arrays, 16–32 per rank, grouped into bank groups) → **rows** (the actual capacitors, each row several KB wide). Accessing a closed row takes three timed phases: **ACT** (activate, open the row into the bank's sense amplifiers — the "row buffer" — tRCD), **CAS** (column read/write within the open row — CL), and **PRE** (precharge, close the row — tRP). A hit to an already-open row costs only the CAS latency; a miss pays activate + precharge. Banks exist precisely so that the *latency* of one access doesn't stop the machine: while bank A is mid-activate, bank B can issue a read. **Refresh** periodically recharges every row (cells leak), stealing cycles. **ECC** adds 8 side-band bits per 64, and registered/buffered DIMMs (RDIMM, LRDIMM, MRDIMM) add clock drivers between controller and chips, trading a little latency for the electrical ability to run many DIMMs at high speed — the server capacity-vs-speed compromise.

**Bandwidth arithmetic.** Peak bandwidth is simple and worth internalizing:

```text
peak GB/s = (#channels) × (transfer rate in MT/s) × (8 bytes per transfer)

Turin-class EPYC: 12 × 6400  × 8 = 614 GB/s
Venice EPYC:     16 × 8000  × 8 = 1024 GB/s   (DDR5-8000 RDIMM)
                 16 × 12800 × 8 = 1638 GB/s   (MRDIMM-12800, ~1.6 TB/s)
NVIDIA Vera:     LPDDR5X wide interface  ≈ 1.2 TB/s
```

This is exactly the calculation behind the "1.6 TB/s per socket" headline of the Venice launch (section 2), and it is why core counts and memory channels must scale together: 256 cores starve behind 12 channels, so Venice went to 16.

**Latency vs bandwidth.** The profound fact about DRAM: a *single random access* costs ~80–100 ns (~250+ cycles) and essentially always will, on any machine. What improves generation over generation is *how many accesses can be in flight* — more channels, more banks, deeper queues in the controller — i.e., **bandwidth through parallelism**, not latency. The echo of GPU scheduling is exact: DRAM hides its own latency with bank-level parallelism the way an SM hides latency with warp switching. A workload that offers no memory-level parallelism (pointer chasing) or no locality (random gather) sees DRAM as a ~100 ns device no matter what the datasheet bandwidth says; a streaming workload with addresses known ahead of time sees the full 1.6 TB/s.

**Practical HPC consequences.** (1) The host staging buffers of the section 1.1 pipeline live in DRAM — if host reads can't keep up with the DMA engines, the overlap diagram is theoretical (this is precisely the bottleneck AMD profiled when justifying PCIe Gen 6 hosts, section 2). (2) Streaming writes that will never be re-read should use **non-temporal stores**, bypassing the cache to avoid evicting useful lines. (3) Numeric intensity decides everything: a compute node can deliver ~1 TB/s to a well-behaved stream, so any kernel below roughly 10–20 flops per 8-byte operand is memory-bound on the CPU side too — the same roofline logic that governs GPUs.

### 1.5 Sockets and NUMA (GPU-6.4)

**The multi-socket reality.** Once a server has two sockets, each with its own on-die memory controllers and its own DIMMs, the machine becomes a small distributed-memory system held together by a coherent inter-socket link (Intel UPI; AMD Infinity Fabric/xGMI). The abstraction is preserved — one physical address space, any core can read any address — but the *cost* is not: local DRAM is ~100 ns; remote DRAM crosses the socket link and the other socket's controller, landing at ~140–200 ns with materially lower bandwidth. That is **NUMA: Non-Uniform Memory Access**. The performance rule that falls out: *memory placement is a first-class optimization*, worth tens of percent on memory-bound codes, not a detail.

**Which memory is "local"?** Linux allocates a physical page on first touch, on the NUMA node of the touching thread (**first-touch policy**). A single-threaded initialization loop therefore pins an entire array to one node — and every other socket's threads then pay remote latency for the whole run. The standard toolkit: `numactl -H` (topology), `numactl --cpunodebind=0 --membind=0` (bind both), `--interleave` (round-robin placement — right for genuinely stream-like access with no reuse), and first-touch parallel initialization (have each parallel region touch the data it will later own). Interleaving helps bandwidth-hungry codes by spreading load across all controllers; it hurts locality-hungry codes by scattering hot data.

**Chiplets made NUMA nested.** AMD's chiplet design puts multiple compute dies (CCDs) behind an I/O die; each CCD has its own L3, and on many SKUs the memory controllers sit in the I/O die shared by all CCDs — so cross-CCD access is not cross-socket-expensive, but cross-CCD *cache locality* is gone. Intel's tiles and sub-NUMA clustering (SNC, which exposes each cache domain as its own NUMA node) express the same idea. The Mississippi State HPC documentation gives the practical formula: **NUMA nodes ≈ physical sockets × compute dies** (when SNC/die-splitting is enabled). The lesson generalizes: *every level of physical partitioning in modern silicon (socket, die, cluster) shows up as a NUMA boundary or a cache locality boundary — check `lscpu` before assuming "one machine, one memory."*

**HPC patterns where NUMA decides the outcome.**

- **Rank + GPU affinity.** In a dual-socket node with 8 GPUs, typically 4 GPUs attach to each socket. An MPI rank pinned to socket 0 driving a GPU on socket 1 pays remote latency on every staging buffer and every launch — a 10–30% tax on communication-heavy phases, fixed by binding rank *i* to the CPU cores and memory of its GPU's socket. (Full topology treatment comes in GPU-6.5–6.6.)
- **Network locality.** The NIC is a PCIe device on one socket; a rank on the other socket's cores sends and receives across the inter-socket link, doubling latency on the critical path of collectives.
- **Panel broadcasts in dense factorization** (HPL theme): when one rank's panel must reach all ranks, whether the source memory is local to the NIC decides whether the broadcast streams at link speed or throttles through a socket hop.
- **False sharing across sockets** is the worst case of section 1.3: a line ping-ponging between sockets pays remote-invalidate latency, not just local-invalidate — the 10–100× penalty applies *per remote hop*.

**Tying the day together.** The asynchronous pipeline of section 1.1 ends, physically, in host DRAM pages whose *placement* — which controller, which socket, which side of which link — determines whether the DMA engine and the CPU see the bandwidth the datasheet promises. Engines and queues (5.7) move the data; the CPU core (6.1) orchestrates; caches (6.2) and the memory controllers (6.3) carry the bytes; and NUMA (6.4) is the map over which all of it must be planned. Module 6 continues next GPU-day with the PCIe topology that connects this host world to the devices.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 AMD EPYC 9006 "Venice" — 256 Zen 6 cores, 1.6 TB/s, and the first PCIe Gen 6 server CPU

**What happened.** At its Advancing AI 2026 event (July 2026), AMD announced the EPYC 9006 series, codenamed **Venice**, its first Zen 6 server CPUs, built on TSMC's 2 nm node. The flagship EPYC 9996 packs **256 cores / 512 threads** of the dense **Zen 6c** core at up to 600 W, with 1024 MB of L3; high-frequency variants use standard Zen 6 cores (up to 96 cores at up to 5 GHz). SP7 platforms ship in Q4 2026, with SP8 enterprise parts in H1 2027 and the Venice-X 3D V-Cache (1152 MB) and Verano LPDDR host CPUs in H2 2027.

**What technically changed.** Four numbers, each mapping to a concept from today's lesson. (1) **16 channels of DDR5-8000, or MRDIMM at 12,800 MT/s, for up to 1.6 TB/s per socket** — versus 614 GB/s for the 12-channel Turin generation: the section-1.4 arithmetic, applied (16 × 12800 × 8 B ≈ 1.64 TB/s). (2) **The first server CPU with PCIe Gen 6** — 128 lanes at 64 GT/s, doubling per-lane bandwidth for accelerator attachment, with CXL 3.1 for memory expansion (a preview of GPU-7.2). (3) **Chiplet restructure**: Zen 6c CCDs hold 32 cores each with 128 MB of L3 per CCD (~4 MB/core, double Turin) — meaning the 256-core part carries a gigabyte of L3 as *eight per-CCD coherence domains*, the section-1.3/1.4 locality structure at extreme scale. (4) Two data-movement engines straight out of today's first topic: **SDXI**, a system DMA engine offloading memory copies (and reportedly 30–50 cores' worth of crypto) without burning cores, and **Smart Data Cache Injection**, which drops incoming network packets into cache lines instead of routing them through DRAM — an explicit application of "copy engines and cache placement beat general-purpose handling of bulk data."

**Why it matters.** Venice is the throughput philosophy of section 1.2 pushed to its current limit — density-optimized Zen 6c for throughput, frequency-optimized Zen 6 for latency, both on one socket — and it makes the host-CPU memory system the explicit design target for AI/HPC nodes: AMD's own bottleneck analysis of LLM weight streaming from host DRAM to GPUs found the PCIe Gen 5 receive path at 89–95% of theoretical ceiling while host DRAM read bandwidth sat below 10% — i.e., the CPU-side memory hierarchy, not the GPU, was the binding constraint, which is the entire motivation for 16 channels plus Gen 6.

**Read the claims with care.** The launch comparisons — up to 3.4× over Intel Xeon 6, 2.2× SPECrate throughput and ~20% per-core lead over NVIDIA's 88-core Vera (using NVIDIA's own published SPEC results and a matched compiler) — are vendor benchmarks under vendor-chosen conditions. The 2.2× throughput figure pits 256 cores against 88; the per-core figure is the more meaningful one, and it is ~1.2×. **What to watch next**: Venice-X (H2 2027) for cache-sensitive HPC codes, where 1152 MB of stacked L3 changes the blocking calculations of section 1.3; MRDIMM ecosystem maturity (12800 MT/s in volume); and whether Verano's LPDDR5X approach or Venice's DDR/MRDIMM approach wins as the AI host CPU.

### 2.2 NVIDIA Vera — the opposite pole: 88 latency-optimized cores in a coherent GPU fabric

**What happened.** NVIDIA's **Vera** CPU — the host processor of the Vera Rubin NVL72 rack and the Vera Rubin Superchip (one Vera CPU plus two Rubin GPUs on NVLink-C2C) — is now fully specified and entering systems: **88 custom "Olympus" Arm cores** producing 176 threads via **NVIDIA Spatial Multithreading**, up to **1.5 TB of LPDDR5X at 1.2 TB/s**, a second-generation **Scalable Coherency Fabric (SCF)** with 3.4 TB/s bisection bandwidth connecting cores, cache, memory, and I/O on a single compute die, and **NVLink-C2C at up to 1.8 TB/s of coherent bandwidth between CPU and GPU**.

**What it means technically.** Vera is section 1.2's latency philosophy embodied: fewer, faster cores chosen because the host workload — orchestration, tokenization, KV-cache management, RL evaluation loops — is control-heavy and latency-sensitive, not data-parallel. Two design choices deserve mechanism-level attention. First, **Spatial Multithreading** creates the 176 threads by *partitioning core resources* rather than by classic SMT's compete-for-shared-resources model — trading peak single-thread throughput for *predictable* throughput per thread, the same predictability argument that governs real-time and HPC scheduling. Second, the **LPDDR5X choice** over DDR RDIMM: roughly 2× the bandwidth at half the power per NVIDIA's claims, in exchange for DIMM-style capacity/flexibility — a memory-technology decision driven entirely by the host-node role (feed GPUs, hold KV caches), exactly the section-1.4 trade space. And NVLink-C2C's coherence changes the *system* model: instead of a CPU and a GPU with separate memories joined by PCIe (the module-7 world), Vera and Rubin present one coherent domain with 1.8 TB/s of CPU↔GPU traffic — NUMA "with the GPU," per section 1.5, rather than a device behind a bus. Notably, NVIDIA's own Rubin NVL8 *lower-end* platform keeps dual Intel Xeon 6 host CPUs — even NVIDIA treats the host CPU as a workload-dependent choice, not a settled matter.

**Why it matters and what to watch.** Together with Venice, Vera frames the industry's live experiment in host-CPU design for accelerated systems: throughput-density (Zen 6c, DDR/MRDIMM, PCIe Gen 6) versus latency-plus-coherence (Olympus, LPDDR5X, NVLink-C2C) — with AMD's Verano (72 cores, LPDDR5X, 112 GT/s xGMI, H2 2027) aiming at the same pole from the other direction. Watch the independent SPEC CPU 2027-era results as systems deploy, and specifically how HPC codes — not just AI inference — behave when the CPU-GPU boundary is coherent fabric rather than PCIe: that is a direct test of the synchronization and visibility questions coming in GPU-7.8.
