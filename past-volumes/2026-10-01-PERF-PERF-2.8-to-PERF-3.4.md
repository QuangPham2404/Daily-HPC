# 0. Concise Summary

Today's volume closes out the measurement-mechanics part of the curriculum and opens the statistics part. We first finish **PERF-2.8**: what a timer actually is on modern hardware, why reading one costs tens of nanoseconds, why `rdtsc` needs careful fencing, and why CUDA events have roughly 0.5 µs of resolution. We then move into **Module 3, Variability and Reproducibility**, covering its first four topics: why identical runs are not identical (**PERF-3.1**), which summary statistic to report and when (**PERF-3.2**), how to quantify spread with standard deviation and the coefficient of variation (**PERF-3.3**), and how to tell a genuine system event from ordinary noise (**PERF-3.4**).

The single most important technical connection in this issue is that **variance is not random junk — much of it is structured system behavior that summary statistics can either expose or hide**. On a tightly synchronized parallel machine, the *slowest* rank sets the pace at every barrier, so small per-node disturbances become an extreme-value statistic that grows with machine size. This is why the mean of N runs can mislead, why the minimum can be the most informative number, and why one 100 ms stall on one node out of thousands can dominate an entire run.

Three developments anchor the lesson in the real world: the classic **ASCI Q "missing performance" study** (SC'03), where LANL traced a 2× performance loss to OS daemons and kernel heartbeats; the **SC'25 El Capitan noise-isolation paper**, which reports a 192× improvement in full-scale `MPI_Allreduce` latency through service isolation and OS tuning; and the **OSU Micro-Benchmarks 8.0rc release** (September 2026), whose new unified launcher exists precisely because rigorous multi-point measurement is tedious enough to need tooling. Together they show that the statistics you compute are only as good as the timer underneath and the environment around it.

# 1. Learn the Concepts

## 1.1 Timer overhead and resolution (PERF-2.8)

Every timing measurement you will ever make reduces to the same operation: read a counter, run the code, read the counter again, subtract. It is worth understanding what that counter physically is, because its properties bound what you can measure.

**What you are actually reading.** On x86 CPUs, the primary high-resolution clock is the Time Stamp Counter (TSC), a 64-bit register incremented at a constant rate, readable with the `rdtsc` instruction and its variant `rdtscp`. On Linux, `clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, ...)` typically resolves to this TSC (via the vDSO, without a syscall); older or alternative platforms fall back to the HPET or ACPI PM timer, which are far slower to read. On GPUs, CUDA events are timestamped by the device clock itself — NVIDIA documents `cudaEventElapsedTime()` as returning time in milliseconds "with a resolution of around 0.5 microseconds," and notes the timing is measured on the GPU clock, making it independent of host-OS scheduling. In MPI, `MPI_Wtime()` returns wall-clock seconds as a `double` and is the portable way to time regions of an MPI program.

**Resolution, accuracy, and precision are different things.** *Resolution* is the smallest interval the timer can distinguish — the tick period. *Accuracy* is how close the reported interval is to the true elapsed time. *Precision* (in the statistical sense) is how repeatable a measurement of the same interval is. A timer can have 1 ns ticks and still be wildly inaccurate: on older CPUs with frequency-scaling TSCs, the counter tick rate changed with the core frequency, so converting cycles to seconds required knowing the frequency history. Modern "invariant TSCs" tick at a constant rate regardless of P-state, which fixed this class of error, but you should never assume a cycle count divides by the nominal frequency to give wall time without checking.

**Overhead: the measurement perturbs the measured.** Reading a timer is not free. A `clock_gettime` vDSO call costs on the order of 20–30 ns; a bare `rdtsc` is cheaper but still tens of cycles, and the instruction has a subtle problem: it is not fully serializing. The CPU may execute `rdtsc` *out of order* relative to your measured code — before all prior instructions have retired. Gabriele Paoloni's Intel whitepaper, *How to Benchmark Code Execution Times on Intel IA-32 and IA-64 Instruction Set Architectures* (2010), is the standard reference here: it walks through why naive `rdtsc`-bracketed measurements are corrupted by out-of-order execution and how to fix them — using `cpuid` as a serializing fence before `rdtsc`, or `rdtscp` (which waits for prior instructions) followed by another `cpuid` fence. The recommended pattern is: fence, read start, measured code, fence, read end.

To calibrate the overhead itself, time an *empty* interval: read the clock twice back-to-back, many times, and take a summary statistic of the differences. That number — typically tens of nanoseconds — is the floor of your instrument. Any code you measure that runs in less than a few hundred nanoseconds is comparable to the instrument's own cost, and you must either subtract the calibrated overhead or, better, measure many repetitions back-to-back and divide.

**A worked micro-example.** Suppose a calibrated empty-interval measurement on your machine gives a median of 24 ns. You want to time a function you believe takes ~100 ns. One-shot timing gives you something between roughly 76 and 124 ns of true content wrapped in 24 ns of instrument — a 20% distortion, plus whatever the fences cost. Instead, call the function 1000 times between the two clock reads and divide by 1000: the instrument's 24 ns is now amortized to 0.024 ns per call. This is exactly the pattern the OSU Micro-Benchmarks use (warmup iterations `-x`, then `-i` measurement iterations per message size): never trust a single short measurement; construct a measurement whose duration dwarfs the timer's cost, then divide.

**GPU-side timing pitfalls.** CUDA events look trivial — record a start event, launch kernels, record a stop event, call `cudaEventElapsedTime()` — but two rules from PERF-2.7 still apply and are worth restating in timer terms. First, both events must be recorded in the *same stream* as the work, otherwise you are measuring unrelated intervals. Second, the events must be synchronized (e.g. `cudaEventSynchronize`) before the elapsed time is read, or you will read garbage. Note also the 0.5 µs resolution floor: timing a 5 µs kernel with CUDA events leaves you blind to sub-microsecond variation, which is precisely the scale at which launch overhead and short noise events live. For finer GPU-side intervals, you eventually need profiling timestamps (Nsight Systems) rather than event pairs.

**System clocks and cross-node timing.** On a single node, `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` is internally consistent. Across nodes, clocks must be synchronized (PTP, or the node-local timers of the cluster interconnect), and synchronizing clocks to sub-microsecond accuracy across thousands of nodes is hard. This is why MPI applications time *durations* locally and reduce them, rather than comparing absolute timestamps across ranks — a theme we will return to in PERF-3.2 when we discuss what statistic to reduce with.

The takeaway of PERF-2.8: **know your instrument's tick, its read cost, and its error sources before trusting any number it produces.** A performance conclusion built on a timer that is being read out of order, at the wrong granularity, or without calibration is not a measurement — it is a rumor.

## 1.2 Why repeated runs differ (PERF-3.1)

Run the same binary, on the same input, on the same machine, twice — you will not get the same wall time. For a discipline that prides itself on determinism, this is uncomfortable, so let us enumerate the mechanisms rather than shrug.

**Sources of run-to-run variance, by layer:**

| Layer | Mechanism | Typical scale |
|---|---|---|
| OS/kernel | Timer interrupts, scheduler ticks, RCU callbacks, page-migration threads | µs–ms, periodic |
| Daemons/services | Monitoring agents, cluster managers, logging | ms–100s of ms, periodic |
| Hardware state | Frequency (DVFS) ramping, thermal throttling, cache/TLB cold vs warm | %–tens of % |
| Memory system | Page placement, first-touch NUMA policy, transparent huge pages on/off | %–tens of % |
| Interconnect | Other tenants' traffic, adaptive routing, congestion | highly variable |
| Placement | Which node/socket/core you land on; rank-to-node mapping | %, sometimes steps |
| Application | Nondeterministic reductions, atomic contention, thread schedules | code-dependent |

Two structural facts make this list important rather than just long.

**First, much of the "noise" is periodic, not random.** The landmark demonstration is the ASCI Q study we develop in Section 2. Los Alamos ran a *fixed work quantum* (FWQ) microbenchmark — one million 1 ms computations — on every node and histogrammed the per-quantum durations. The histograms did not show a smooth Gaussian blur around 1 ms; they showed **sharp spikes at well-defined latencies recurring at well-defined intervals**: a daemon spawned every 30 seconds, a kernel heartbeat every 125 ms, and so on. Each event could be characterized by a tuple ⟨F, L, E, P⟩ — frequency, average duration, distribution, and placement (which nodes generate it). When you see run-to-run variance in your own measurements, the right mental model is a sum of a few structured processes plus a small random floor — not white noise.

**Second, variance is amplified by synchronization.** A serial program that suffers a 5 ms disturbance is 5 ms slower. A BSP-style parallel program that barrier-synchronizes every 1 ms and has one rank delayed by 100 ms waits 100 ms — *every iteration it happens*, and the whole machine waits with it. The ASCI Q paper makes the arithmetic vivid: if one process out of 4,096 experiences a 100 ms delay per iteration, the application runs ~100× slower. As node count grows, the probability that *at least one* rank is disturbed in any given interval approaches 1 — the effective statistic governing your runtime is the **maximum** over thousands of samples of the disturbance distribution, and maxima grow with sample count. This is the deep reason large-scale machines are noisier than small ones, and it is why variance grows with scale even when the per-node environment is identical.

**Variance you can control vs. variance you must report.** This distinction organizes the rest of Module 3. Some variance sources are environmental and *controllable* (PERF-3.6): pinning frequencies, warming caches, choosing quiet nodes. Others are structural and *must simply be measured honestly* (PERF-3.2, 3.3): the machine's daemons will run whether you like it or not. Hoefler and Belli (SC'15) propose the vocabulary: state whether your measurement is **deterministic** (same value every run) or **nondeterministic**, and if nondeterministic, report confidence intervals rather than bare means. Their survey of 120 HPC papers found the practice rare — only 2 of 95 relevant papers reported confidence intervals around the mean. An honest performance number today is not "X seconds" but "X seconds, ±Y, under conditions Z."

A concrete anchor: in their running example, 50 HPL executions on 64 nodes of Piz Daint (N=314k) varied by up to 20%, with the reported 77.38 Tflop/s run sitting well above the slowest run at 61.2 Tflop/s — against a 94.5 Tflop/s theoretical peak. A single-number report hides all of that structure.

## 1.3 Which number do you report? Mean, median, minimum, maximum (PERF-3.2)

Once you have N measurements, you must collapse them into something reportable. The choice is not cosmetic: different statistics answer different questions, and picking the wrong one can invert a conclusion.

**The mean — expected cost, but fragile.** The arithmetic mean answers "what is the expected total time of many runs?" It is the right statistic when the cost of interest is additive over repetitions — e.g., a service that will run this job 1,000 times, where what matters is total time. But the mean is sensitive to outliers: one 2-second stall among 100 fast runs drags the mean visibly, even though it represents 1% of the runs. If your distribution is skewed (as timing distributions almost always are — they have a hard floor at the minimum and a long tail upward), the mean sits above the bulk of the data.

Hoefler and Belli's Rule 3 sharpens this: **use the arithmetic mean only for summarizing costs (times); use the harmonic mean for summarizing rates (operations per second).** If you average throughputs measured over unequal time windows, the arithmetic mean over-weights the fast windows. And Rule 4: **avoid summarizing ratios at all** — report the underlying costs or rates, because averaging speedups (or percentages of peak) across kernels with different absolute runtimes produces numbers that answer no real question. When ratios are unavoidable, the geometric mean is the defensible choice.

**The median — the typical run.** The median is the 50th percentile: half the runs are faster, half slower. It is robust to outliers and to skew, and it answers "what will a typical run look like?" For reporting application performance on a shared machine — where a reader wants to know what to *expect* — the median is often the most honest single number. Its weakness is the mirror of the mean's: it is indifferent to the tail, and sometimes the tail is exactly what you care about.

**The minimum — the machine's capability.** The minimum over N runs is special in performance engineering: it is (usually) the run with the *least* interference, the closest sample to what the hardware+software system can do when nothing goes wrong. The ASCI Q team used exactly this logic — after removing noise sources, the *minimum* SAGE cycle time over 50 cycles matched their performance model almost exactly, while the average sat above it. That gap (mean minus min) is itself a measurement: it quantifies the residual cost of noise, and it tells you whether further system-level tuning has headroom.

The danger of the minimum is that it can overstate what users will experience — and it is a favorite of benchmark gaming: run 1,000 times, report the best. The defense is to report min *alongside* a spread statistic, and to say how many runs you made. A minimum without N is marketing.

**The maximum and percentiles — the tail.** The maximum answers worst-case questions. Hoefler and Belli's Rule 8: for problems like worst-case latency, measures of central tendency are simply the *wrong tool* — a real-time constraint or a timeout-sensitive collective wants a high percentile (95th, 99th), not the mean. And their Rule 10 notes that for parallel time measurements you should report your summarization technique *across processes* — when timing a collective, for instance, plotting the maximum across processes assesses the worst-case experience, which is what the collective's performance actually is.

**Choosing among them — a decision view:**

| Question the reader has | Statistic to report | Also report |
|---|---|---|
| What should I expect for a typical run? | median | N, spread |
| What's the total cost of many repetitions? | mean | N, CI, spread |
| What can the system do at its best? | minimum | N, spread |
| Will I hit a timeout / SLA? | 95th/99th percentile or max | N |

One more subtlety for parallel codes: *which rank's time?* For a distributed job, the "runtime" of the application is the time of the last rank to finish — effectively a max over ranks. If you average per-rank compute times, you will produce a number that no rank experienced and that understates wall time whenever there is imbalance. We will return to this in Module 18 (Load Balance), but note now that the statistic you reduce over ranks is a modeling decision, not a formality.

## 1.4 Quantifying spread: standard deviation and CoV (PERF-3.3)

A single statistic says nothing about stability. Two kernels can both average 10 ms while one varies between 9.9 and 10.1 ms and the other between 5 and 40 ms. For optimization work — where you must decide whether a 3% change is a real effect or noise — spread quantification is not optional.

**Variance and standard deviation.** For a sample $x_1, \dots, x_N$, the sample variance is

$$s^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \bar{x})^2, \qquad \text{CoV} = \frac{s}{\bar{x}}$$

with $s$ the sample standard deviation and $\bar{x}$ the mean. The $N-1$ (Bessel's correction) matters at small N: with N=5 measurements, dividing by N underestimates spread by ~10%. Your timing samples are samples — use the sample formulas.

**A worked example.** Ten timings of a kernel (ms):

```
10.2, 10.1, 10.3, 10.2, 10.4, 10.2, 10.1, 10.3, 10.2, 10.0
```

Mean = 10.20 ms; the deviations are ±0.0–0.2; $s \approx 0.115$ ms; CoV ≈ 1.1%. Now suppose one run hit a daemon event: replace the last value with 14.0. Mean becomes 10.60 ms (+3.9%), $s$ jumps to ~1.20 ms, CoV ≈ 11%. One disturbed run out of ten changed the mean by more than many real optimizations achieve — this single example is the entire argument for reporting spread, and it foreshadows Section 1.5.

**Why CoV beats raw σ for comparison.** Standard deviation has units — 1.2 ms is huge for a 10 ms kernel and negligible for a 10-second HPL run. The coefficient of variation (σ/μ, often as a percentage) is dimensionless, so you can compare the *relative stability* of a 5 µs kernel, a 50 ms timestep, and a 4-hour HPL run on one axis. Rules of thumb that HPC engineers actually use informally: CoV below ~1% means you can see 3–5% optimizations directly; CoV of 5–10% means you must average many runs or improve the environment before trusting any comparison; CoV above 10% means the measurement environment, not your code, is the thing to optimize first.

**From spread to confidence intervals.** If the data were normal, the standard error of the mean would be $s/\sqrt{N}$, and a 99% confidence interval would be roughly $\bar{x} \pm 2.58\, s/\sqrt{N}$. Hoefler and Belli turn this into a practical stopping rule for experiments: *collect measurements until the 99% CI is within 5% of the reported mean* — a single sentence you can put in a report ("we collected measurements until the 99% confidence interval was within 5% of our reported means") that tells the reader exactly how trustworthy the number is. This also answers the "how many runs?" question quantitatively rather than by folklore: needed N scales as $(2.58 \cdot \text{CoV} / 0.05)^2$. At CoV = 1%, that's N ≈ 27; at CoV = 10%, N ≈ 2,658 — which is usually infeasible, and is itself the message: *reduce the environment's variance first* (PERF-3.6), then measure.

**The normality trap.** The CI formula above assumes normality, and timing data is usually *not* normal — it is skewed with a long right tail (the floor at the minimum, the noise events above). Hoefler and Belli's Rule 6: do not assume normality based on sample count alone; check with diagnostic plots (a histogram or quantile-quantile plot of your raw timings costs one line of Python), and if the data refuses to normalize, use non-parametric summaries (median with a bootstrap CI) instead. Their Rule 7 completes the toolkit: when *comparing* two nondeterministic configurations, do not eyeball two means — require non-overlapping confidence intervals, or use ANOVA-style tests. If the CIs overlap, you have not demonstrated a difference, whatever your intuition says.

**Connection backward and forward.** This topic operationalizes PERF-2.4 (steady-state measurement): steady state is precisely the regime where CoV stops collapsing as you remove warm-up runs. It feeds PERF-3.5 (number of repetitions), next volume's topic, which turns the CI algebra into an experiment-cost decision, and PERF-3.8 (baselines): a baseline without a spread estimate cannot support any later claim of improvement.

## 1.5 Outliers: real events vs. ordinary variation (PERF-3.4)

An outlier is a measurement far from the bulk of the distribution. The engineering question is never "should I delete it?" — it is "**what mechanism produced it, and is that mechanism part of what I'm trying to measure?**"

**A standard detection rule.** The classical boxplot criterion flags points beyond $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ or $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$, where IQR is the interquartile range (Hoefler and Belli's own figures draw whiskers at exactly 1.5 IQR). For timing data, which has a hard lower bound, outliers are almost always on the *high* side. More robust still for skewed timing data: flag anything above median + k·MAD (median absolute deviation), which does not itself get corrupted by the outliers it is hunting.

**The classification you must do before acting.** Every outlier belongs to one of three families:

1. **Measurement artifacts** — the timer was read wrong (unsynchronized GPU event, non-serialized `rdtsc`), the machine was swapped out mid-measurement, or the process was descheduled by something you control (your own debugger, an ssh session you left open). These are *your* errors; they say nothing about the system under test and should be fixed, then excluded, with the exclusion documented.
2. **Real, attributable system events** — a monitoring daemon ran; a kernel thread did memory compaction; another tenant's traffic hit your network. These are not errors; they are *the system behaving as it actually behaves*. Excluding them paints a rosier picture than users will experience.
3. **Unexplained events** — the interesting ones. An outlier you cannot attribute is a lead: it may be the visible tip of an intermittent problem (the ASCI Q team's histograms showed a few clusters that were "especially noisy (probably misconfigured)" — visible only as excess tail mass until someone investigated).

**Why HPC cares especially about the tail.** Two mechanisms make high-side outliers disproportionately important in parallel computing. First, the barrier amplification from Section 1.2: the application's time is governed by max-like statistics over ranks and iterations, so a rare event on any of thousands of nodes becomes a routine event for the application. Second, in any system with strict time limits — job schedulers, watchdogs, real-time coupling — the tail is the binding constraint. Dean and Barroso's *The Tail at Scale* (CACM, 2013) made this famous for web services: at warehouse scale, a request fan-out of thousands means the request completes only when the slowest component does, so 99th-percentile latency directly shapes typical user-visible latency. Swap "web request" for "collective operation" and the argument is a description of an MPI application at scale.

**Sound practice for outlier handling.** (a) Keep every raw measurement — never overwrite your datafile with the summary. (b) Plot the distribution (histogram, ECDF) before computing anything; structure that is invisible in mean±σ is obvious in a histogram — ASCI Q's entire discovery started as a histogram. (c) Classify each outlier into the three families above; report the count and attribution. (d) If you exclude, report both with and without. (e) If outliers are frequent enough to move your summary statistics, stop tuning code and fix the environment (that is PERF-3.6, and it is exactly what the El Capitan team did — see Section 2).

**Where you'll meet all five topics at once: HPL tuning.** When you tune an HPL run (block size NB, process grid P×Q), each configuration is expensive, so you run it few times — and each run inherits every variance source above: daemon noise, clock states, network neighbors. A 2–5% "improvement" from a parameter change is well inside the run-to-run spread on a noisy machine (recall the 20% spread in the Piz Daint example). The disciplined approach — measure N runs per configuration, compare medians with spread estimates, keep raw data, and treat a configuration as better only when the distributions separate — is precisely the difference between tuning and guessing. Your author's rule of thumb: the cheaper the experiment, the more repetitions you can afford; microbenchmarks earn statistics that full HPL runs rarely get, which is exactly why microbenchmarks (like OSU's, below) are designed to loop internally.

# 2. Key Developments / News

## 2.1 Historical landmark: the "missing" performance of ASCI Q (SC'03)

**What happened.** In 2002, Los Alamos National Laboratory noticed that SAGE — a ~150,000-line Fortran+MPI hydrodynamics code — ran at only about *half* the speed predicted by a validated performance model (<10% typical error) on the 8,192-processor ASCI Q machine (2,048 HP ES45 AlphaServer nodes, four EV68 processors each, Quadrics QsNet interconnect). Fabrizio Petrini, Darren Kerbyson, and Scott Pakin's SC'03 paper, *The Case of the Missing Supercomputer Performance*, documents the investigation.

**What they technically did.** Running a *fixed work quantum* microbenchmark (one million nominally-1 ms computations per node) and histogramming per-quantum durations, they found the noise was **structured**: discrete events with well-defined durations recurring at fixed intervals — a Quadrics RMS daemon spawning every 30 s (15 and 18 ms disturbances), a kernel-level cluster heartbeat every 125 ms (sub-millisecond), heavier daemons concentrated on each 32-node cluster's nodes 0, 1, and 31. Each event class was summarized as a tuple ⟨F, L, E, P⟩ (frequency, duration, distribution, placement), fed into a discrete-event simulator that reproduced the measured barrier latency, and used to predict which interventions would pay.

**Why it matters.** The paper is the founding demonstration of today's topics at machine scale: run-to-run variance was traceable to *specific, structured* system events, not randomness; summary statistics (the minimum SAGE cycle time over 50 cycles) revealed how much headroom noise was stealing; and the tail of the FWQ histograms was the diagnostic signal. Their headline finding (#2): *on fine-grained applications, more performance is lost to short-but-frequent noise on all nodes than to long-but-infrequent noise on a few nodes* — an extreme-value-statistics effect, since with thousands of nodes a frequent-anywhere event is an everywhere event. After removing ~10 daemons (envmod, insightd, snmpd, lpd, niff), halving RMS monitoring frequency (30 s → 60 s), and confining heavyweight daemons to the cluster-manager node, barrier microbenchmark latency improved by 13× (0 ms granularity), 2.5× (1 ms), and 2.2× (5 ms), and **SAGE's performance roughly doubled without touching a line of code or recompiling**. The lessons — look at distributions, not averages; noise is structured; the slowest rank governs; environment beats code sometimes — are exactly Sections 1.2–1.5.

**What to watch next.** The FWQ/⟨F,L,E,P⟩ methodology remains the template for noise characterization; its modern descendants appear in the next item.

## 2.2 Recent: breaking the system-noise barrier on El Capitan (SC'25)

**What happened.** At SC'25 (November 2025), Edgar A. León and colleagues from LLNL and HPE published *Breaking the System Noise Barrier at Exascale* (SC '25 proceedings, pp. 411–436), a study of OS noise on El Capitan — the ~11,000-node, 1M+-core, MI300A-based system that led the June 2025 TOP500 list.

**What technically changed.** The problem is the ASCI Q problem at 2025 scale: BSP-style applications doing frequent global collectives (CORAL-2 codes Pennant and Nekbone were named as casualties) are governed by their slowest rank, so per-node system activity — interrupts, kernel threads, ssh and monitoring daemons — translates directly into whole-application delay. The team's strategy has two components: (1) **isolating system services** onto dedicated CPUs ("SysCPUs") carved out of each node so daemons and kernel work never preempt application CPUs ("AppCPUs"), including kernel boot parameters that remove kernel processing and the scheduler-clock interrupt from AppCPUs; and (2) **systematic OS-level tuning** (eliminate, minimize, defer, or dedicate each noise source). The reported result is striking: a **192× improvement in `MPI_Allreduce` latency at full machine scale** after the mitigation configuration. They also published a distribution-independent Linux tuning guide, generalizing the approach beyond one vendor's stack.

**Why it matters.** This is the direct lineal descendant of ASCI Q's daemon hunt — same mechanism (structured system noise amplified by synchronization), different era (lightweight kernels like Catamount/CNL have given way to full Linux, which reintroduced the noise problem at exascale), and the mitigation is now an architectural knob (CPU partitioning) rather than a daemon purge. For your curriculum it demonstrates PERF-3.1/3.4 conclusions operating on the world's fastest machine: the fix was not application tuning but restoring a low-variance measurement environment — and the evidence that it worked is a latency distribution collapsing toward its minimum.

**What to watch.** CPU-partitioned compute nodes (dedicated system cores) are becoming the norm on new large systems; expect scheduler/affinity interfaces (Slurm cgroups, `hwloc` topologies) to expose SysCPU/AppCPU distinctions your jobs must respect — a theme PERF Module 15 (affinity) will pick up.

## 2.3 Tooling: OSU Micro-Benchmarks 8.0rc with unified launcher (Sep 2026)

**What happened.** The MVAPICH group at Ohio State released OSU Micro-Benchmarks (OMB) 8.0b in November 2025 and, per the project's benchmarks page, has **8.0rc (September 1, 2026)** as the current leading edge, following stable 7.5.2 (December 2025).

**What technically changed.** OMB 8.0 is a rebuild of the benchmark suite on common template files (reducing code duplication to make community contributions and single-point fixes easier) plus a **unified launcher** that batch-runs some or all point-to-point, collective, and RMA tests in one step, reports failures/incompatible arguments on exit, and summarizes results — plus colored terminal output and a fix for a zero-warmup edge case. The suite measures min/max/average latency for collectives across processes (and since 7.4, supports configurable percentile reporting — e.g., custom percentiles for latency evaluation — and congestion benchmarks like `osu_bw_fan_in/out`), covering MPI, OpenSHMEM, UPC, UPC++, NCCL, CUDA/ROCm, Java, and Python bindings.

**Why it matters.** This is the statistics curriculum becoming infrastructure. The reasons a microbenchmark suite needs warmup controls (`-x`), iteration counts (`-i`), min/max/avg over processes, *and* percentile support are precisely Sections 1.1–1.5: warm-up effects (PERF-2.3), run-to-run variance, the choice of summary statistic per question, and tail-aware percentiles for collectives whose cost is a max-like statistic over ranks. When you next run `osu_allreduce` on a cluster, read its output with today's lens: the average hides the structure; the min approximates the capability; the max is where ASCI Q-style noise shows up; and the new launcher exists because a sweep over message sizes × collectives × percentiles is exactly the Cartesian explosion (PERF-21.4 preview) that demands automation.

**What to watch.** The 8.x line's launcher design (one-command batch testing, machine-readable summaries) is aimed at automated regression testing of clusters — expect CI-style performance testing of new machine deployments to become standard practice.

### Further Reading

- **Hoefler & Belli SC'15 paper — 30–45 min, deep dive.** The full twelve rules with examples; the single highest-value read for measurement discipline in HPC.
- **Petrini et al. SC'03 — 45 min, classic paper.** Read Section 3 slowly: the FWQ methodology and the noise-histogram forensics are a masterclass in turning "mysterious variance" into named mechanisms.
- **León et al. SC'25 — 1–2 h, current research.** The exascale continuation of the noise story, with a practical Linux tuning guide; skim Sections 5–8 for what a modern mitigation stack looks like.
- **Paoloni's Intel whitepaper — 30 min, hands-on.** If you will ever time anything at the nanosecond scale on x86, read this first; includes reference code for fenced timing loops.
- **Dean & Barrozo, "The Tail at Scale" — 20 min, conceptual.** Web-services framing, but the percentile-amplification argument transfers directly to MPI collectives.
