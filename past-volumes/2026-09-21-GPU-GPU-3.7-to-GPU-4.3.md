## 0. Concise Summary

Friday's volume closed out the GPU compute datapath: number formats, the fused multiply-add, vector lanes, tensor engines, and the dataflow contract that feeds them. Today we finish that module and walk through the door it opened — because a matrix engine is only as fast as the memory system that feeds it. The five topics form one continuous argument.

First, **precision, throughput, and accuracy are three different axes**, and confusing them is the most common way to misread an accelerator spec sheet (GPU-3.7). A format's bit budget sets what the hardware must pay — multiplier area grows roughly with the *square* of the mantissa width, and every extra bit per operand costs wire energy and memory bandwidth — but *accuracy* is a property of the whole algorithm, not the format: low-precision storage plus high-precision accumulation plus scaling is how modern hardware buys accuracy back. Second, **theoretical peak compute is a derived number**, not a mystery: units × operations-per-cycle-per-unit × clock, per precision (GPU-3.8). AMD's CDNA 3 whitepaper publishes the middle factor explicitly, which lets us reproduce every MI300X peak to the digit — and exposes every marketing multiplier (sparsity, accumulator precision, TOPS-vs-FLOPS) hiding in vendor footnotes. Third, **no single memory technology can be simultaneously fast, big, and cheap**, so every GPU is a hierarchy (GPU-4.1) — registers, shared memory/L1, L2, HBM — each level existing to filter traffic for the level below.

The hierarchy's top two programmer-visible levels get full treatments: **registers** (GPU-4.2), the per-thread state whose demand silently decides how many warps can reside on an SM — and which Blackwell just relieved with a new tier of on-chip memory — and **shared memory / LDS** (GPU-4.3), the programmer-managed scratchpad whose whole purpose is to manufacture arithmetic intensity by amortizing each global load across many reuses.

Two developments anchor today's material in the real world: AMD's Helios rack and MI455X GPU (launched at Advancing AI 2026, July 2026) — whose MXFP4 peak claims are a perfect exercise in reading derived numbers critically — and JEDEC's HBM4 standard, which doubles the interface width of the bottom tier of every upcoming accelerator hierarchy.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Precision, throughput, accuracy: three axes, not one (GPU-3.7)

Friday's lesson (GPU-3.2) catalogued the formats: FP64 down to FP4, block-scaled MX and NVFP4 containers. Today's question is different and more fundamental: *what does a bit of precision cost, what does it buy, and where does accuracy actually come from?* Keep three axes firmly separated:

- **Precision** — how many bits encode a value, and how they split into sign, exponent, and mantissa.
- **Throughput** — how many operations per second the hardware sustains *at that precision*.
- **Accuracy** — how far the computed answer is from the exact one. This is a property of the *algorithm and its error analysis*, not of any single format.

**Why low precision is cheap: the multiplier area argument.** A floating-point multiplier's core is an integer multiplier over the significands plus alignment and normalization logic. Schoolbook multiplication of two *p*-bit significands generates on the order of *p*² partial products, so the multiplier array scales roughly with the square of mantissa width. Going from FP64's 53-bit significand to FP16's 11-bit shrinks that array by roughly (53/11)² ≈ **23×** — first-order silicon-area arithmetic, ignoring the wider shifters and adders FP64 also needs. In a fixed transistor budget, you can buy dozens of narrow multipliers for the price of one wide one. That is why a GPU's peak-throughput table reads like an inverse price list for mantissa bits, and why vendors keep shipping smaller formats: on the datacenter side, Blackwell adds FP6 and FP4; on the consumer side, NVIDIA's GB202 (GeForce RTX 50) keeps just 384 FP64 cores — two per SM, running at 1/64th the FP32 rate — explicitly "to ensure any programs with FP64 code operate correctly", in NVIDIA's own words. Consumer chips have essentially stopped paying for FP64; AMD's Instinct line, built for HPC, keeps full-rate FP64 matrix cores (MI300X: 256 FP64 FLOPs/clock/CU on the matrix path, 1:8 against FP16's 2048 — a deliberate HPC tax, not an oversight).

**The energy and bandwidth argument is at least as important.** Even if arithmetic were free, data movement is not: an FP64 FMA must move 24 bytes of operands where an FP4 FMA moves 1.5. Published energy estimates (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014) put a DRAM access at roughly two orders of magnitude more energy per byte than a small on-chip SRAM access — so halving the bytes per value halves the dominant energy cost and, in any bandwidth-limited phase of a kernel, directly doubles the rate. Lower precision is a memory-technology statement as much as an arithmetic one.

**Where accuracy actually comes from.** Here is the part spec sheets never say: a format's precision bounds *per-operation rounding error*, but the error that matters accumulates over the computation. The unit roundoff of a format with *p*-bit significand is *u* = 2⁻ᵖ (FP16: ≈4.9×10⁻⁴; FP32: ≈6×10⁻⁸; FP64: ≈1.1×10⁻¹⁶). For a naive dot product of length *n*, worst-case relative error grows like *n·u*. Concrete example: summing 4096 values stored in FP16 gives a worst case around 4096 × 4.9×10⁻⁴ ≈ 2 — order-100% error. Sum the *same* values in FP32 and the bound is 2.4×10⁻⁴. The lesson: **storage precision and accumulation precision are different knobs**, and this is precisely why every tensor core since Volta multiplies narrow inputs but *accumulates in FP32*. It is also why block-scaled formats exist (an E8M0 or E4M3 scale per 16–32 elements restores dynamic range the tiny exponent can't represent — Friday's topic 3.2), and why HPL-MxP works: factor in low precision, then recover accuracy with high-precision iterative refinement, where the correction work is a tiny fraction of the factorization. The June 2026 TOP500 shows the payoff in the wild — El Capitan's 16.7 EFLOP/s HPL-MxP result against 1.74 EFLOP/s FP64 HPL, a 9.6× ratio that is itself an architectural fingerprint.

**The trade-off, honestly stated.** Low precision buys throughput, capacity, and energy at the price of range and per-operation accuracy — and whether that price is acceptable is decided by the *algorithm's* error analysis (conditioning, accumulation length, refinement availability), not by the format table. That is why the same physical chip legitimately has half a dozen "peak" numbers, and it motivates the next topic: deriving them yourself.

### 1.2 Deriving theoretical peak compute (GPU-3.8)

Every theoretical peak on every spec sheet is the same three-factor product:

$$\text{Peak FLOP/s} = (\#\text{execution units}) \times (\text{ops/cycle/unit}) \times (\text{clock})$$

Learn to compute it and vendor numbers stop being magic. You already met the simplest case in GPU-1.6 (H100's 67 TFLOP/s FP32 = 132 SMs × 128 FP32 lanes × 2 FLOPs/FMA × ~1.98 GHz). Today we generalize it, and the cleanest worked example in the industry is AMD's, because the CDNA 3 whitepaper *publishes the middle factor*: a table of FLOPs per clock per compute unit, per precision. Reproduce MI300X's peaks from it:

| Path (CDNA 3, per CU) | FLOPs/dclock/CU | × 304 CUs × 2.1 GHz | Whitepaper peak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matrix FP64 | 256 | 256 × 304 × 2.1 GHz | 163.4 TF ✓ |
| Vector FP32 | 256 | 256 × 304 × 2.1 GHz | 163.4 TF ✓ |
| Matrix FP16/BF16 | 2048 | 2048 × 304 × 2.1 GHz | 1,307.4 TF ✓ |
| Matrix FP8/INT8 | 4096 | 4096 × 304 × 2.1 GHz | 2,614.9 TF ✓ |

Every digit checks — 304 × 2048 × 2.1×10⁹ = 1.3074×10¹⁵ exactly. Notice what the table encodes: the FP16:FP32 ratio is 8:1 on the matrix path (not 2:1 like the vector path), because the matrix core is a wider datapath built for reuse; FP64 matrix is full-rate while vector FP64 is half-rate. Peak tables are *datapath design decisions* written as arithmetic.

For tensor-core peaks the "units × ops/cycle" factor becomes *the per-instruction operation count*: one MMA instruction that computes an m×n output tile from k-deep products performs m·n·k·2 FLOPs, and the per-SM rate is that count times instructions issued per cycle. This is also where the FMA-counting convention (FMA = 2 FLOPs — GPU-3.3) enters the arithmetic.

**Now the footnotes — the multipliers that turn one chip into six different peaks.**

1. **Boost clock is not sustained clock.** Peaks are computed at maximum boost, which modern GPUs hold only within power and thermal budget. Under a dense tensor-core load, sustained clocks sit below boost; a "peak" is an upper bound, sometimes an optimistic one. (TOP500 *efficiency* — Rmax/Rpeak, typically 60–80% on HPL — bundles this with everything else; you met it in PERF-1.7.)
2. **Dense versus sparse.** NVIDIA's Ampere-introduced 2:4 structured sparsity and AMD's sparse matrix support *double* the quoted rate when the workload's zeros align with the format's metadata (the CDNA 3 whitepaper says sparse INT8 "enables doubling the computational throughput up to 8K operations per clock for a CU"). Datasheets now print "dense/sparse" pairs (e.g., HGX B200: 9/18 PFLOPS FP4 per GPU) — the second number is a best case most HPC workloads cannot trigger.
3. **Accumulator precision can halve the rate.** Some parts run a given input format at different speeds depending on the accumulator ("FP8 with FP16 accumulate" versus "with FP32 accumulate" are different rows in some tables). If your algorithm needs FP32 accumulation — and Section 1.1 says it usually should — check which number you were quoted.
4. **TOPS ≠ FLOPS.** Integer "operations" (INT8 TOPS) count one operation per multiply; a floating-point FMA counts two. A 2,614.9 "TOPS" INT8 figure and a 2,614.9 "TFLOPS" FP8 figure describe different amounts of numerical work.
5. **Per what?** Per SM, per GPU, per superchip (GB200 pairs two dies), per rack (NVL72 = 72 GPUs). Divide before comparing.

**The derived peak is only half the machine.** A peak FLOP/s means nothing without the peak bytes/s that feed it. Divide the two and you get the machine's *balance point* — the arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte of DRAM traffic) at which the machine is simultaneously compute-bound and memory-bound. On an HGX B200 (8 TB/s HBM3E), the dense per-GPU ridge points run: FP64 ≈ 5 FLOP/B, FP16 ≈ 281, FP8 ≈ 562, FP4 ≈ 1,125 FLOP/B. Read that spread carefully: at FP4, the machine must perform over a thousand FLOPs for *every byte* it fetches from HBM before the tensor cores can run at peak. No real algorithm streams operands that intensively from DRAM — the intensity has to be *manufactured* on chip, by reusing each loaded element many times. That is the entire reason the next three topics exist, and it is why GEMM kernels stage tiles through on-chip memory: a 128×128 shared-memory tile lifts intensity in proportion to tile size (a square tile of *n* delivers ≈ *n*/4 FLOP per byte from global memory — compute this yourself in Section 1.5). The roofline formalism that unifies this is a later PERF topic; today, keep the ratio in your head as the bridge from compute peaks to the memory hierarchy.

### 1.3 Why GPUs need a hierarchy (GPU-4.1)

The previous section ended with a machine that wants 1,000 FLOPs per byte. The next question is *why the bytes can't just be delivered* — why every GPU is built as a memory hierarchy at all. The answer is that **latency, bandwidth, and capacity trade off physically, and no memory technology wins all three**.

**The physics.** An on-die SRAM cell (6 transistors, ~150–300 F² of area) is fast — sub-nanosecond access, no refresh, directly wired to the execution units — but you can only fit a few MB per chip before SRAM crowds out compute: a hypothetical 192 GB of on-chip SRAM would require thousands of square meters... it is not a design option at any price. A DRAM cell (1 transistor + 1 capacitor, ~4–6 F²) is ~50× denser and is fabricated on cheap, specialized processes — but it needs periodic refresh, row activation before reads, and off-chip signal paths, so accesses cost hundreds of cycles and the interface can only be made wide and fast with exotic packaging (HBM's stacked dies and thousands of through-silicon vias). Latency at the bottom of the stack has been stuck near a few hundred nanoseconds-equivalents for decades, while compute per chip has kept climbing — the classic "memory wall". Worse, off-chip energy per byte runs roughly two orders of magnitude above on-chip access energy (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014), so every byte that reaches DRAM and returns has already lost the energy battle even before it waits in queue.

**The resolution: a ladder, not a solution.** Since one level cannot serve everything, designers stack levels, each tuned to a different point of the trade-off:

```text
        capacity per level            typical access cost         who manages it
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Registers      100s of KB/SM      issue-time (0 extra cycles)  compiler        │
│ Shared/L1      100s of KB/SM      ~tens of cycles              programmer/HW   │
│ L2             tens of MB/GPU     ~200 cycles (order of)       hardware        │
│ HBM stacks     80–432 GB/GPU      ~hundreds of cycles          hardware + DRAM │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   bandwidth per level DECREASES as you descend; capacity INCREASES
```

(Exact cycle counts vary by generation and measurement methodology — treat them as orders of magnitude, not datasheet values.)

Each level's job is to **filter traffic for the level below**: if a working set has temporal locality (reused soon) or spatial locality (neighbors used together), an upper level absorbs the repeat traffic and the lower level sees far fewer requests than the execution units issue. The hierarchy is a *locality amplifier* — and its effectiveness is measured in exactly the FLOP-per-DRAM-byte terms of Section 1.2.

**Concrete ladders, two vendors** (figures from vendor documentation):

| Level | NVIDIA H100 SXM | AMD Instinct MI300X |
|---|---|---|
| Registers | 64K 32-bit regs = 256 KB per SM (33.8 MB/GPU) | 512 KB VGPR file per CU (~152 MB/GPU) |
| Shared / L1 | 256 KB combined L1+shared per SM (up to 228 KB shared) | 64 KB LDS + 32 KB L1 vector cache per CU |
| L2 | 50 MB, chip-wide coherence point | 4 MB per XCD (32 MB total), coherent within XCD |
| Extra level | — | 256 MB "Infinity Cache" (memory-side, in the IOD) |
| Device memory | 80 GB HBM3 @ 3.35 TB/s | 192 GB HBM3 @ 5.3 TB/s |

Two observations. First, AMD inserts an *additional* level (the memory-side Infinity Cache) between L2 and HBM — a direct consequence of MI300X's chiplet design, where the L2s live on the compute dies and the HBM controllers on the I/O dies; the Infinity Cache absorbs cross-die traffic before it crosses the fabric. Hierarchies follow packaging. Second, compare the top and bottom of either column: capacity spans six orders of magnitude and bandwidth drops comparably — which is why *every byte the kernel needs should be touched as high in the ladder as possible*. A register operand re-fed to an FMA costs picjoules; the same value re-fetched from HBM costs hundreds of times more and stalls the pipeline for hundreds of cycles unless something else hides it (latency hiding was GPU-2.6; the hierarchy is its partner, not its alternative).

**Why GPUs lean on explicit management more than CPUs.** A CPU has a few heavyweight threads per core, so hardware caches with sophisticated replacement policies can transparently extract locality from almost any access pattern. A GPU has tens of thousands of threads whose aggregate working set would swamp any affordable cache, but it *knows* its access patterns — tiling, stencils, reductions are regular and programmer-declared. Hence the GPU bargain: the hardware gives you a small L1 plus a **programmer-managed scratchpad** (shared memory / LDS), and in exchange you, the programmer, state your reuse explicitly. The next two sections cover the two levels of that bargain.

### 1.4 Registers: the top of the hierarchy (GPU-4.2)

**What registers are on a GPU.** Architecturally, a register is a thread's private 32-bit slot — the operands and results of its instructions. Physically, a GPU's register file is a large multi-ported SRAM per SM/CU, *partitioned among all resident threads*: there is no per-thread storage off to the side; your 32 registers and the other thousands of resident threads' registers interleave in one physical structure that the warp schedulers read every issue cycle. The numbers are big and public: every NVIDIA SM since Volta through Hopper carries **64K 32-bit registers = 256 KB** (Hopper Tuning Guide; the Hopper whitepaper table lists 256 KB/SM and 33.8 MB per GPU), with a per-thread maximum of 255. AMD's CDNA computes units carry a **512 KB vector register file (VGPR) per CU** plus a small scalar register file (12.5 KB SGPR on MI300X, per ROCm's hardware specifications), allocated in wave-sized rows — a wave64 occupies 64-lane rows of 64 consecutive VGPRs per thread, a wave32 half that.

**Why register demand controls hardware residency.** Because the register file is physically partitioned, a thread block can only be *resident* (occupying an SM, eligible to be scheduled, able to use the SM's shared memory and synchronization) if its register demand fits alongside the blocks already there:

$$\text{resident warps}_{\max} = \left\lfloor \frac{64\text{K registers}}{(\text{regs/thread}) \times 32} \right\rfloor$$

Worked numbers on an H100: at 32 registers/thread the SM can hold its architectural maximum of 64 warps (2,048 threads); at 64 registers, 32 warps; at 128, 16 warps (512 threads); at the 255-register maximum, 8 warps. Since latency hiding (GPU-2.6) depends on having enough resident warps to swap in behind stalls, **register pressure is a first-order performance variable, not a detail**: a kernel that compiles to 65 registers instead of 64 can, at certain block sizes, halve its occupancy. Full occupancy math arrives with GPU-5.x (resource residency); the mechanism to understand today is the physical partitioning. AMD's side has the same arithmetic with different constants, and independent analysis (Chips and Cheese) notes a telling CDNA 3 design point: AMD raised tracked waves per SIMD from 8 to 24 *without* announcing a register-file increase — meaning the extra residency only helps kernels that are already register-thrifty.

**Register bandwidth: the pyramid's wide top.** Back-of-envelope, just to size it: an H100 SM issues up to 4 warp instructions per cycle, each reading up to 2 source registers and writing 1 across 32 lanes — order 1.5 KB of register traffic *per SM per cycle*, which at ~1.8 GHz across 132 SMs is on the order of hundreds of TB/s chip-wide, against HBM's 3.35 TB/s. The register file is the only storage fast enough to feed the datapath at full rate — everything below it exists to *keep it loaded*. (This estimate is illustrative arithmetic, not a vendor figure.)

**Spilling: when you run out.** If a kernel's live variables exceed the per-thread register budget, the compiler *spills*: it stores register values to a per-thread off-chip area — **local memory** in CUDA, **scratch** in AMD's model — cached in L1/L2 but physically DRAM-backed, at 10–100× the cost of a register access. AMD's optimization guidance describes the ladder bluntly: kernels first exhaust VGPRs, and scratch traffic "comes at a significant performance penalty." Checking a compiled kernel's spill count (`cuobjdump`/`nvcc -Xptxas -v`, or ROCm's compiler stats) is one of the cheapest performance audits in existence — and `__launch_bounds__` / `-maxrregcount` are the levers that trade spills against occupancy.

**2024's hierarchy event: the register file got a sibling.** When tensor-core accumulators grew with tile sizes, they competed with ordinary thread state for the register file — a 128×256 FP32 accumulator tile is 128 KB, half an SM's entire register budget. Blackwell's answer is architectural: a dedicated **256 KB Tensor Memory (TMEM) per SM**, beside the tensor cores, holding MMA accumulators (and warp-synchronous intermediates) under explicit `tcgen05` instruction control, with two CTAs able to share operands for one MMA (Friday's GPU-3.6 covered the dataflow; the point *here* is hierarchical): the register file was insufficient for the tensor era's working sets, so the hierarchy grew a new tier *between registers and shared memory*. AMD's CDNA answers the same pressure differently — MFMA accumulators live in the vector/AccVGPR file, fed from the LDS — a reminder that hierarchy design is a set of engineering choices, not a law of nature.

### 1.5 Shared memory / LDS / local memory: the programmer-managed tier (GPU-4.3)

**Disambiguate the vocabulary first — it trips everyone.** Three "memories" sound alike and are entirely different things:

| Name (CUDA / AMD) | Scope | Physical location | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shared memory / LDS** (Local Data Share) | one thread block / workgroup | on-chip SRAM in each SM/CU | programmer-managed staging, reuse, cooperation |
| **Local memory / scratch** | one thread (private) | DRAM (cached in L1/L2) | register spills, private arrays |
| **Global memory** | whole GPU + peers | HBM | bulk data, kernel I/O |

CUDA "shared" and AMD "LDS" name the same concept; CUDA "local" (despite the name!) is off-chip. In HIP, shared memory *is* the LDS. Intel calls the equivalent SLM (shared local memory).

**Physical placement differs by vendor, instructively.** NVIDIA *unifies* shared memory with the L1 data cache in one 256 KB-per-SM structure (Hopper), with a runtime-configurable carveout — H100 lets you set 0, 8, 16, 32, 64, 100, 132, 164, 196, or 228 KB as shared, with a per-block maximum of 227 KB (1 KB is reserved per block) and the classic 48 KB static-allocation limit requiring an opt-in beyond. AMD *separates* them: a dedicated 64 KB LDS per CU alongside a distinct 32 KB L1 vector cache (CDNA 3 whitepaper; ROCm specs). NVIDIA's way lets a kernel that doesn't use shared memory turn it all into cache; AMD's way makes LDS capacity and bandwidth fixed and predictable. Both are defensible answers to "who decides what the on-chip SRAM is for" — the vendor split is itself the lesson.

**The bank structure — and why it matters.** Shared memory is not one monolithic SRAM; it is **banks** that can serve one access word each per cycle. Both H100 and MI300X organize it as **32 banks of 4 bytes**; on AMD the bank index of a byte is `(address / 4) % 32`. The rule: if threads in a warp access *different words in the same bank* in the same instruction, the accesses serialize — a *bank conflict*, multiplying the access cost by the conflict degree. If they access the *same* word, it broadcasts — free. So:

```text
stride-1 access (conflict-free):     stride-32 access (32-way conflict):
lane:   0    1    2   ...  31        lane:   0    1    2   ...  31
addr:   w0   w1   w2   ...  w31      addr:   w0   w32  w64  ...  w992
bank:   B0   B1   B2   ...  B31      bank:   B0   B0   B0   ...  B0
        → 1 cycle                      → serialized, ~32× cost
```

AMD documents the access mechanics for MI300X: the LDS accepts up to 16 addresses per SIMD per cycle (32 per CU), delivered in phases — and the next generation widens the structure (MI355X doubles read bandwidth with **64 banks**, bank index `(address / 4) % 64`). Bank conflicts get their own curriculum topic (GPU-4.8); today, internalize that the scratchpad is a *parallel* structure whose performance depends on the address pattern your indexing generates.

**What shared memory is *for*.** Three jobs, in increasing order of importance to HPC:

1. **Cooperation** — it is the only fast way threads in a block exchange data. `__syncthreads()` (GPU-2.8) exists *because* blocks share an LDS allocation; the block is the sync scope precisely because it is the sharing scope.
2. **Staging** — the global→shared→register pipeline of every GEMM (Friday's GPU-3.6): load a tile once from DRAM, read it many times from the scratchpad.
3. **Manufacturing arithmetic intensity** — the quantitative payoff. Consider a square output tile of size *n* produced by a block: per k-step, the block loads 2*n* elements (a column of A, a row of B — 8*n* bytes at FP32) but performs 2*n*² FLOPs. Intensity from global memory ≈ *n*/4 FLOPs per byte. For *n* = 64: 16 FLOP/B — already above an H100's FP64 balance point (5) but far below a B200's FP4 point (1,125). For *n* = 128: 32 FLOP/B. Larger tiles buy intensity linearly, and *shared memory capacity is what caps tile size* — with 228 KB you can hold a few 128×128 FP32 tiles and double buffers, and no more. The ladder of Section 1.2 and the capacity column of Section 1.3 meet here: **tile size is bounded by scratchpad capacity, intensity is bounded by tile size, and achievable FLOP/s is bounded by intensity.**

**A canonical example, worked.** Parallel reduction of 4,096 floats with 256 threads — the pattern behind dot products, norms, and (at scale) HPL-MxP's refinement norms:

```cuda
__shared__ float sdata[256];
unsigned tid = threadIdx.x;
float acc = 0.f;
for (unsigned i = tid; i < 4096; i += 256)   // grid-stride: warp reads are coalesced
    acc += x[i];                              // 16 adds/thread, all in REGISTERS
sdata[tid] = acc;                             // one write to shared
__syncthreads();
for (unsigned s = 128; s > 0; s >>= 1) {      // tree: 256→1 in 8 steps
    if (tid < s) sdata[tid] += sdata[tid + s];
    __syncthreads();
}
```

Read it as a hierarchy tour: 4,096 loads come from DRAM *once* (coalesced — GPU-4.7 will formalize this); 4,080 of the 4,095 additions happen in *registers* (the top of the hierarchy — the sequential loop per thread is not laziness, it is the whole point); only 8 additions per thread-block-wide tree touch *shared memory*, and the stride-1 `sdata[tid] += sdata[tid+s]` pattern is conflict-free. A naive version that sums through global memory instead would issue ~8× more DRAM traffic and lose ~an order of magnitude. The same skeleton, tiled and transposed, is the classic shared-memory matrix transpose, where padding the tile stride from 32 to 33 words (`tile[32][33]`) shifts consecutive rows off the same bank and removes a 32-way conflict — the standard demonstration that *layout in the scratchpad is a performance decision*.

**Modern extensions, one line each** (details in later modules): Hopper's Thread Block Clusters let blocks in a cluster read one another's shared memory (distributed shared memory), and TMA/async-copy engines move global→shared tiles without consuming warp issue slots — both deepen the "staging" job; Blackwell's TMEM (Section 1.4) adds a tier below the registers that tensor kernels use for accumulators.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 AMD Helios and MI455X: a rack-scale exercise in reading peak numbers

**What happened.** At its Advancing AI conference (July 22–23, 2026), AMD launched the **Helios** rack-scale system: 72 **Instinct MI455X** GPUs (fifth-generation CDNA) with EPYC "Venice" CPUs and Pensando networking, in an open Open Rack Wide design, with OEM systems from Bull, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro. Engineering samples ship in H2 2026; mass production is slated for Q2 2027.

**What technically changed.** The per-GPU figures: **256 Work Group Processors with Wave32 execution** (CDNA adopting the RDNA-style 32-lane wave — which halves the register-allocation granularity of the classic wave64), and **432 GB of HBM4 at 23.3 TB/s** — 12 stacks, consuming JEDEC's new 2 TB/s-per-stack standard (below). The rack aggregates to **2.9 exaFLOPS of OCP MXFP4** (≈40 PF/GPU dense), **1.4 EF FP8**, **31 TB of HBM4** (72 × 432 GB), and up to **1.7 PB/s** of aggregate memory bandwidth, with 260 TB/s scale-up (UALink, per launch coverage) and 43 TB/s scale-out (Ultra Ethernet). A sibling part, the MI430X, targets sovereign-AI/HPC deployments.

**Why it matters — as a GPU-3.7/3.8 lesson.** Run today's derivation discipline on the claims. First, AMD emphasizes *OCP MXFP4* — the portable, vendor-neutral block-scaled format — while NVIDIA's headline 3.6 EF (Rubin NVL72) is NVFP4; AMD additionally claims "up to 15% better OCP MXFP4 peak theoretical performance" than Rubin NVL72's FP4 (AMD Performance Labs, June 2026). Public specs do not reconcile 2.9 vs 3.6 EF directly — the difference lives in footnote territory (dense/sparse accounting, format variants, what "peak theoretical" includes), which is exactly the multipler-hunting skill of Section 1.2. Second, AMD counter-positions on precision: it claims **up to 8.7× the FP64 FLOPS** of Vera Rubin for HPC — the FP64-vs-FP4 fork in accelerator design philosophy (Section 1.1), now a marketing axis. Third, apply Section 1.2's balance math: 2.9 EF FP4 over 1.7 PB/s requires ~1,700 FLOPs per byte at rack level — three orders of magnitude beyond what any DRAM streaming can supply, i.e., the hierarchy of Section 1.1 (HBM4 → Infinity-Cache-class levels → LDS/registers) is the *load-bearing structure* of the entire claim. Watch next: MI455X independent benchmarks, and whether UALink/UEC openness changes rack economics against NVLink.

### 2.2 JEDEC HBM4: the hierarchy's bottom tier doubles its channel count — and grows a cheaper sibling

**What happened.** JEDEC published the **HBM4 DRAM standard (JESD270-4)** on April 16, 2025 (updated as JESD270-4A v1.1 in December 2025), with contributions from AMD, Google, Meta, Micron, NVIDIA, Samsung, SK hynix, and others. On December 11, 2025, JEDEC announced it is preparing a companion **SPHBM4** ("Standard Package HBM") standard.

**What technically changed.** HBM4 doubles the interface from HBM3's 1024-bit to a **2048-bit** stack interface running up to **8 Gb/s per pin — 2 TB/s per stack** — and doubles the independent channels per stack from 16 to **32, each with two pseudo-channels**, giving controllers finer-grained, more concurrent access (more parallelism to hide DRAM latency with — Section 1.3's "filtering" logic applied *inside* the DRAM). The standard adds selectable VDDQ (0.7–0.9 V) and VDDC (1.0/1.05 V) rails, Directed Refresh Management for row-hammer mitigation, HBM3-controller backward compatibility, and 24/32 Gb die densities enabling up to 64 GB in a 16-high stack. **SPHBM4** targets the same throughput with only **512 data signals and 4:1 serialization**, so the memory mounts on standard *organic* substrates instead of silicon interposers — trading pin count and signaling rate for dramatically cheaper packaging, longer supported channels, and therefore more stacks (more capacity) per device.

**Why it matters.** This is the bottom of the ladder from Section 1.3 becoming an industry-wide, standardized battleground: every 2026–27 accelerator (NVIDIA's Rubin at 288 GB/22 TB/s, AMD's MI455X at 432 GB/23.3 TB/s) sits on this standard. The channel-doubling is a bandwidth story; the pseudo-channels are a latency-hiding story; SPHBM4 is a *cost* story — a reminder that even within one tier of the hierarchy, the latency/bandwidth/capacity trade-off is negotiated again, this time against packaging economics. For HPC: memory capacity per accelerator keeps climbing (432 GB!) precisely because the hierarchy above it needs deep buffers to keep intensity high. Watch next: HBM4E-class extensions pushing per-stack rates toward 4 TB/s, and whether SPHBM4 brings HBM-class bandwidth to mid-range HPC nodes.
