## 0. Concise Summary

Today's issue sits at the hinge between the floating-point module (completed with HPL-3.9) and the BLAS module that opens the road to blocked LU, distributed factorization, and HPL itself. It answers two coupled questions. First: why does the *same silicon* run dense arithmetic dramatically faster at lower precision — roughly 16× to 64× from FP64 to INT8 on a datacenter GPU — and what does that speed cost numerically (HPL-3.10)? Second: what exactly are the Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms (BLAS), and why did the field organize dense linear algebra into three levels — vector, matrix-vector, matrix-matrix — whose performance ceilings differ by two orders of magnitude on identical hardware (HPL-4.1 through HPL-4.4)?

The unifying idea is that two independent ratios govern dense linear algebra performance. The first is the **arithmetic intensity** of the operation — FLOPs per byte moved — which is essentially what the BLAS levels classify: Level 1 and Level 2 are memory-bound almost no matter how clever the kernel, because every matrix element is used once; Level 3 intensity *grows* with matrix size, which is why every high-performance dense solver since the late 1980s is built from matrix-matrix kernels. The second ratio is **precision** — bits per element — today's new axis: narrower formats both multiply the throughput of the math units (multiplier cost grows roughly quadratically with mantissa width) and halve the bytes per element, so precision acts as a multiplier on *both* axes at once.

The developments section applies both axes at full system scale. On the June 2026 TOP500, China's CPU-only LineShine debuted at No. 1 with 2.198 EFlop/s on FP64 HPL — yet ranked only fourth on the mixed-precision HPL-MxP list with a 3.6× speedup, while accelerator-based systems showed 8–24× speedups: a direct demonstration that the precision axis is where matrix engines separate from CPUs. Meanwhile cuBLAS now *emulates* FP64 GEMM using INT8 tensor cores (the Ozaki scheme, with an automatic dynamic-precision framework), turning precision into a software-tunable parameter *inside* the BLAS layer — and BLIS 2.x shows the same layer still evolving on the CPU side. Together they make one point: the BLAS interface is where algorithms, memory systems, and precision meet, which is exactly why HPL's algorithm structure is designed around it.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 HPL-3.10 — Precision versus speed: why fewer bits buy more FLOP/s

**The problem.** Module 3 built the floating-point model: formats, rounding, error accumulation, fused multiply-add. One question was left open. Given that hardware *can* represent numbers in FP16, FP32, or FP64, why does the same chip deliver wildly different FLOP/s depending on which format a kernel chooses — and why can't everyone simply use the fastest one?

**Mechanism 1: multiplier cost grows roughly quadratically with mantissa width.** A floating-point multiply aligns exponents (cheap, O(w) wiring) and multiplies two w-bit significands. A schoolbook w×w-bit multiplier computes w² bit products and reduces them with an adder tree — O(w²) area — whereas a w-bit adder is O(w). An FP64 multiply manipulates 53-bit significands; FP16 only 11. To first order, one FP64 multiplier therefore costs roughly (53/11)² ≈ 23× the silicon area of an FP16 multiplier. Real designs differ in detail (booth encoders, Wallace trees, pipelining), but the direction and rough magnitude hold. Since a chip has a fixed transistor budget and target clock, you can place many more narrow multipliers than wide ones — and clock them at least as fast. The result is the characteristic staircase of peak rates on a single GPU. On the NVIDIA A100 (vendor datasheet figures):

| Precision | Execution path | Peak (dense) | vs FP64 CUDA cores |
|---|---|---|---|
| FP64 | CUDA cores | 9.7 TFLOP/s | 1× |
| FP64 | Tensor cores | 19.5 TFLOP/s | 2× |
| FP32 | CUDA cores | 19.5 TFLOP/s | 2× |
| TF32 | Tensor cores | 156 TFLOP/s | 16× |
| FP16 / BF16 | Tensor cores | 312 TFLOP/s | 32× |
| INT8 | Tensor cores | 624 TOPS | 64× |

Notice TF32's position: it keeps FP32's 8-bit exponent (full FP32 dynamic range) but truncates the significand to 10 explicit bits, exactly FP16's mantissa. It exists so that FP32-shaped problems can ride the tensor-core path — a format designed as a compromise on this very precision-versus-speed axis. On CPUs the same mechanism appears as vector-lane packing: a 512-bit vector unit holds 8 FP64 lanes or 16 FP32 lanes per instruction, so FP32 vector throughput is typically 2× FP64.

**Mechanism 2: narrower operands mean less data movement.** From HPL-2.6, arithmetic intensity is FLOPs per byte moved. Halving element width (FP64→FP32, FP32→FP16) halves bytes per element, so the *same operation on the same matrices* doubles its arithmetic intensity. For memory-bound work (Levels 1 and 2 below), precision is effectively a bandwidth multiplier: an FP16 GEMV moves half the bytes of its FP32 counterpart and can run up to 2× faster even with identical math-unit throughput. Memory energy per element halves too. This is why low precision pays off even when the kernel is not compute-bound.

**Mechanism 3: energy per operation.** Fewer bits per operand means fewer switched wires and latches per FLOP, and the O(w²) argument applies to energy as well as area. On modern hardware, moving data costs far more energy than computing with it once it arrives, but *both* terms shrink with narrower formats — which is why low precision is as much a Green500 (GFLOP/s per watt) lever as a TOP500 lever.

**The price: numerical robustness.** Recall from HPL-3.4 and HPL-3.8 (do not re-derive): unit roundoff \(u = 2^{-p}\) for p significand bits — FP64 \(u \approx 1.1\times10^{-16}\), FP32 \(u \approx 6.0\times10^{-8}\), TF32 and FP16 \(u \approx 4.9\times10^{-4}\), BF16 \(u \approx 3.9\times10^{-3}\) — and error over a long accumulation of n terms grows like \(n \cdot u\) worst-case, \(\sqrt{n}\,u\) on average. Concrete example: accumulating \(n = 10^6\) products. In FP64 the worst-case relative error is ~\(10^{-10}\) — harmless. In FP16 it is ~490 worst-case and ~0.5 even in the optimistic random-walk model — the result is numerical garbage. On top of that, FP16's dynamic range saturates near 65,504 (HPL-3.6), so intermediate growth in elimination (HPL-7.5, upcoming) can overflow where FP64 would not even flinch. Low precision is therefore never a drop-in replacement: it requires *algorithmic repair* — scaling, higher-precision accumulation, iterative refinement — which is precisely the design of HPL-MxP (modules 14–16, upcoming).

**The key subtlety: multiply precision and accumulate precision are separate knobs.** A tensor core takes FP16 (or BF16, TF32) *inputs* but accumulates the inner products in FP32. The products are computed cheaply at narrow width, while the long sums — where error accumulates — stay in a wide accumulator. cuBLAS exposes exactly this distinction through its compute types and implementation flags (e.g. `CUBLAS_COMPUTE_32F_FAST_16F`, or `CUBLASLT_NUMERICAL_IMPL_FLAGS_INPUT_16F` with `ACCUMULATOR_32F`). This decoupling is the foundation of mixed-precision kernels (HPL-17.2, upcoming) and of the FP64 emulation development in Section 2.2, which pushes the idea to its logical extreme: INT8 *inputs*, INT32/INT64 accumulation, FP64-accurate results.

**A speed-of-light estimate.** Take a GPU with 9.7 TFLOP/s FP64 and 312 TFLOP/s FP16 tensor cores (A100-class). A dense LU factorization of an \(n = 100{,}000\) matrix is \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3 \approx 6.7\times10^{14}\) FLOPs (derivation in HPL-5.6, upcoming). Pure FP64 execution: ~69 seconds. Pure FP16 tensor-core execution: ~2.1 seconds — a 32× gap. Raw FP16 LU fails numerically, but HPL-MxP's recipe — factor in low precision, then repair with a few high-precision refinement steps — keeps most of the 32× if refinement costs only a few percent of runtime. That is the entire economic argument of mixed-precision HPC in one calculation, and it is why the hardware staircase above matters to a benchmark whose results are judged in FP64-accurate answers.

### 1.2 HPL-4.1 — What BLAS is: a standard contract between algorithms and hardware

**The problem.** Every dense algorithm — LU, Cholesky, QR, eigenvalue reductions — decomposes into a small vocabulary of primitive array operations. Before standardization, each machine's library implemented these idiosyncratically; codes were non-portable, unreadable, and performance claims were unreproducible. The community's answer, beginning in the 1970s, was to standardize the primitives themselves.

**History.** In 1973, Hanson, Krogh, and Lawson published a short article in the SIGNUM Newsletter proposing a set of basic linear algebra routines. The original **Level 1 BLAS** (vector operations) were specified by Lawson, Hanson, Kincaid, and Krogh (ACM TOMS, 1979). The **Level 2 BLAS** (matrix-vector operations) followed, proposed by Dongarra, Du Croz, Hammarling, and Hanson (TOMS, 1988), motivated by vector-register machines. The **Level 3 BLAS** (matrix-matrix operations) were specified by Dongarra, Du Croz, Duff, and Hammarling (TOMS, 1990), motivated explicitly by cache hierarchies and parallel machines — the paper's introduction says so almost verbatim: block operations "provide for full reuse of data while the block is held in the cache or local memory... and give a surface-to-volume effect for the ratio of operations to data movement." A BLAS Technical Forum convened from 1995 onward later extended the standard (C and Fortran interfaces, sparse BLAS, extended- and mixed-precision BLAS). The levels are named by asymptotic work per data touched: Level 1 performs O(n) work on O(n) data, Level 2 O(n²) on O(n²), Level 3 O(n³) on O(n²) — the 1990 paper's own framing.

**The idea: BLAS is a contract.** The algorithm writer calls, say, `DGEMM` with fully specified semantics — operand shapes, transposes, accumulation conventions, rounding expectations. The implementer — a vendor (cuBLAS, rocBLAS, oneMKL) or an open-source project (OpenBLAS, BLIS, ATLAS's descendant auto-tuning approach) — guarantees a *correct* and *machine-optimized* kernel behind that interface. Two payoffs follow:

1. **Portability of performance.** Write the algorithm once; every machine's tuned BLAS makes it fast. This is why LAPACK code from the early 1990s still runs near peak on hardware that did not exist when it was written.
2. **Leverage.** Because the whole stack is layered — LAPACK on BLAS, ScaLAPACK on PBLAS, vendor solvers on vendor BLAS — a single kernel-level improvement propagates everywhere above it. Section 2.2 gives a live example: when cuBLAS gained emulated-precision GEMM, libraries that call cuBLAS inherited the speedup.

**Naming convention.** A letter prefix encodes precision and complexity — S (single), D (double), C (complex), Z (double complex) — followed by an operation code: `DAXPY` is double AXPY, `SGEMM` single GEMM. The codes are abbreviations: AXPY ("A times X Plus Y") is \(y \leftarrow \alpha x + y\); GEMM is GEneral Matrix-Matrix multiply; TRSM is TRiangular Solve with Multiple right-hand sides; SYRK is SYmmetric Rank-K update.

**What the standard does not specify.** BLAS defines *what* is computed, not *how*. Blocking strategy, packing, microkernel structure, threading, and (increasingly) which arithmetic units and precisions are used internally are all implementation freedom — within the contract's accuracy requirements. This is exactly where hardware specifics — SIMD width, tensor-core tile shapes, cache geometry, memory hierarchy — get absorbed, so that algorithm code does not have to care. HPL itself is best understood as a *distributed orchestrator of BLAS calls*: its achievable performance is set substantially by the BLAS layer underneath it (plus communication, Theme 2's territory).

```
   Application / benchmark        (HPL, HPL-MxP, CFD solvers, ...)
              │
   Dense solver layer             (LAPACK, ScaLAPACK, cuSOLVER, SLATE)
              │
   BLAS kernels            Level 1 (vector) / Level 2 (mat-vec) / Level 3 (mat-mat)
              │
   Hardware                 (cores, vector units, tensor cores, caches, HBM)
```

### 1.3 HPL-4.2 — BLAS Level 1: vector operations and the memory wall

**Definition.** Level 1 BLAS operate on vectors: `DOT` (\(\alpha \leftarrow x^T y\)), `AXPY` (\(y \leftarrow \alpha x + y\)), `SWAP`, `SCAL`, `COPY`, `NRM2`, `I_AMAX` (index of max magnitude — you met this as the pivot search primitive in Gaussian elimination contexts). All perform O(n) FLOPs while touching O(n) data.

**Cost model, concretely.** Take `DAXPY` on length-n FP64 vectors: 2n FLOPs (n multiplies, n adds). Data movement: read x (n words), read y (n), write y (n) — 3n words = 24n bytes. Arithmetic intensity:

\[
\frac{2n \text{ FLOPs}}{24n \text{ bytes}} = \tfrac{1}{12} \approx 0.083 \ \text{FLOP/byte}.
\]

`DDOT` is slightly better — 2n FLOPs on 16n bytes (read x and y, write one scalar) — 0.125 FLOP/byte. Both are *constants*: intensity does not grow with n.

**Roofline consequence (worked example).** Consider a hypothetical but representative CPU: 200 GB/s DRAM bandwidth, 2 TFLOP/s FP64 peak. AXPY's bandwidth-limited ceiling is \(200 \times \tfrac{1}{12} \approx 17\) GFLOP/s — under 1% of the arithmetic peak. No amount of kernel cleverness fixes this: each element is touched exactly once, so there is no reuse to exploit. Level 1 performance is a property of the *memory system*, not of code quality. This is why STREAM-class bandwidth benchmarks predict Level-1 BLAS speed so well.

**What levers exist?** Since the math units sit idle waiting for data, the only real speedups are (a) better bandwidth (hardware), and (b) *precision*: an FP32 AXPY moves half the bytes of DAXPY and roughly doubles speed — Section 1.1's mechanism 2 in its purest form.

**Historical note and forward connection.** Level 1 was designed for vector machines, where long vector registers and operation chaining let `AXPY`-style kernels saturate the memory system; the name itself is 1970s Cray idiom. On cache-based machines Level 1 gains nothing from the hierarchy — there is nothing to reuse — which is what motivated the next two levels. Keep this in mind: *unblocked* Gaussian elimination (HPL-5.x, upcoming) is essentially a sequence of Level-1/Level-2 operations, and it will inherit exactly this bandwidth-bound ceiling. The entire blocked-algorithm story of module 8 exists to escape it.

### 1.4 HPL-4.3 — BLAS Level 2: matrix-vector operations and the reuse ceiling

**Definition.** Level 2 BLAS combine a matrix with vectors: `GEMV` (\(y \leftarrow A x + y\)), `TRMV`/`TRSV` (triangular multiply/solve), `SYMV`, and `GER` (rank-1 update, \(A \leftarrow \alpha x y^T + A\)). For an m×n matrix, these perform 2mn FLOPs.

**Cost model.** `DGEMV`: 2mn FLOPs; data: read A once (mn words) plus x (n) and y (m) — about 8mn bytes in FP64. Intensity ≈ 0.25 FLOP/byte. Better than Level 1 — each *matrix* element now participates in 2 FLOPs — but still a constant. `DGER` is worse: it reads *and writes* A, giving ~0.125 FLOP/byte, the same as a dot product.

**The deep reason.** In GEMV, each matrix element \(a_{ij}\) is multiplied by exactly one \(x_j\) and contributes to exactly one \(y_i\) — a reuse factor of one. The O(n²) work comes from having n² elements × 2 FLOPs each, not from reusing data. Compare GEMM in the next section, where each element of A meets *every* column of B. This distinction — reuse factor 1 versus reuse factor n — is the entire technical content of the Level 2 / Level 3 divide.

**Roofline numbers.** On the hypothetical CPU above: GEMV ceiling = 200 × 0.25 = 50 GFLOP/s, ~2.5% of peak. On an A100 with ~2 TB/s HBM: ~500 GFLOP/s against 9.7 TFLOP/s FP64 peak — still memory-bound. Switching the same kernel to FP32 doubles intensity to 0.5 FLOP/byte and roughly doubles throughput: for Level 2, precision is a first-class optimization, not an afterthought.

**Why Level 2 exists at all.** The 1988 Level-2 proposal targeted vector-register machines: in GEMV, x and y stay resident in vector registers while A streams past, so the *vector register* reuse (not cache reuse) is real, and one result vector is produced per pass. On such machines Level-2 formulations of factorization algorithms ran well. But when cache hierarchies and truly parallel machines arrived, the O(1) intensity ceiling bit hard: classic (unblocked) LU is built from rank-1 updates and dot products — Level 1/2 work — so it runs at a few percent of peak, a factor of 30–100 below what the same hardware achieves on matrix-matrix work. LAPACK's reorganization of factorizations into *blocked* form (module 8, upcoming) exists precisely to convert those rank-1 updates into rank-b GEMM updates.

**Where you still meet Level 2 in practice.** Level 2 never disappeared from real codes; it just stopped being where the FLOPs are. In HPL/HPL-MxP contexts: panel factorization is a tall-skinny, Level-2-flavored computation (HPL-19.2, upcoming); iterative refinement's residual evaluation \(r = b - Ax\) is GEMV-shaped (HPL-15.2, upcoming); iterative sparse methods live on SpMV, the sparse cousin of GEMV. Optimizing Level 2 is a bandwidth problem: coalesced/contiguous access, minimal temporaries, tall-skinny kernel design.

### 1.5 HPL-4.4 — BLAS Level 3: matrix-matrix operations and the arithmetic-intensity escape

**Definition.** Level 3 BLAS operate on matrices: `GEMM` (\(C \leftarrow \alpha A B + \beta C\)), `SYMM`, `HEMM` (symmetric/Hermitian multiply), `TRMM` (triangular multiply), `TRSM` (triangular solve with multiple right-hand sides), `SYRK`/`SYR2K` and their Hermitian variants (rank-k updates). For n×n×n GEMM: 2n³ FLOPs.

**Cost model.** Counting one pass over the operands (read A and B, write C; β=0 avoids reading C): 2n³ FLOPs on ~3n² words = 24n² bytes:

\[
\text{intensity} \;=\; \frac{2n^3}{24n^2} \;=\; \frac{n}{12} \ \text{FLOP/byte} \quad (\text{FP64}).
\]

This *grows with n*. For n = 256: ~21 FLOP/byte. For n = 2048: ~171. For n = 16,384: ~1,365. There is no ceiling from the memory system once n is large enough — Level 3 is where dense linear algebra finally becomes compute-bound.

**The reuse mechanism.** In \(C = AB\), each element of A participates in 2n FLOPs (once for every column of B) and each element of B likewise (once for every row of A). If blocks of A and B of size b fit in cache, every byte fetched from DRAM is reused ~b times before eviction, so the *realized* DRAM traffic approaches the ideal 3n² words even for enormous n. That is HPL-2.8's surface-to-volume effect, now realized at the kernel level; register-level tiling (HPL-9.3, upcoming) pushes reuse one level deeper, into the register file. Note carefully: the n/12 formula is a *lower bound on traffic* that is achievable only with blocking — the naive triple loop re-reads B once per row of A and is orders of magnitude worse in bytes moved. Blocking is not an optimization nicety; it is what makes the formula true.

**The three levels side by side (FP64):**

| Level | Example kernel | FLOPs | Bytes moved | Intensity (FLOP/B) | Bound by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | DAXPY (length n) | 2n | 24n | 0.083 | memory bandwidth |
| 1 | DDOT (length n) | 2n | 16n | 0.125 | memory bandwidth |
| 2 | DGEMV (m×n) | 2mn | ~8mn | 0.25 | memory bandwidth |
| 2 | DGER (m×n) | 2mn | ~16mn | 0.125 | memory bandwidth |
| 3 | DGEMM (n³) | 2n³ | ~24n² | n/12 | FLOP units (large n) |

**Worked roofline example.** Same hypothetical CPU (200 GB/s, 2 TFLOP/s): DGEMM at n = 2048 has a bandwidth ceiling of 200 × 171 ≈ 34 TFLOP/s — far above the 2 TFLOP/s arithmetic peak, so the kernel is compute-bound and can actually *use* the FLOP units. Contrast: AXPY 17 GFLOP/s, GEMV 50 GFLOP/s, GEMM ~1.5–2 TFLOP/s. A ~100× spread between levels on identical silicon is the single most important performance fact in dense linear algebra, and it explains more about HPL behavior than any other single number.

**Why Level 3 "changes everything" (the 1990 paper's own argument).** The Level 3 BLAS specification was written to enable portable *blocked algorithms*: factorizations reorganized so that the bulk of work becomes matrix-matrix operations on blocks. The authors' stated motivations map exactly onto our mechanisms: (1) full reuse of data while a block is held in cache or local memory, avoiding data movement; (2) a favorable surface-to-volume ratio of operations to memory traffic; (3) two levels of parallelism — operations on distinct blocks can run concurrently, *and* operations within a block can be parallelized — both exploitable inside the BLAS, transparently to the caller. LAPACK (then under development, the successor to LINPACK) was designed around this: LINPACK's factorizations had been Level-1 formulations; LAPACK's are Level-3 formulations.

**Consequences for the rest of the curriculum.** Blocked LU (module 8, upcoming) decomposes into: panel factorization (Level 1/2-heavy, hard to make fast — see 1.4), a triangular solve (TRSM), and a trailing-matrix update (DGEMM). Because the trailing update holds ~all the FLOPs (HPL-9.1, upcoming), a well-tuned HPL run spends most of its time inside Level-3 kernels, and HPL's block-size parameter NB (HPL-12.4, upcoming) is precisely the knob that sets how much of the algorithm is GEMM versus panel work. The critical-path story of module 20 — GPUs starving while waiting for panels — is an indirect tribute to Level 3: GEMM became *so* fast that the Level-2-flavored panel and the communication around it now dominate the timeline. And Level 3 is where the precision axis plugs in: FP64 tensor-core DGEMM, TF32/FP16 mixed-precision GEMM with FP32 accumulation, and the INT8-based FP64 emulation of Section 2.2 are all, mechanically, Level-3 kernels. The fastest dense kernel achievable on given hardware is: **Level-3 structure × lowest numerically-safe precision × tuned microkernel** — modules 9 and 17 are about the third factor; today was about the first two.

**A note on hierarchy.** One subtlety: "Level 3 is fast" is a statement about *asymptotic* intensity. Real GEMM efficiency still depends on shape (HPL-9.9), on the local memory hierarchy depth (HPL-9.4–9.6), and on whether dimensions align with hardware tile sizes (HPL-17.3/17.8). A 4096×64×4096 GEMM is Level 3 by classification but behaves partly like Level 2 — tall-skinny shapes have one long dimension but little blocking freedom in the thin one. The panel problems of module 19 live exactly there.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 The June 2026 TOP500: a CPU-only system wins FP64 HPL — and loses the precision race

**What happened.** The 67th TOP500 list, announced June 23, 2026 at ISC in Hamburg, has a new No. 1: **LineShine**, installed at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, China, built on the custom "LingKun" platform. It reported **2.198 EFlop/s on HPL** using 13,789,440 cores of 304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz (public reporting identifies LX2 as Armv9-derived), a proprietary LingQi interconnect, and Kylin OS — about 80% of its 2.736 EFlop/s theoretical peak, at roughly 42.2 MW (≈52 GFLOP/s per watt). It is the first Chinese system to lead the list since Sunway TaihuLight in 2017, and the first system to exceed two exaflops of sustained FP64 using CPUs only — no GPUs or accelerators. It also took No. 1 on HPCG (22.0 PFlop/s). El Capitan (1.809 EFlop/s), Frontier (1.353), Aurora (1.012), and JUPITER Booster (1.000) complete the exascale cohort.

**The technically interesting part is the split with HPL-MxP.** On the mixed-precision HPL-MxP list, LineShine debuted only **fourth at 7.92 EFlop/s — a 3.6× speedup over its own HPL score** — while accelerator-based systems showed: El Capitan 16.7 EFlop/s (9.2×), Aurora 11.6 (11.5×), Frontier 11.4 (8.4×), and SoftBank's B200-based CHIE-4 3.3 EFlop/s with a striking **24.4×** speedup over its FP64 result. TOP500's own commentary attributes LineShine's modest multiplier to its CPU-only design without dedicated low-precision matrix engines.

**Why it matters, and the connection to today's concepts.** This is HPL-3.10 demonstrated at the scale of the world's fastest machines. FP64 HPL rewards a huge fleet of wide vector units fed by high-bandwidth memory — which a 13.8-million-core CPU system can supply, as LineShine's 80%-of-peak efficiency shows (itself a Level-3 BLAS and load-balance story: sustaining 80% of Rpeak on HPL requires near-peak DGEMM and excellent communication balance). But the moment the benchmark allows low precision with algorithmic accuracy recovery — HPL-MxP — matrix-engine-equipped systems jump by roughly an order of magnitude, because their FP16/BF16/FP8 tensor-core rates are 8–30× their FP64 rates (Section 1.1's staircase), while a CPU's FP32 is only ~2× its FP64. The two lists now measure genuinely different hardware virtues: peak wide-precision throughput versus low-precision matrix-engine throughput plus the software to exploit it safely.

**What to watch.** Whether CPU designs respond with on-die low-precision matrix units (the AMX/SME pattern on server CPUs is the precedent); whether LineShine-class systems publish HPL-MxP-improving submissions; and — connecting to the next development — whether FP64 *emulation* on tensor cores narrows the FP64 gap for GPU systems from the other direction.

### 2.2 cuBLAS floating-point emulation: FP64 GEMM computed on INT8 tensor cores

**What happened.** NVIDIA has productized *floating-point emulation* inside cuBLAS: FP32 matrix multiplication emulated on Blackwell BF16 tensor cores (the **BF16x9** algorithm, released with CUDA 12.9, May 2025), and FP64 matrix multiplication emulated on INT8 tensor cores (the **Ozaki-1 scheme**, fixed-point emulation, released in the CUDA 13.0 Update 2/13.0.x timeframe), both opt-in via math modes (`CUBLAS_FP32_EMULATED_BF16X9_MATH`, `CUBLAS_FP64_EMULATED_FIXEDPOINT_MATH`) or environment variables (`CUBLAS_EMULATE_SINGLE_PRECISION`, `CUBLAS_EMULATE_DOUBLE_PRECISION`).

**What technically changed.** The FP64 path is the deeper one. Blackwell's FP64 vector throughput barely improved over prior generations — NVIDIA's own GTC 2025 materials state that FP32/FP64 native rates are no longer growing like the low-precision rates — while INT8 tensor-core throughput is enormous. The Ozaki scheme exploits that: scale rows of A and columns of B, align exponents, slice the 53-bit mantissas into 8-bit chunks, run a *variable number* of INT8 GEMMs with INT32 accumulation, add terms of equal exponent weight (INT64), and reconstruct FP64 outputs from the scaling factors. The number of mantissa slices is data-dependent — this is not one kernel but a family — so cuBLAS ships an **automatic dynamic precision (ADP)** framework that inspects the inputs, decides whether emulation can beat native FP64 at equal-or-better accuracy, chooses the mantissa-bit budget (default ≥53 bits), and falls back to native FP64 otherwise. NVIDIA reports (vendor figures, GTC 2025 and the cuBLAS blog): FP32 emulation up to ~3× over native Blackwell FP32 and ~4× over Hopper; FP64 emulation up to ~2.8× over native Blackwell FP64 and ~1.5× over Hopper's native FP64; end-to-end application benchmarks around 1.5× by default and nearly 3× when tuned to 39 mantissa bits, with accuracy reported indistinguishable from native FP64 (12 significant decimal digits at the 39-bit setting). NVIDIA also states the opt-in propagates automatically to libraries built on cuBLAS — cuSOLVER's FP32-emulated QR/LU paths were cited at ~2.3× — and that emulation is being evaluated for HPL and dense LU/QR workloads.

**Why it matters.** This is Section 1.1's trade-off turned into a *product*, and it inverts the usual mixed-precision logic. HPL-MxP (modules 14–16, upcoming) changes the *algorithm*: factor cheaply at low precision, then repair with iterative refinement to reach FP64-accurate results. Emulation keeps the *algorithm and the interface* exactly FP64 and recovers the precision inside the BLAS kernel, by decomposing one wide-precision GEMM into many narrow-precision GEMMs — paying ~9+ inner multiplies for BF16x9 FP32, or a data-dependent count for FP64, against a 30–60× hardware rate advantage. It is also a perfect illustration of HPL-4.1's "contract" idea: the caller still calls DGEMM; the implementation freedom (which units, which precisions, how many inner products) was always the implementer's — and the accuracy guarantees are enforced by the ADP framework and tested against purpose-built BLAS-accuracy screens (Demmel et al.'s "how to grade a BLAS implementation" tests, which NVIDIA says its libraries are validated against, including not-gameable fixed-point variants).

**What to watch.** Whether NVIDIA flips emulation from opt-in to opt-out as announced; Ozaki-II support (a variant said to handle harder cases at lower overhead); support spreading to Ampere/Hopper-class GPUs; and — the interesting governance question — whether TOP500 HPL submissions can or should use emulated FP64. That debate rhymes with the one that produced HPL-MxP itself: what does it mean for a benchmark to "run FP64" when FP64 can be synthesized from cheaper arithmetic at equal accuracy?

### 2.3 BLIS 2.x: the BLAS layer keeps evolving on the CPU side

**What happened.** BLIS (the open-source BLAS library from the FLAME group at UT Austin) shipped **2.0** (June 2025) and **2.1** (June 2026). The headline features: a **plugin architecture** that lets users register custom microkernels — even from outside the BLIS source tree, compiled and runtime-selected by the framework as if they were native kernels; a **control-tree API** for defining custom GEMM-like level-3 operations by modifying BLIS's internal algorithmic decomposition (kernels, block sizes, packing formats); and full mixed-precision and mixed-domain support across level-3 operations (except TRSM).

**Why it matters, briefly.** BLIS's architecture — where a handful of hand-written microkernels per architecture (HPL-9.7, upcoming) drive every level-3 operation — has long been the cleanest public demonstration that "BLAS" is a framework, not a fixed set of kernels. The 2.x releases push that further: the microkernel is now a formal extension point, and mixed precision is a first-class citizen at the same layer where cuBLAS is adding emulation. For HPL practitioners, BLIS is a common DGEMM engine on CPU partitions (it was developed by the same research lineage — Goto's blocking strategy, van de Geijn's analytical models — that module 9 will cover), and its releases are a good barometer of where CPU dense-kernel engineering is going: register-blocked microkernels, runtime dispatch, and precision flexibility.

**What to watch.** Plugin adoption (vendor or third-party kernels for new ISAs landing without upstream patches), and whether mixed-precision level-3 APIs converge across BLIS, OpenBLAS, and the BLAS Technical Forum's mixed-precision extensions.
