## 0. Concise Summary

Last Wednesday (HPL-5.5–6.2) we established the algebraic identity at the heart of dense linear algebra: Gaussian elimination and LU factorization are the same computation, with partial pivoting upgrading \(A = LU\) to \(PA = LU\). Today we make that factorization *operational*. Five topics, one arc: how the factors are **used** (triangular solves), how they are **stored** (in place, with no extra memory), and how the factorization **computation itself is organized** — the outer-product view that converts elimination into GEMM, and the two great traversal orders, right-looking and left-looking, that every dense LU implementation must choose between.

First, **solving \(Ax=b\) using LU** (HPL-6.3): once \(PA = LU\), solving any system costs two triangular solves — forward substitution \(Ly = Pb\), then back substitution \(Ux = y\) — for \(2n^2\) flops, a factor of \(n/3\) cheaper than the \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) factorization. This asymmetry is the entire economic argument for factoring: pay once, solve many. Second, **in-place storage** (HPL-6.4): \(L\)'s strict lower triangle and \(U\)'s upper triangle occupy exactly complementary regions of the original array, because each eliminated entry is overwritten by the very multiplier that annihilated it; the permutation costs just \(n\) integers. Third, the **outer-product view** (HPL-6.5): each elimination step is a rank-1 update \(A^{(k)} = A^{(k-1)} - \ell_k a_k^T\), and blocking converts the whole recursion into one rank-\(b\) update \(A_{22} \leftarrow A_{22} - L_{21}U_{12}\) — a GEMM that carries over 99% of the flops at realistic block sizes. Fourth and fifth, the two loop orders: **right-looking** LU factors a panel then eagerly updates the entire trailing submatrix (the choice of both LAPACK's DGETRF and HPL's main loop), while **left-looking** LU defers all updates, factoring each panel only after accumulating every previous panel's contribution. They execute the *same* task graph — push versus pull — and trade large square GEMMs and fork-join parallelism against write locality and lower I/O.

In developments: an IPDPS 2025 paper finally makes pivoted LU run efficiently on task-based runtimes (Chameleon/StarPU) by attacking exactly the small-task and pivot-reduction pathologies that today's loop-structure analysis predicts; and the SLATE project's retrospective shows the right-looking skeleton surviving the exascale transition — with panels, pivoting, and communication, not GEMM, being what had to be re-engineered.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

Last week ended with a factorization looking for a job. We had shown that the multipliers produced by elimination assemble into a unit lower-triangular \(L\), that the surviving rows assemble into an upper-triangular \(U\), and that pivoting interleaves a permutation \(P\) so that \(PA = LU\). What we did *not* yet do is store the result, use it, or organize its computation for a machine. Those three moves — use, store, schedule — are today's material, and they are precisely the material out of which LAPACK, ScaLAPACK, HPL, and HPL-MxP are built.

### 1.1 Using the factorization: solving \(Ax = b\) (HPL-6.3)

**Why factor before solving?** A linear system could be solved by elimination plus back substitution in one pass — that is literally Gaussian elimination as we first met it (HPL-5.1–5.4). The reason to split the computation into *factor* then *solve* is reuse: the factorization depends only on \(A\), while the solve phase depends on \(b\). Factor once for \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) flops, then each new right-hand side costs only \(2n^2\). In an HPC simulation that steps a fixed discretization matrix through thousands of time steps or parameter studies, this factor-of-\(n/3\) amortization is the difference between tractable and impossible.

**The solve, mechanically.** Suppose we have \(PA = LU\), and we want \(Ax = b\). Write the system as \(PAx = Pb\), i.e.

\[
LUx = Pb .
\]

Define the intermediate vector \(y = Ux\). Then the solve splits into two *triangular* systems:

\[
Ly = Pb \qquad \text{(forward substitution)}, \qquad Ux = y \qquad \text{(back substitution)} .
\]

Each is solvable by simple recursion because its matrix is triangular. Forward substitution exploits the zeros *above* the diagonal of \(L\): the first equation already isolates \(y_1\), which feeds the second equation, and so on downward:

\[
y_1 = (Pb)_1, \qquad
y_i = (Pb)_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \ell_{ij}\, y_j, \quad i = 2,\dots,n .
\]

Back substitution exploits the zeros *below* the diagonal of \(U\) and runs upward from \(x_n\):

\[
x_n = \frac{y_n}{u_{nn}}, \qquad
x_i = \frac{1}{u_{ii}}\left( y_i - \sum_{j=i+1}^{n} u_{ij}\, x_j \right), \quad i = n-1,\dots,1 .
\]

The permutation step is not optional bookkeeping: the row interchanges performed during factorization must be applied to \(b\) in the same order, or the solves act on a scrambled system. In LAPACK this is exactly what the `LASWP`-then-`TRSM` sequence inside the driver routines `DGETRS` does: apply the recorded interchanges to \(b\), then call the triangular-solve kernels. (HPL-7.3 will revisit permutation mechanics; today we only need "apply the swaps, then solve.")

**A worked example, continued from last week.** Take the matrix from the 2026-11-04 volume,

\[
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\quad
U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix},
\]

where no row swaps occurred, so \(P = I\). Choose \(b = (4,\,10,\,24)^T\). Forward substitution on \(Ly = b\):

\[
y_1 = 4;\qquad
y_2 = 10 - 2\cdot 4 = 2;\qquad
y_3 = 24 - 4\cdot 4 - 3\cdot 2 = 2 .
\]

Back substitution on \(Ux = y\):

\[
x_3 = 2/2 = 1;\qquad
x_2 = 2 - 1\cdot 1 = 1;\qquad
x_1 = (4 - 1 - 1)/2 = 1 .
\]

So \(x = (1,1,1)^T\) — which is no accident: \(b = A(1,1,1)^T\) by construction, and the solves recovered it exactly. The point of doing it by hand is to see the *data dependency*: every \(y_i\) waits on all earlier \(y_j\); every \(x_i\) waits on all later \(x_j\). A triangular solve is a length-\(n\) recurrence, not a big parallel reduction.

**Cost and character.** Forward substitution performs \(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i-1)\) multiply-adds, i.e. \(n^2 + O(n)\) flops; back substitution the same. Total: \(2n^2\), against \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) for the factorization — a ratio of \(3/n\). At \(n = 10{,}000\), the solve is one part in 1,500 of the factorization cost; at HPL scale (\(n \sim 10^7\)), it is one part in ten million.

But notice the *character* of those \(2n^2\) flops. The solve reads all \(n^2\) entries of \(L\) and \(U\) (8 bytes each in FP64) while doing \(2n^2\) flops — an arithmetic intensity of roughly 0.25 flop/byte, deep in memory-bound territory (HPL-2.6). And because each component depends on its predecessors, parallelism is limited to the inner dot products. This is why the solve and residual phases of an HPL run, while a rounding error of the runtime, are completely different performance animals from the factorization: latency- and bandwidth-bound recurrences versus a compute-bound GEMM storm. HPL-22.3 and HPL-22.4 will formalize this phase-by-phase bottleneck map; file today's observation as the first entry.

Two related points. First, with *many* right-hand sides stacked as columns of a matrix \(B\), the same recursions become \(L Y = PB\) and \(U X = Y\) — triangular solves with multiple right-hand sides, which is the Level-3 BLAS `TRSM`. The "block row of \(U\)" computed inside every step of blocked LU is precisely this operation, as section 1.3 will show. Second, never compute \(A^{-1}\) to solve systems: forming the inverse costs about \(2n^3\) flops — three times the factorization — and using it (\(x = A^{-1}b\), another \(2n^2\)) is generally *less* accurate than the two triangular solves. The factorization is the reusable object; the inverse is a wasteful intermediary.

### 1.2 In-place storage: two triangles, one array (HPL-6.4)

**The observation.** \(L\) is unit lower triangular: all entries above its diagonal are structurally zero, and its diagonal is all ones. \(U\) is upper triangular: everything strictly below its diagonal is zero. The *nonzero* parts of \(L\) and \(U\) therefore live in disjoint triangles of an \(n \times n\) grid:

```
        n columns                    n columns
      ┌─────────────┐              ┌─────────────┐
      │ U U U U U U │              │ ∙ ∙ ∙ ∙ ∙ ∙ │   ∙ = structurally zero
      │ L U U U U U │              │ L ∙ ∙ ∙ ∙ ∙ │   (never stored, never
      │ L L U U U U │      +       │ L L ∙ ∙ ∙ ∙ │    needed explicitly)
      │ L L L U U U │              │ L L L ∙ ∙ ∙ │
      │ L L L L U U │              │ L L L L ∙ ∙ │
      │ L L L L L U │              │ L L L L L ∙ │
      └─────────────┘              └─────────────┘
        strict lower triangle        everything else
        = multipliers ℓ_ij           = U (diag + upper)
```

The union of the two stored regions is exactly the footprint of the original matrix \(A\). So the factorization can destroy \(A\) and leave \(L\) and \(U\) in the *same* array — LAPACK's DGETRF documents precisely this contract: "On exit, the factors L and U from the factorization A = P*L*U; the unit diagonal elements of L are not stored."

**Why the overwriting is safe — the mechanism.** During elimination of column \(k\), every entry \(a_{ik}\) below the pivot is annihilated. A zero needs no storage. The multiplier \(\\ell_{ik} = a_{ik}^{(k-1)} / a_{kk}^{(k-1)}\) — the one number we must remember to build \(L\) — is written into exactly the slot that just became zero. Storage is reclaimed at the moment it is created. There is no aliasing hazard because after step \(k\), the eliminated entries of column \(k\) are never read again by the *remaining* elimination steps (they only matter as \(L\)-entries for the trailing update, which is precisely how they are used).

For the running example, the array on exit holds

\[
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf{2} & 1 & 1 \\
2 & \mathbf{1} & 1 \\
4 & 3 & \mathbf{2}
\end{pmatrix}
\]

where bold diagonal-and-above entries are \(U\)'s \(\{2,1,1,1,1,2\}\) and the below-diagonal entries \(\{2,4,3\}\) are \(L\)'s multipliers. Reconstructing \(L\) mentally means "imagine ones on the diagonal"; reconstructing \(U\), "ignore the lower triangle." An entire factorization of a million-by-million matrix fits in the space the input already occupied.

**The permutation is almost free.** A permutation matrix \(P\) would cost \(n^2\) storage — as much as the matrix itself. Instead, LAPACK records an integer vector `IPIV` of length \(\min(M,N)\), with the documented semantics: "row \(i\) of the matrix was interchanged with row IPIV(\(i\))." Applying \(P\) to a vector or matrix row means replaying these pairwise swaps *in order* — an \(O(n)\)-space, \(O(n)\)-swap representation of an \(O(n^2)\) object. The routine `DLASWP` does nothing but apply this swap sequence to a block of columns. Notice the efficiency pattern: LU's expensive part is \(O(n^3)\) arithmetic, its storage is \(O(n^2)\), and its permutation metadata is \(O(n)\). Every layer of the algorithm's structure shrinks by a factor of \(n\).

**Why HPC cares: the memory arithmetic.** In-place storage is not an aesthetic nicety; the matrix *is* the memory budget of dense HPC. Storage is \(8n^2\) bytes in FP64. As a rough illustration (author's arithmetic, not a measured system): a problem size \(n = 10^7\) — the order of a leadership-class HPL run — needs \(8 \times 10^{14}\) bytes = 800 TB of matrix, split across the machine's ranks; on 10,000 ranks that is 80 GB of matrix per rank, which is why HPL problem-size selection (HPL-13.1, HPL-13.2) is fundamentally a memory-occupancy calculation. A second copy of the matrix — which separate \(L\) and \(U\) arrays would require — would halve the maximum \(n\) and thus cap the \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) flop count a machine can reach in reasonable time. In-place storage directly buys benchmark score.

A practical footnote: Fortran stores matrices column-major with a *leading dimension* `LDA` — the allocated stride between columns, which may exceed the logical number of rows. Kernels step through memory with that stride; panel operations and cache alignment are why tuned codes care about it (HPL-9.6 will meet packing and layout again at the BLAS level).

### 1.3 The outer-product view: elimination as rank-1 updates (HPL-6.5)

**From elimination matrices to rank-1 updates.** Last week each elimination step was written as multiplication by a Gauss transform, \(A^{(k)} = M_k A^{(k-1)}\) with \(M_k = I - \ell_k e_k^T\). Multiply the expression out:

\[
A^{(k)} = (I - \ell_k e_k^T)\, A^{(k-1)} = A^{(k-1)} - \ell_k \big( e_k^T A^{(k-1)} \big) = A^{(k-1)} - \ell_k\, a_k^T,
\]

where \(a_k^T = e_k^T A^{(k-1)}\) is simply **row \(k\) of the current matrix** — the pivot row. The update subtracts from every remaining row \(i\) its scaled copy of the pivot row, with scale factor \(\ell_{ik}\). The product \(\ell_k a_k^T\) is an \((n\times 1) \times (1 \times n)\) matrix product: a **rank-1 matrix**. So Gaussian elimination is the repeated application of rank-1 updates to the trailing submatrix. (Rank-1 because the outer product of two vectors spans a one-dimensional space; this is the same "outer product" as in AXPY-land, generalized.)

Check it on the running example. Step 1: \(\ell_1 = (0, 2, 4)^T\), pivot row \(a_1^T = (2,1,1)\), so

\[
\ell_1 a_1^T = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&0 \\ 4&2&2 \\ 8&4&4 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A^{(1)} = A - \ell_1 a_1^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2&1&1 \\ 0&1&1 \\ 0&3&5 \end{pmatrix},
\]

matching last week's \(M_1 A\) exactly. Step 2 subtracts \(\ell_2 a_2^T\) with \(\ell_2 = (0,0,3)^T\), \(a_2^T = (0,1,1)\), producing the final \(U\). The rank-1 picture and the Gauss-transform picture are algebraically identical; the rank-1 picture is the one that *blocks*.

**Blocking the recursion: the \(2\times 2\) partition.** Now partition the matrix after \(b\) columns, where \(b\) is a block size:

\[
A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
L = \begin{pmatrix} L_{11} & 0 \\ L_{21} & L_{22} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U = \begin{pmatrix} U_{11} & U_{12} \\ 0 & U_{22} \end{pmatrix},
\]

with \(A_{11}, L_{11}, U_{11}\) of size \(b \times b\). Multiply out \(A = LU\) and match blocks:

\[
A_{11} = L_{11} U_{11}, \qquad
A_{12} = L_{11} U_{12}, \qquad
A_{21} = L_{21} U_{11}, \qquad
A_{22} = L_{21} U_{12} + L_{22} U_{22}.
\]

Read these as a *recipe*:

1. **Factor the leading block**: \(A_{11} = L_{11} U_{11}\) — recursively (it is just a smaller LU).
2. **Block row of \(U\)**: from \(U_{12} = L_{11}^{-1} A_{12}\) — a triangular solve with \(n - b\) right-hand sides: **TRSM**.
3. **Block column of \(L\)**: from \(L_{21} = A_{21} U_{11}^{-1}\) — another **TRSM**.
4. **Trailing update**: rearrange the last equation as
   \[
   \underbrace{A_{22} - L_{21} U_{12}}_{\text{Schur complement}} = L_{22} U_{22},
   \]
   so first update \(A_{22} \leftarrow A_{22} - L_{21} U_{12}\) — a rank-\(b\) update: **GEMM** — then recurse on the result.

The trailing update is where the rank-1 picture pays off: \(b\) consecutive rank-1 updates \(-\ell_k a_k^T\) for \(k = 1..b\) assemble into the single rank-\(b\) product \(-L_{21} U_{12}\), computed by one GEMM call. The unblocked recurrence has an innermost operation of intensity \(< 1\); the blocked update streams two rectangular blocks once and does \(2(n-b)^2 b\) flops of work with them.

**The flop split — why this is *the* engine.** For one step with trailing matrix \((n-b) \times (n-b)\), counting as in HPL-2.1:

- the two TRSMs cost about \(2 b^2 (n-b)\) flops combined;
- the GEMM costs \(2 (n-b)^2 b\) flops.

The ratio is \((n-b)/b \approx n/b\). With a realistic division — say \(n = 102{,}400\) and \(b = 256\), i.e. 400 block columns — the GEMM carries roughly 400× the TRSM work, over 99.7% of each step's flops. Summed over all steps, the same ratio holds: **blocked LU is, to first order, a sequence of GEMMs.** This single identity — factor a small piece, TRSM two strips, GEMM the rest — is why GEMM performance is the ceiling of dense LU performance (HPL-9.1), why BLAS quality dominates library quality (HPL-4.10), and why HPL-MxP's acceleration strategy is fundamentally "run the GEMMs on tensor cores" (HPL-16.3). It also previews the block-size trade-off: larger \(b\) raises the GEMM share but enlarges the panel that must be factored by weak Level-2 work — the subject of HPL-8.6 once blocking is formalized.

### 1.4 Right-looking LU: factor a panel, update everything (HPL-6.6)

The \(2\times 2\) recipe above is *one step*. A full factorization needs a policy for sweeping the whole matrix, and the first great policy is **right-looking**: at every step, factor the current panel, then *immediately* apply its consequences to everything to the right and below.

**The algorithm.** With \(A\) partitioned at each step around the current block column \(J\) of width \(JB\),

```text
for J = 1 .. n step NB:                       # main loop over panels
    JB = min(NB, n-J+1)
    (1) Factor the panel:      A(J:M, J:J+JB-1)  →  L₁₁, U₁₁, pivots
        (recursive/unblocked LU on the tall-skinny panel)
    (2) Swap left:   apply the panel's row interchanges to columns 1:J-1
        (the already-finished block rows of U)
    (3) Swap right:  apply the same interchanges to columns J+JB:N
        (the trailing submatrix)
    (4) Block row of U:        A(J, J+JB:N)   ←  L₁₁⁻¹ · A(J, J+JB:N)      [TRSM]
    (5) Trailing update:       A(J+JB:, J+JB:) −=  A(J+JB:, J:J+JB-1)
                                              ·  A(J:J+JB-1, J+JB:)       [GEMM]
```

This is not a paraphrase; it is the annotated structure of LAPACK's `DGETRF`, whose own header declares it "the right-looking Level 3 BLAS version of the algorithm." Step (1) calls the recursive routine `DGETRF2` on the panel; steps (2)–(3) are two `DLASWP` calls; step (4) is a `DTRSM`; step (5) is a `DGEMM`. (The same structure appears recursively *inside* `DGETRF2`: factor the top half, swap the right half, TRSM, GEMM, factor the bottom-right, then swap the bottom-left — the left/right swap pair propagating at every level.)

**A subtlety worth internalizing: why swap the *finished* columns?** Step (2) surprises newcomers — why should pivots found while factoring column block \(J\) touch columns \(1..J-1\), which are already done? Because a row interchange reorders *entire rows*, and rows of the finished block columns hold rows of \(U\). Row \(i\) of \(U\) and row \(j\) of \(U\) must end up permuted consistently with how rows of \(L\) and of the trailing matrix are permuted, or the identity \(PA = LU\) silently breaks. Partial pivoting's row swaps are global-row events; the factorization applies them everywhere eagerly (both left and right of the panel) precisely because it is right-looking — the "rest of the matrix" is being kept *current* at all times. Section 1.5 shows the alternative.

**Why "right-looking"?** The name describes the data-access pattern: at each step the algorithm *writes* to the right of and below the current block column. The trailing submatrix is the active region; everything to the left is finished and frozen. Equivalently: updates are **eager**. Each step's rank-\(JB\) update is applied to the trailing matrix *now*, not later.

**Properties that follow from the structure.**

- *Big, square-ish GEMMs.* The trailing update at step \(J\) multiplies three large blocks — for early \(J\), nearly the whole matrix. Large GEMMs are exactly what BLAS libraries execute best (HPL-9.9, HPL-9.10), so the dominant flops run near peak.
- *Simple parallelism.* Each GEMM/TRSM is internally parallel; a fork-join implementation just links multithreaded BLAS and lets each call use all cores. LAPACK's DGETRF is the canonical example. The cost, as the FLAME group's look-ahead work observed, is that *implicit synchronization points exist between each call to a parallelized BLAS routine* — every kernel boundary is a join.
- *A serial critical path.* The loop body is sequential: panel → swaps → TRSM → GEMM → next panel. The next panel cannot start until the GEMM has updated the columns it will factor. This dependency chain — not lack of parallelism inside the GEMM — is what limits scalability of the plain right-looking loop, and it is precisely what **look-ahead** relaxes by starting the next panel while the current trailing update is still in flight (previewed at HPL-8.8/8.9, HPL-11.6, and central to HPL-12.9).
- *Write amplification.* Every step reads and rewrites the entire remaining trailing submatrix. In cache, the hardware absorbs this. Out of core or across a network, rewriting the whole trailing matrix each step means moving \(O(n^3/b)\) words of I/O or communication — the weakness that motivates the alternative below (and later, communication-avoiding algorithms, HPL-18).

**Who uses it.** Both of the dense-LU heavyweights chose right-looking for their main loops: LAPACK's DGETRF, as cited above, and HPL itself — the benchmark's algorithm description states plainly: "The right-looking variant has been chosen for the main loop of the LU factorization. This means that at each iteration of the loop a panel of nb columns is factorized, and the trailing submatrix is updated." At HPL scale the GEMM is distributed over a 2D process grid (HPL-10, HPL-11), and look-ahead hides the panel critical path — but the skeleton is today's loop. One elegant detail: HPL's *panel factorization itself* offers a choice of Crout, left-, and right-looking recursive variants. The orderings compose hierarchically — a right-looking main loop whose panels may be factored left-looking — because each variant is just a schedule of the same task graph, which is the point of the next section.

### 1.5 Left-looking LU: accumulate updates, then factor (HPL-6.7)

The second policy inverts the timing. Instead of eagerly pushing each panel's update across the whole trailing matrix, **left-looking** LU touches one block column at a time: it factors panel \(J\) only *after* pulling in the contributions of all previously computed panels \(1..J-1\), and it never touches anything to the right of \(J\).

**The algorithm.** LAPACK ships this variant in its source tree under `SRC/VARIANTS/lu/LL/dgetrf.f`; its structure (annotations mine):

```text
for J = 1 .. n step NB:                                # one block column at a time
    JB = min(NB, n-J+1)
    for K = 1 .. J-NB step NB:                         # sweep over finished panels
        (1) Apply panel K's pending row swaps
            to the current panel's columns J:J+JB-1                      [LASWP]
        (2) Block-row-of-U piece:  A(K:K+NB-1, J:J+JB-1)
                                   ←  L(K,K)⁻¹ · A(K:K+NB-1, J:J+JB-1)   [TRSM]
        (3) Update piece:  A(K+NB:, J:J+JB-1)
                           −=  A(K+NB:, K:K+NB-1) · A(K:K+NB-1, J:J+JB-1) [GEMM]
    (4) Factor the now fully updated panel:  A(J:M, J:J+JB-1)  →  L, U, pivots
        (unblocked LU, e.g. DGETF2)
```

Read the inner loop carefully: for the *current* panel, the algorithm walks over every earlier panel \(K\), applies \(K\)'s interchanges to the current panel's rows (just-in-time — those swaps were found long ago but never applied to these columns), computes the strip of \(U\)'s block row that panel \(K\) is responsible for, and subtracts the corresponding rank-\(NB\) piece. Only when all previous panels' contributions have been accumulated does the algorithm factor the panel. After step (4), the column block \(J\) is finished and never written again.

**Why "left-looking"?** All reads come from the *left* of the current block column (the finished \(L\) and \(U\) panels); all writes stay *inside* the current block column. The out-of-core analysis by Kurzak, Luszczek, and Dongarra (LAPACK Working Note 110) puts it exactly: "data accesses all occur to the left of the block column being updated. Moreover, the only write access occurs within this block column."

**Push versus pull: the same DAG, two traversals.** Here is the conceptual heart of the right/left distinction. Both algorithms execute the *same* set of primitive operations — the same panel factorizations, the same TRSM strips, the same rank-\(NB\) GEMM pieces — and honor the *same* dependencies (a panel can only be factored after all updates into it are complete). They are two topological orders of one task graph. Right-looking **pushes** each update as early as possible (eager); left-looking **pulls** each update as late as possible (lazy). The mathematics (section 1.3's block equations) is untouched; only the schedule differs. This graph view is the modern way to see all LU variants, and it is exactly the abstraction task-based runtimes exploit (see Developments).

**Properties, and the trade.**

- *Write locality.* Only the current block column is ever written. For out-of-core factorization — matrix on disk, working set in RAM — LAWN 110's I/O model shows the left-looking variant moves meaningfully less data than right-looking (in their model, roughly \(M^3/(2\,nb)\) read-dominant traffic versus the right-looking variant's read-plus-rewrite of the whole trailing matrix each step, for an \(M \times M\) matrix with block \(nb\)); the paper builds its parallel OOC LU on the left-looking variant for this reason. The same write-avoidance logic reappears on any storage hierarchy where writes are expensive — including distributed memory, where "less written" translates to "less communicated."
- *Weaker per-step parallelism.* While right-looking updates a large trailing matrix (thousands of independent GEMM tiles), left-looking works on a single tall-skinny panel at a time, and its inner sweep is a *chain* of dependent update pieces. LAWN 110 notes the left-looking algorithm "has less inherent parallelism since it acts only on single blocks," and proposes a hybrid — grouping several block columns into wider "superblocks," right-looking within a superblock and left-looking across superblocks — trading I/O volume against parallelism.
- *More, smaller GEMMs.* The update work for panel \(J\) arrives as \(J/NB - 1\) separate GEMM pieces with tall-skinny right-hand sides (HPL-19.2 territory), rather than one large square GEMM. Kernel efficiency suffers accordingly (HPL-9.9).
- *Read amplification in cache.* Every finished panel is re-read by every subsequent panel — \(O(n^2/b)\) block-panel interactions overall. In RAM this is cheap; it is the disk/network context where the trade flips in left-looking's favor, because re-reading the left region beats re-reading *and rewriting* the right region.

**Who uses it.** LAPACK's standard DGETRF is right-looking; the left-looking variant lives in the VARIANTS tree precisely because, for in-cache, fork-join, multithreaded-BLAS execution, the right-looking order's big GEMMs win. Left-looking shines where writes are dear: out-of-core solvers, and conceptually the "deferred update" idea behind communication-avoiding formulations (HPL-18.3) and modern look-ahead schedulers, which effectively run right-looking GEMMs lazily enough to start the next panel early. One more data point: LAPACK Working Note 19's study of block algorithm variants is the classic reference for how these orderings were evaluated when the Level-3 BLAS standard crystallized — the "i, j, k variants" naming it popularizes refers to the six possible orderings of the three nested loops, of which right- and left-looking are the two families that matter today; the third, Crout-style, arrives next volume (HPL-6.8).

### 1.6 Synthesis: one algorithm, two schedules

| Dimension | Right-looking (HPL-6.6) | Left-looking (HPL-6.7) |
|---|---|---|
| Update timing | Eager: push immediately | Lazy: pull just before factoring |
| Region written each step | Entire trailing submatrix | Current block column only |
| Region read each step | Current panel | All finished panels (the left) |
| GEMM shape | Large, square-ish | Many tall-skinny pieces |
| Parallelism per step | High (whole trailing matrix) | Low (one panel, dependent chain) |
| I/O / communication volume | Higher (rewrites everything right) | Lower (LAWN 110 OOC result) |
| Fork-join friendliness | Excellent (multithreaded BLAS) | Poor |
| Critical path | Panel-after-GEMM serialization | Update-chain before each panel |
| Chosen by | LAPACK DGETRF, HPL main loop | OOC LU, VARIANTS, hybrid designs |

The deeper lesson is that "the algorithm" — the mathematics of section 1.3 — is separable from "the schedule." Right- and left-looking compute identical \(L\), \(U\), and `IPIV`; they differ only in when each task fires. Once you see LU as a task graph with panel, TRSM, and GEMM nodes, every serious implementation becomes readable as a scheduling policy: fork-join right-looking (LAPACK), look-ahead right-looking (HPL, with its tunable depths and broadcast topologies), task-DAG with runtime scheduling (PLASMA/Chameleon/StarPU, SLATE), or hybrid superblocked forms for out-of-core. HPL-MxP adds one more axis — precision per task — without changing the graph. From here the curriculum climbs the same ladder the libraries did: blocking formalized (HPL-8), GEMM's anatomy (HPL-9), distribution across process grids (HPL-10, HPL-11), and HPL itself as a parameterized instance of today's skeleton (HPL-12, HPL-13).

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 Pivoted LU finally runs well on task-based runtimes (IPDPS 2025)

**What happened.** At IPDPS 2025 (pp. 1011–1022), Lisito, Faverge, Kuhn, Pruvost, and Ramet (Eviden; Univ. Bordeaux/CNRS/Bordeaux INP/INRIA/LaBRI) published "Scalable and portable LU factorization with partial pivoting on top of runtime systems," implementing LU with partial pivoting — the HPL algorithm — using the sequential task-flow model in the CHAMELEON library on the STARPU runtime, and closing most of the gap to hand-tuned pivoted codes.

**What technically changed.** The paper attacks two structural problems that map directly onto today's material. First, task granularity: pivoting decomposes the panel into a huge number of very small tasks (pivot searches, row swaps, rank-1 eliminations), which overwhelm the runtime's scheduler — the task-graph view of section 1.6 is only as good as the runtime's ability to schedule it. Their remedies are blocking strategies adapted to task-based codes and batching of task submission. Second, the distributed critical path: partial pivoting forces a reduction (pivot election across the panel's process column) at every step of every panel — exactly the serial dependency chain of sections 1.4–1.5, now implemented as runtime-managed reductions. They design two task-based reduction algorithms for these and show improved runtime reactivity on the critical path. The reported outcome: their pivoted LU asymptotically reaches the performance of the *non-pivoting* algorithm on their cluster — i.e., the price of numerical safety (HPL-7) has been driven to nearly zero at task level.

**Why it matters.** The authors note that HPL itself — the TOP500's yardstick — has never been successfully implemented on task-based runtime systems; this work is the credible path there, because it solves the two things that made runtime-based HPL fail: tiny-task overload and pivot reductions. It is also a live demonstration of the fork-join critique from the FLAME look-ahead line of work: implicit synchronization at every BLAS call boundary (section 1.4) is what task graphs were invented to remove.

**What to watch.** Whether runtime-scheduled LU can hold up at TOP500 scale — where panel broadcasts, not task overhead, dominate — and whether these batching/reduction techniques migrate into SLATE-class libraries.

### 2.2 SLATE's exascale retrospective: the right-looking skeleton survives, everything around it changes

**What happened.** The SLATE project (Software for Linear Algebra Targeting Exascale; ICL, University of Tennessee), the DOE Exascale Computing Project's distributed dense linear algebra library and intended ScaLAPACK successor, published a retrospective — "Evolution of the SLATE linear algebra library" — documenting a decade of design changes; the library's latest release at the time of writing is v2025.05.28.

**What technically changed.** Several reversals, all instructive for today's loop structure. SLATE's original design was hybrid CPU–GPU: one large batched trailing-matrix update (the GEMM of step 5 in section 1.4) on the GPU, while panel factorization and look-ahead ran on the CPU. As GPUs outpaced CPUs, the CPU-side panel work became the bottleneck — the critical-path analysis of section 1.4 biting at machine scale — so panel operations moved to the GPUs, leaving modern SLATE algorithms GPU-resident (their Cholesky, with panels moved on-device, gained a reported 1.5× on 72 Summit nodes). Host-to-host MPI was replaced by device-to-device transfers. And the algorithm menu widened beyond classic right-looking LU: Communication-Avoiding LU and the Random Butterfly Transform (a pivoting replacement) were added. Throughout, portability across NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel accelerators stayed a design constraint.

**Why it matters.** SLATE is the existence proof that today's curriculum skeleton — panel, TRSM, GEMM, with pivoting — is the *durable* part of the software stack, while the schedule, the device placement, and the pivoting strategy are the parts that get re-engineered per hardware era. Every change the paper describes is a response to one of the bottleneck categories the curriculum formalizes later: panel serialization (HPL-8.8, HPL-20.2), communication on the critical path (HPL-20.5), and pivoting as a parallel bottleneck (HPL-7.7, HPL-7.8).

**What to watch.** Adoption of SLATE over ScaLAPACK in ECP-descendant applications, and whether CA-LU-style variants (HPL-18.3) move from research options to defaults as machine balance tilts further toward compute.

### 2.3 A date to watch: SC26 and the November TOP500

The next TOP500 list is due November 17 at SC26 in Chicago (as previewed in the 2026-11-04 volume). Every entry on it, from the exaflop leaders down, will be measured by the algorithm studied today: a right-looking, partially pivoted, look-ahead LU factorization of a distributed block-cyclic matrix — with the GEMM share derived in section 1.3 explaining why the ranking is, to first order, a ranking of sustained GEMM throughput.
