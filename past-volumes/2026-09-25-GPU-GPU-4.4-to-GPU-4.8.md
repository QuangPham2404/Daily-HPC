## 0. Concise Summary

The last GPU volume (September 21) built the top of the memory hierarchy: registers, the shared-memory/LDS scratchpad, and the argument that every level exists to filter traffic for the level below. Today we descend the rest of the ladder — L1, L2, HBM — and then turn around and study the two **contracts** the hardware expects software to honor while moving data through it.

The five topics form one continuous argument. The **L1 cache** (GPU-4.4) is the SM-local filter: a small cache fused into the same physical SRAM as shared memory, whose job is to shape a warp's scattered requests into dense, sector-aligned traffic. The **L2 cache** (GPU-4.5) is the chip-wide level where all global traffic converges — the coherence point between SMs, the place where global atomics execute, and the last opportunity to filter DRAM traffic. **HBM** (GPU-4.6) is the bottom rung: not a monolithic "VRAM" but a stack of DRAM dies exposing independent channels and pseudo-channels, whose 256-bit access granularity is the physical origin of the GPU's 32-byte memory sector. **Coalescing** (GPU-4.7) is the software side of that contract: how the 32 addresses named by one warp instruction become the minimum number of 32-byte sectors — the difference between four sectors and thirty-two per warp load is an 8× swing in effective bandwidth. **Bank conflicts** (GPU-4.8) are the same lesson one level up: shared memory is 32 parallel banks, and conflicting accesses serialize into replays exactly the way uncoalesced accesses waste sectors.

Three developments anchor the material in the real world. First, HBM4E sample shipments — Samsung in May 2026, SK hynix in June 2026 — push per-stack bandwidth to 3.6–4 TB/s, and the derivation is exactly today's stack arithmetic. Second, at Hot Chips and SEMICON Taiwan 2026 the memory makers declared the memory wall *widening*, with Samsung proposing zHBM — DRAM stacked directly on top of the processor — as the way through. Third, Blackwell's 126 MB partitioned L2 and MI350's 256 MB memory-side Infinity Cache are two rival answers to "where does the chip-wide cache live?", a live architectural fork you can now read fluently.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 The L1 cache: the SM's traffic shaper (GPU-4.4)

**The problem.** An SM issues memory instructions on behalf of whole warps — 32 addresses per instruction — while DRAM sits roughly half a thousand cycles away. Something close to the execution units must absorb the requests, serve repeated hits cheaply, and turn whatever misses into dense traffic for the levels below. That is the L1.

**The mechanism.** On every NVIDIA SM since Volta, the L1 data cache, the texture cache, and shared memory are **one physical SRAM**, presented as two logical resources (GPU-4.3 covered the scratchpad role). Its total size and the split between the two personalities are architectural facts worth memorizing for the current generation. On Hopper (compute capability 9.0): **256 KB of combined L1/texture/shared per SM**, with a runtime-selectable *carveout* that can assign 0, 8, 16, 32, 64, 100, 132, 164, 196, or 228 KB to shared memory (`cudaFuncSetAttribute` with `cudaFuncAttributePreferredSharedMemoryCarveout`). CUDA reserves 1 KB per thread block for its own use, so a single block can address at most 227 KB of shared memory. A100 was 192 KB combined / 164 KB shared; Hopper grew both by about a third. The consumer Blackwell GB202 keeps the same structure at smaller sizes, and — as we saw on September 14 — Blackwell datacenter parts add a *new* tier, Tensor Memory, above this level; today we stay with the classic two.

For **global loads**, L1 works like any cache — but its geometry is unusual and important: the line size is **128 bytes, divided into four 32-byte sectors**. The sector, not the line, is the unit of traffic between L1 and L2: a load that touches one word of a line pulls only the 32-byte sector containing it, not the whole line. Sectors exist because, as we will see in Section 1.3, 32 bytes is the natural quantum of the DRAM system underneath. NVIDIA's own description of the L1's second job is worth internalizing: it "acts as a coalescing buffer for memory accesses, gathering up the data requested by the threads of a warp before delivery of the data to the warp" (Hopper Tuning Guide). Section 1.4 is precisely about how well the warp's addresses gather.

For **global stores**, L1 steps aside: stores stream through to L2 at sector granularity and are not cached in L1. L1 is a *read filter* for global memory (plus the shared-memory scratchpad). The practical consequence: read-heavy kernels with reuse benefit from L1 sizing; pure streaming stores do not, and their cost lands directly on L2 and DRAM. Read-only data has an additional path — `const __restrict__` pointers and `__ldg()` map to the read-only/texture path, which historically issues efficiently and avoids write-related invalidation logic.

**What L1 is *not*: coherent across SMs.** Each SM's L1 is private, and the hardware does not keep the 100+ L1s mutually coherent. If SM 0 writes address X and SM 1 later reads X, the value is guaranteed to flow through the L2 — the point where all SMs meet (Section 1.5 of the September 14 volume discussed synchronization; today's Section 1.2 gives its memory-system counterpart). This is a deliberate trade: cache coherence traffic across a full GPU would consume exactly the bandwidth and die area the per-SM filter exists to save. Instead, the CUDA memory model makes inter-SM visibility a *scope* question, resolved at L2, and intra-block ordering a barrier question (`__syncthreads()`), resolved within the SM.

**Latency and role in the hierarchy.** Approximate, microbenchmark-derived numbers for a modern datacenter GPU: a shared-memory or L1-hit access costs on the order of 20–40 cycles; an L2 hit around 200; a DRAM round trip several hundred. L1's bandwidth is matched to the SM's issue rate — it can feed a warp instruction per cycle when accesses are conflict-free and localized. That is the design intent of the whole hierarchy from GPU-4.1: registers feed arithmetic, L1/shared filter and reshape, L2 aggregates, HBM supplies bulk. Two knobs pull against each other: a bigger shared-memory carveout gives the kernel more programmer-managed reuse but less L1 for implicit caching, and (as Module 5 will quantify) shared-memory demand per block also limits how many blocks reside per SM.

### 1.2 The L2 cache: the chip's coherence point (GPU-4.5)

**The problem.** Dozens of SMs share one address space and one DRAM system. Something must (a) absorb and combine their traffic, (b) buffer writes back to DRAM in dense bursts, (c) serve as the single place where a memory location has one authoritative on-chip copy, and (d) execute the read-modify-write sequences that atomics require. That something is L2.

**The mechanism.** *Every* global-memory access — load, store, atomic, and even local-memory spill traffic — traverses L2. As NVIDIA engineers put it, L2 is effectively a proxy for device memory: writes land in L2 and are written back to DRAM only on eviction (write-back policy), so a producer kernel and a consumer kernel can exchange data entirely through L2 without DRAM ever seeing it. L2 is also **partitioned**: it is built from slices attached to the memory partitions, each sitting near its own HBM controllers, with addresses hashed so that consecutive sectors spread across partitions. The partitioning matters twice — it multiplies L2 bandwidth, and it means an SM's "near" partition (low latency, high bandwidth) differs from its "far" ones, a distinction that microbenchmarks measure and that Blackwell's dual-die design sharpens (Section 2.3).

**Where atomics execute.** Global atomic operations are resolved *at the L2 slice that owns the target line* — not in the SMs. This placement is the hardware answer to the read-modify-write hazard: serialize conflicting updates at the one place that holds the authoritative copy. The cost profile is instructive. An uncontended atomic costs roughly the L2 round-trip (~200 cycles); contended atomics on the same line serialize at the slice. A single accumulator hammered by every thread in a grid is the canonical pathology — the line lives permanently in L2 (a high L2 hit rate!) yet throughput collapses, because hits do not skip the serialization. The standard fixes — privatized per-block accumulators in shared memory, warp-shuffle reductions (GPU-2.8), then one atomic per block — reduce contention by the privatization factor, and are among the highest-leverage rewrites in GPU programming.

**Capacity trend, and why it is exploding.**

| GPU | L2 capacity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A100 (2020) | 40 MB | single die |
| H100 (2022) | 50 MB | Hopper Tuning Guide |
| GB200 / Blackwell (2024) | 126 MB | Blackwell Tuning Guide; B200 reports four partitions — double Hopper's two (microbenchmark paper, arXiv:2512.02189) |
| GB202 consumer Blackwell | 128 MB (96 MB on RTX 5090) | RTX Blackwell whitepaper |
| AMD MI350X | 4 MB per XCD (×8 = 32 MB) **plus** 256 MB memory-side Infinity Cache | ROCm docs; Tom's Hardware |

A 2.5× generational jump (H100 → GB200) is not incremental tuning. It reflects what L2 is *for* in an era of 8 TB/s DRAM: filtering. NVIDIA's Blackwell-class L2 at ~126 MB can hold the entire active weight set of mid-size models or the halo regions of large stencil problems, converting DRAM streams into L2 streams (measured L2 hit rates in one Blackwell study rose from 68% to 84% as precision dropped, precisely because smaller operands fit better). Micron-scale irony: the "cache" is now one of the largest SRAM structures anyone fabricates.

**Programmer control.** Since Ampere, CUDA exposes L2 *persistence*: `cudaDeviceSetLimit(cudaLimitPersistingL2CacheSize, size)` sets aside up to typically 75% of L2, and per-stream *access policy windows* mark a global-memory address range as persisting (prioritized in the set-aside) or streaming (evicted early). For an HPC kernel with a small, hot working set — a lookup table, a coarse grid reused every timestep — this is a genuine tuning knob; Nsight Compute's L2 hit-rate counters tell you whether to pull it.

**Performance character.** L2 hit latency is around 200 cycles; aggregate L2 bandwidth is several times DRAM bandwidth (third-party H100 microbenchmarks report ~12–14 TB/s for near-partition reads versus 3.35 TB/s HBM — treat the exact figure as a measurement, not a spec). Two consequences follow. First, an L2-filtered kernel is not merely "somewhat faster": it changes which resource saturates. Second, on multi-partition and multi-die parts, the *address-dependent* spread of near/far latency and bandwidth becomes a real effect — one that Blackwell's Tensor Memory was explicitly designed to sidestep for tensor operands (Section 2.3).

### 1.3 HBM: the bottom rung, built from channels (GPU-4.6)

**The problem.** An accelerator needs terabytes per second of memory bandwidth. A DDR5 DIMM offers 64 bits of interface at high pin rates — but you cannot hang thousands of DDR pins off one GPU package, and narrow-fast interfaces burn disproportionate power per bit moved. HBM's answer is the opposite corner of the design space: **wide and moderate**. Thousands of slow, short, low-voltage wires, packed by stacking the DRAM itself.

**Anatomy of a stack.** An HBM stack is 8–12 DRAM dies (the "8-Hi"/"12-Hi" stacking you see on spec sheets) bonded vertically with through-silicon vias (TSVs), sitting on a logic **base die**, and placed *beside* the GPU die on a silicon interposer — the 2.5D packaging that Module 9 will treat properly. Capacity is pure arithmetic: stack capacity = number of dies × die density; a GPU's capacity = stacks × stack capacity. H100 SXM: 5 stacks × 16 GB = 80 GB. B200: 8 stacks × 24 GB (8-Hi) = 192 GB. Blackwell Ultra B300 and MI350X: 8 stacks × 36 GB (12-Hi) = 288 GB each. Twelve-high stacking is how both vendors reached 288 GB without a new memory standard.

**The channel structure — the part that explains everything else.** JEDEC's HBM standard (JESD235D for the HBM2 generation; the structure carries forward) divides each stack's interface into **8 independent channels of 128 bits each — 1024 bits per stack**. A channel is not a marketing subdivision: it has its own command/address pins, its own banks, its own timings, and can be clocked independently. In pseudo-channel (PC) mode each channel splits again into **two 64-bit pseudo-channels** sharing a command bus but decoding and executing independently, with burst length 4. Do the arithmetic on one pseudo-channel access: 64 bits × 4 = **256 bits = 32 bytes fetched per pseudo-channel access**. Hold that number. The 32-byte *sector* of Sections 1.1 and 1.4 is not an arbitrary hardware choice — it is one HBM pseudo-channel burst. The GPU's memory system, its cache granularity, and its coalescing rules are all shaped to match the quantum of the DRAM underneath. A stack's 16 pseudo-channels × 32 B = 512 B can be in flight simultaneously — that bank- and channel-level parallelism is how HBM hides DRAM latency from a machine with tens of thousands of threads in flight (the same "many independent banks" logic as GPU-4.1's hierarchy argument, implemented inside the memory device).

**Deriving bandwidth.** The formula is width × rate:

> **Peak BW = (total data pins) × (transfer rate per pin) ÷ 8**

- H100: 5 stacks × 1024 = 5,120 pins at ~5.2 Gb/s ≈ **3.35 TB/s** ✓
- B200 / B300: 8 stacks → 8,192 pins (Blackwell Ultra's controllers are organized as 16 × 512-bit) at ~8 Gb/s HBM3e ≈ **8 TB/s** ✓
- MI350X: 8 × 1024 = 8,192 pins at 8 Gb/s ≈ **8 TB/s** ✓
- HBM4E (sampling now, Section 2.1): one stack = 2,048 pins at 14–16 Gb/s ≈ **3.6–4.0 TB/s per stack** ✓

Note what the formula exposes: NVIDIA and AMD reached 8 TB/s identically — by *width* (8 stacks) and standard-issue HBM3e pin rates — not by exotic speed. And when HBM4 doubles the per-stack interface to 2048 bits, per-stack bandwidth doubles at constant pin rate; that is the lever Rubin and MI455X pull.

**Why not just clock pins faster?** HBM's interface runs at 1.2 V, unterminated, over a few millimeters of interposer trace — a low-energy, short-reach link. Multiplying pin rate instead of width would demand terminated, equalized links and burn energy per bit that the wide design avoids. Wide-and-short is the whole trick; it is also why HBM cannot be socketed and lives on the package (with consequences Section 2.2 explores).

**What HBM is not: fast.** Per-access latency is still DRAM — row activate, column access, refresh — on the order of hundreds of cycles. HBM buys *bandwidth and concurrency*, not latency; that is GPU-4.10's coming lesson (latency-bound versus bandwidth-bound), and it is why everything above the DRAM exists.

### 1.4 Memory transactions and coalescing (GPU-4.7)

**The problem.** One warp load instruction names 32 addresses chosen by *software*. The memory system moves data in fixed 32-byte sectors. Coalescing is the hardware's mapping between the two — and the software's responsibility to make that mapping cheap.

**The mechanism.** When a warp issues a global load, the L1 (in its coalescing-buffer role, Section 1.1) examines all 32 per-thread addresses, determines which distinct 32-byte sectors they touch, and issues one request per sector. A warp's instruction therefore becomes **1 to 32 sector transactions**. Nsight Compute counts both sides of this: `l1tex__t_requests_pipe_lsu_mem_global_op_ld.sum` (warp instructions' requests) and `l1tex__t_sectors_pipe_lsu_mem_global_op_ld.sum` (sectors actually moved). The **sectors-per-request ratio** is the single most diagnostic coalescing metric.

**The canonical worked example** (numbers from NVIDIA's developer blog, measured on a CC 8.9 device). Thirty-two threads each load one `float` (4 B) from consecutive addresses: the warp touches a contiguous 128-byte range = 4 sectors. Four 32-byte transactions, 32 useful words: **100% efficiency**. Now the same warp with stride 32 (each thread `i` reads element `32·i`): every thread lands in a different sector — 32 sectors = 1,024 bytes moved for 128 bytes used, **8× waste**, with the profiler reporting "only 4.0 of the 32 bytes transmitted per sector are utilized," an estimated 83% speedup available from fixing it. The general cases:

| Warp access pattern (32-bit elements) | Sectors touched | Bytes moved / used | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consecutive, aligned | 4 | 128 / 128 | 100% |
| Consecutive, misaligned by 8 B | 5 | 160 / 128 | 80% |
| Stride 2 | 8 | 256 / 128 | 50% |
| Stride 32 | 32 | 1024 / 128 | 12.5% |
| All threads read same word | 1 | 32 / 4 | broadcast — cheap in bandwidth, wasted in lanes |

Two structural rules fall out. **Alignment**: a range that straddles a 32-byte boundary costs an extra sector; structures padded to sector/line boundaries avoid this. **Thread-to-address mapping**: the fastest-varying thread index (`threadIdx.x`) should map to the fastest-varying address — for a row-major matrix, that means consecutive `threadIdx.x` reading consecutive columns of one row. Column walks (`M[row][tid]` in row-major) are stride-`N` accesses — the 12.5%-class pathology. Vectorized accesses widen the warp's footprint productively: 32 threads × `float4` = 512 contiguous bytes = 16 sectors, still 100% efficient, because per-thread *width* composes with warp *parallelism*, while per-thread *stride* fights it.

**Caching modifiers** let you steer this machinery. Default loads cache in L1 (128-byte granularity); `.cg` loads (`__ldg` cousins aside) bypass L1 and fetch at 32-byte sector granularity — better for scattered access patterns that would otherwise drag four-useful-bytes-per-line through L1. Stores, as Section 1.1 established, go to L2 at sector granularity regardless.

**Why the contract has this shape.** Coalescing is not an arbitrary tax; it is the mapping of SIMT parallelism onto DRAM parallelism. A warp's 32 lanes → 32 banks of shared memory (Section 1.5) → up to 32 bytes of one sector → one pseudo-channel burst (Section 1.3). When your addressing is coalesced, the memory system's channel-level parallelism and the SM's lane-level parallelism line up, and the pipeline runs at structural speed. When it is not, you pay for DRAM concurrency you never use.

**Fixing bad patterns** is a small toolbox: reorder dimensions or transpose the layout; stage through shared memory (GPU-4.3) to make *both* the global read and the global write coalesced even when the algorithm needs a transposed view (the tiled-transpose pattern, which Section 1.5 completes); pad structures to alignment; convert array-of-structures to structure-of-arrays when lanes need independent fields (GPU-4.9 will generalize this as a locality question). Nsight Compute's memory tables make every one of these visible as sectors/request before and after.

### 1.5 Shared-memory bank conflicts: the on-chip contract (GPU-4.8)

**The problem.** Shared memory must feed a whole warp per issued instruction — up to 32 distinct words per cycle — out of a small SRAM. A single monolithic memory could not deliver 32 arbitrary words in one cycle, so the SRAM is built as a **banked** structure, and software addressing must respect the banks.

**The mechanism.** Shared memory is **32 banks of 4 bytes each** (every CUDA compute capability since 2.0; the CUDA Programming Guide states the count flatly). Successive 32-bit words map to successive banks, wrapping:

```
word:  0    1    2   ...  31  | 32   33  ...  63  | 64  ...
bank:  0    1    2   ...  31  |  0    1  ...  31  |  0  ...
       └────── 32-word row ───┘└──── next row ────┘
```

Formally, for a word index `w`: bank = `w mod 32`. If a warp's instruction requests at most one word per bank, the access completes in **one** transaction — 32 banks working in parallel, 128 bytes per cycle per SM. If two or more threads request *different words in the same bank*, the hardware serializes: a **k-way conflict** costs k transactions (issue "replays"), linearly.

The exceptions that are *not* conflicts: multiple threads reading the **same word** in the same bank — the word is broadcast to all requesters (one transaction). The same 32-bit word accessed at different byte offsets — also broadcast. Stores to the same word — one (undefined) thread wins. And since Volta, multiple *distinct* words can be multicast to subsets of lanes in a single transaction. 64-bit accesses are handled as two 32-bit bank-parallel requests.

**The conflict arithmetic.** For the canonical pattern `smem[b + s·tid]` (word stride `s`), bank(tid) = `(b + s·tid) mod 32`. The analysis is pure number theory:

| Stride s | Banks touched | Conflict degree | Transactions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (or any odd s) | all 32, bijectively | none | 1 |
| 2 | 16 banks, 2 lanes each | 2-way | 2 |
| 4 | 8 banks, 4 lanes each | 4-way | 4 |
| 32 | bank 0 only | **32-way** | **32** |

An odd stride permutes banks — `gcd(s, 32) = 1` guarantees conflict-free access; even strides conflict in proportion to their shared factors with 32. The worst case, stride 32, is not exotic: it is exactly what a **column access into a 32-wide row-major matrix** produces.

**The classic case and the classic fix.** A 32×32 `float` tile in shared memory, threads reading column `M[tid][0]`: thread tid's word address is `32·tid + 0`, so every thread hits bank 0 — a 32-way conflict, a 32× slowdown on that instruction, invisible in the source code. Fix: **pad the pitch** — store the tile as 32 rows of *33* words, so `M[tid][0]` sits at word `33·tid`, bank `tid mod 32` — conflict-free. The cost is 4% more shared memory (33/1024 words per tile) and a small loss of coalescing elegance on the *global* load that filled the tile — a trade you evaluate with the profiler, not by folklore. This completes the tiled transpose sketched in Section 1.4: global reads coalesced along rows, global writes coalesced along the other axis, shared memory in the middle with a padded pitch so neither side's shared-memory access conflicts. Both contracts honored simultaneously.

**A second classic: parallel reduction.** The interleaved reduction (stride halving; thread t reads `smem[2t]` and `smem[2t+1]`) hits 2-way conflicts at its first steps — both reads land in even banks, two lanes per bank. The sequential-addressing variant (thread t reads `smem[t]` and `smem[t + n/2]`) is conflict-free at every step, one of the standard findings of the reduction-optimization literature. The general lesson: shared-memory access patterns must be designed *with the modulo arithmetic in the loop*, especially for halo exchanges, stencil sweeps, FFT digit-reversal permutations, and histogram/scatter patterns.

**Scope and portability.** Conflicts are strictly *within one warp's instruction* — different warps, or the same warp at different instructions, never conflict with each other. The same banked design and the same rule govern AMD's LDS (per the CDNA/RDNA ISA model, a wavefront's accesses are serviced bank-parallel and conflicts serialize), with wave64 instructions processed as two 32-lane phases. And the resource keeps growing — MI350's CUs carry **160 KB of LDS each, versus 64 KB on MI300X** (AMD ROCm documentation), a 2.5× jump that makes conflict-aware layout *more* valuable, not less. Detection is direct: Nsight Compute's shared-memory tables report bank conflicts (the `l1tex__data_bank_conflicts_pipe_lsu_mem_shared_op_*` counters) and shared replays.

**The unifying view.** Today's five topics are one idea at five scales. The hierarchy is a stack of **fixed-width parallel structures** — 32 lanes per warp, 32 banks per SM, 32-byte sectors between cache levels, 1024/2048-bit stacks below — and every level delivers full performance only to addressing patterns shaped to its width. Coalescing is the contract at the SM↔DRAM boundary; bank conflict avoidance is the contract inside the SM; the L1/L2/HBM levels are the stages that honor both while filtering traffic downward. Next GPU volume we turn to the *patterns* themselves (GPU-4.9 locality and access patterns, GPU-4.10 latency versus bandwidth), then atomics (GPU-4.11) and formal bandwidth derivation (GPU-4.12) — but the contract metaphor will carry all of them.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 HBM4E enters sampling — 4 TB/s per stack, and the math is today's lesson

**What happened.** Samsung announced the industry's first 12-layer HBM4E sample shipments on May 29, 2026, with SK hynix following on June 17, 2026. Samsung's part: 48 GB per stack (12-Hi), a stable pin speed of 14 Gb/s scalable to 16, "up to 3.6 TB/s" per-stack bandwidth, ~30% more capacity than the previous generation, and ~20% better performance per watt. SK hynix's: 48 GB, 16 Gb/s maximum, >20% power-efficiency improvement, and its Advanced MR-MUF packaging with 17% better thermal resistance than HBM4. Samsung also confirmed at GTC 2026 (March) that its HBM4 — running a consistent 11.7 Gb/s, above the 8 Gb/s JEDEC baseline — is in mass production for NVIDIA's Vera Rubin platform.

**What technically changed.** Run Section 1.3's derivation: HBM4's doubled 2,048-bit interface × 14 Gb/s = 3.58 TB/s per stack; at 16 Gb/s, 4.0 TB/s. Every headline number in both press releases is width × rate, no more and no less. Capacity likewise: 12 layers × 4 GB-class dies = 48 GB — the same stacking arithmetic that took B200 to 192 GB and B300/MI350X to 288 GB.

**Why it matters.** The September 21 volume flagged "HBM4E-class extensions pushing per-stack rates toward 4 TB/s" as the thing to watch; three months later, samples exist from two of the three suppliers, headed for Rubin Ultra-class systems (NVIDIA's Rubin Ultra NVL576, expected 2H 2027, is reported to carry 1 TB of HBM4E per GPU — 4-8 stacks at these densities). For HPC: per-GPU bandwidth in the 20+ TB/s class is now a sampling-cycle away, and the *channel-parallelism* structure that today's sector and coalescing rules are built on scales with it unchanged.

**Watch next.** Customer qualification and volume production timing (neither supplier has committed to a date); Micron's HBM4E entry (its HBM4 ramp is reportedly ahead of schedule, with HBM4E mass production expected next year); and whether 16 Gb/s-class pin rates hold at 12-Hi stack heights, where the 775-micron package-height ceiling begins to bind.

### 2.2 The memory wall gets a formal warning — and Samsung's answer is to move the stack on top of the GPU

**What happened.** At Hot Chips 2026 and SEMICON Taiwan 2026 (September), the memory vendors made the bandwidth wall explicit. Micron presented (as reported by trade press) that compute throughput is scaling roughly 3× every two years while HBM bandwidth grows under 2× — a *widening* gap — with HBM already consuming ~3× the silicon per bit of DDR5 and HBM4's doubled 2,048-I/O interface enlarging the base die further. SK hynix confirmed that hybrid bonding — the tighter-pitch bonding route needed for 20-high stacks — has slipped to HBM5 at the earliest, its mass-reflow MR-MUF process carrying through the Rubin generation, with total stack height capped at 775 µm. And Samsung unveiled a three-phase roadmap for the HBM base die — offload the memory controller onto the base die (freeing GPU silicon for compute), then add telemetry and memory-extension logic, then **zHBM**: bonding DRAM *directly on top of the processor*, eliminating the 2.5D interposer entirely. Samsung's stated targets for zHBM versus HBM4E: up to 8× memory-subsystem performance, 3× performance per watt, and 75–90% lower thermal resistance — explicitly design targets, not measured results, with deployment expected post-2028.

**Why it matters — through today's lens.** This is Section 1.3's design space being renegotiated in public. HBM's wide-and-short principle has always depended on one passive component: the silicon interposer that places the stack *beside* the compute die. The memory wall argument says that as compute scales faster than per-pin bandwidth, the only remaining lever is shortening the wire further — from millimeters of interposer to microns of vertical bond. Every level of today's hierarchy would feel it: zHBM's "memory" would behave less like a distant DRAM and more like an enormous additional on-chip tier, changing what L2 must filter (and making GPU-4.10's latency-vs-bandwidth distinction far murkier). Meanwhile the near-term constraint is brutally physical: taller stacks at fixed height mean thinner dies and worse thermals — which is exactly what MR-MUF improvements (17% thermal resistance, per SK hynix) and, later, hybrid bonding are fighting.

**Watch next.** The first commercialized "custom HBM" products Samsung hinted at for the next SEMICON cycle; whether base-die compute (memory controllers, eventually processing elements) changes where the GPU's memory hierarchy *starts*; and HBM5's stated goal of doubling HBM4E performance.

### 2.3 Two philosophies of the chip-wide cache: Blackwell's 126 MB L2 vs MI350's memory-side Infinity Cache

**What happened.** The two flagship HPC accelerators of the current generation answer "where does the chip-wide cache live?" oppositely, and both answers are now well documented. NVIDIA's Blackwell **concentrates** L2 on the GPU die: 126 MB on GB200 per the Blackwell Tuning Guide, with the B200 microbenchmark study (arXiv:2512.02189) reporting four L2 partitions — double Hopper's two — spread across the two dies that NV-HBI unifies. AMD's MI350 **splits** it: 4 MB of L2 per XCD (32 MB across the eight compute chiplets) plus a 256 MB **Infinity Cache** — a memory-side cache of 32 MB per HBM stack, placed at the memory controllers in the I/O die, filtering traffic before it ever reaches the HBM3E.

**What technically changed.** NVIDIA's concentration buys a short coherence path — all SMs meet on-die — at the price of near/far asymmetry (cross-partition and cross-die L2 access is slower; the same study found Tensor Memory's dedicated arbitration "bypasses L2 partitioning contention," and measured L2 hit rates rising 68%→84% as precision shrank the working set). AMD's memory-side placement buys a huge DRAM filter — 256 MB physically adjacent to the memory it protects — at the price of an extra level of indirection between compute and the coherence point, and it scales naturally with stack count rather than die area. AMD also quietly changed the level *below* the cache: 160 KB of LDS per CU on MI350 versus 64 KB on MI300X (ROCm docs), 2.5× more programmer-managed on-chip capacity per compute unit.

**Why it matters.** This is GPU-4.5 as a live architectural fork rather than a fixed recipe. Neither choice is free: the trade is coherence latency and die spend (NVIDIA) versus filter capacity and chiplet scalability (AMD), and it is no accident that the chiplet-heavy vendor chose the memory-side design — a centralized cache is hard to build across chiplets. When Rubin- and MI455X-class disclosures arrive, the first question to ask is where the cache went and how big the partitions are; you now know why the answer matters.

**Watch next.** Whether Blackwell's TMEM pattern (dedicated operand memory bypassing L2) generalizes beyond tensor work; whether AMD's XCD L2 grows in CDNA 5; and how each design behaves on HPCG-class irregular workloads versus dense GEMM — the workloads L2 philosophy is *for*.
