## 0. Concise Summary

On Monday two weeks ago (HPL-5.1–5.4) we built Gaussian elimination *procedurally*: pick a pivot, compute multipliers, subtract scaled pivot rows, repeat until triangular, then back-substitute. Today we rebuild that machinery algebraically, and the payoff is the single most important structural idea in dense linear algebra: **Gaussian elimination and LU factorization are the same computation**.

The route there runs through five topics. First, **elimination matrices** (HPL-5.5): each elimination step, previously described as "subtract \(2\times\) row 1 from row 3," is re-expressed as left-multiplication by a rank-1-perturbed identity \(M_k = I - \ell_k e_k^T\). This makes elimination composable, invertible, and analyzable as matrix algebra. Second, **computational complexity** (HPL-5.6): we count the work exactly, step by step, and obtain \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3 - \tfrac{1}{2}n^2 - \tfrac{1}{6}n\) flops — the number that dominates every HPL efficiency calculation ever published. Third, **data dependencies** (HPL-5.7): within one step the \((n-k)^2\) trailing updates are mutually independent, but a serial dependency chain runs through the pivots; teasing apart what must wait for what is the seed of panel factorization and look-ahead. Fourth, **what LU factorization is** (HPL-6.1): factoring \(A = LU\) as a standalone mathematical object, with existence and uniqueness conditions. Fifth, the punchline (HPL-6.2): the accumulated multipliers of Gaussian elimination *are* \(L\), and the final triangular matrix *is* \(U\) — factorization is not something you do *after* elimination; it is what elimination leaves behind in storage.

The key technical connection: the matrix view converts a row-manipulation recipe into a **factorization** — a reusable, distributable, blockable object. Factor once for \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) flops, then solve any subsequent \(Ax=b\) for \(2n^2\). It also exposes the rank-1 structure of each step, which is exactly what blocked algorithms later batch into rank-\(NB\) (GEMM) updates, and the dependency structure that makes panels the serial bottleneck of HPL at scale.

In developments: the June 2026 TOP500 list — still the standing list until SC26 — has LineShine (Shenzhen) at No. 1 with 2.198 exaflop/s on HPL, and every number on that list is literally the flop count we derive today divided by measured wall-clock time. And two days ago HPCwire reported the public debut of Sugon 8000 ("Dengfeng"), a 100,000-card Chinese supercluster believed to be that country's fourth exascale-class system — with the new TOP500 list due November 17 at SC26 in Chicago.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Elimination matrices (HPL-5.5)

**Why matrix form?** The procedural description of elimination — "replace row \(i\) by row \(i\) minus \(\ell_{ik}\) times row \(k\)" — is fine for hand computation but analytically weak. It gives no algebraic object to compose across steps, no inverse to undo a step, no way to prove what the whole process computes, and no bridge to blocking. The fix is to notice that every row operation in Gaussian elimination is a **linear map**, and every linear map is a matrix.

**The construction.** Recall the state after \(k-1\) steps: the matrix \(A^{(k-1)}\) has zeros below the diagonal in columns \(1,\dots,k-1\), and the active work is the trailing submatrix of rows and columns \(k,\dots,n\). Step \(k\) eliminates column \(k\) below the diagonal. Define the multiplier vector

\[
\ell_k \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ \ell_{k+1,k} \\ \vdots \\ \ell_{n,k} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ell_{ik} = \frac{a^{(k-1)}_{ik}}{a^{(k-1)}_{kk}},
\]

with the first \(k\) entries zero. Let \(e_k\) be the \(k\)-th standard basis vector. Then the entire elimination step is the single matrix product

\[
A^{(k)} = M_k A^{(k-1)}, \qquad M_k = I - \ell_k e_k^T .
\]

Why does this work? The matrix \(\ell_k e_k^T\) is zero everywhere except column \(k\), where it holds the multipliers. So \(M_k = I - \ell_k e_k^T\) is the identity with the multipliers *subtracted into* column \(k\) below the diagonal. Left-multiplying \(A^{(k-1)}\) by \(M_k\): row \(k\) and every row \(i \le k\) pass through untouched (their entries in the subtracted pattern are zero), while row \(i > k\) gets

\[
\text{row}_i(A^{(k)}) = \text{row}_i(A^{(k-1)}) - \ell_{ik}\,\text{row}_k(A^{(k-1)}),
\]

which is precisely the elimination recipe — and entry \((i,k)\) itself becomes \(a^{(k-1)}_{ik} - \ell_{ik} a^{(k-1)}_{kk} = 0\), as required. Matrices of this form are called **elementary elimination matrices** or **Gauss transforms**. Structurally, \(M_k\) is unit lower triangular (ones on the diagonal, zeros above, the multipliers in column \(k\) below).

**A worked example.** Let

\[
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

Step 1: pivot \(a_{11}=2\), multipliers \(\ell_{21}=4/2=2\), \(\ell_{31}=8/2=4\), so \(\ell_1 = (0,2,4)^T\) and

\[
M_1 = I - \ell_1 e_1^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ -2&1&0 \\ -4&0&1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A^{(1)} = M_1 A = \begin{pmatrix} 2&1&1 \\ 0&1&1 \\ 0&3&5 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

Check row 3: \((8,7,9) - 4\cdot(2,1,1) = (0,3,5)\). Step 2: pivot \(a^{(1)}_{22}=1\), multiplier \(\ell_{32}=3/1=3\), \(\ell_2=(0,0,3)^T\),

\[
M_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 0&-3&1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A^{(2)} = M_2 A^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 2&1&1 \\ 0&1&1 \\ 0&0&2 \end{pmatrix} = U.
\]

Two steps suffice for \(n=3\) (there is nothing below the last diagonal to eliminate), and the result is the upper triangular \(U\).

**Inverses, for free.** The Gauss transforms invert in closed form:

\[
M_k^{-1} = I + \ell_k e_k^T .
\]

The proof is two lines and worth internalizing. Multiply:

\[
(I - \ell_k e_k^T)(I + \ell_k e_k^T) = I - \ell_k \underbrace{(e_k^T \ell_k)}_{=\,0} e_k^T = I,
\]

where \(e_k^T \ell_k = 0\) *because the first \(k\) entries of \(\ell_k\) are zero*. That zero is not a coincidence — it is the structural fact that step \(k\)'s multipliers live strictly below step \(k\)'s pivot row, and it will do even more work for us in Section 1.5.

**Composing the steps.** Since \(U = M_{n-1} \cdots M_2 M_1 A\), we can solve for \(A\) itself:

\[
A = M_1^{-1} M_2^{-1} \cdots M_{n-1}^{-1} \, U .
\]

This is already a factorization of \(A\) into (product of unit lower triangulars) \(\times\) (upper triangular). The product of unit lower triangular matrices is unit lower triangular, so the left factor deserves a name — call it \(L\) — and \(A = LU\). We are two sections away from identifying \(L\) explicitly, but notice what has already happened: the *matrix* view turned a chain of row operations into a statement about factoring the original matrix. Every property of elimination now has an algebraic counterpart: for instance, each \(M_k\) has determinant 1 (unit triangular), so \(\det A = \det U = \prod_i u_{ii}\). On our example: \(\det U = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4\), and directly, \(\det A = 2(27{-}21) - 1(36{-}24) + 1(28{-}24) = 12 - 12 + 4 = 4\). ✓

**Why HPC cares.** Two payoffs come later in the curriculum. First, \(M_k A\) is a **rank-1 update** of \(A\): \(A - \ell_k (e_k^T A)\) subtracts an outer product. Rank-1 updates are memory-bound Level-2 work (one flop per word moved), and dense codes do not execute them one at a time — they batch \(NB\) elimination matrices into a single rank-\(NB\) trailing update, which is a GEMM (HPL-8.x). The elimination-matrix formalism is what makes that batching expressible: \(M_{k+NB-1} \cdots M_k\) collapses into one block operator. Second, the algebraic form is what *distributes*: "apply \(M_k\) to the trailing matrix" partitions cleanly across a process grid (HPL-10.x, 11.x), whereas "row 3 minus 2 times row 1" does not.

### 1.2 Computational complexity (HPL-5.6)

**Counting the work exactly.** How much arithmetic does the full elimination cost? Work step by step. At step \(k\) (with \(m = n-k\) rows/cols still active):

- **Multipliers:** computing \(\ell_{ik} = a_{ik}/a_{kk}\) for \(i = k+1,\dots,n\) costs \(m\) divisions.
- **Updates:** each trailing entry is updated as \(a_{ij} \leftarrow a_{ij} - \ell_{ik} a_{kj}\) — one multiply, one subtract — for \(m^2\) entries: \(2m^2\) flops.

Total, using the standard convention (as in Higham's survey) that one flop is one floating-point addition, subtraction, multiplication, or division:

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \Big[ (n-k) + 2(n-k)^2 \Big]
= 2\sum_{m=1}^{n-1} m^2 + \sum_{m=1}^{n-1} m
= \tfrac{2}{3}n^3 - \tfrac{1}{2}n^2 - \tfrac{1}{6}n \;\text{flops}.
\]

The exact count matters less than its shape, but it is worth seeing how fast the asymptotic form takes over:

| \(n\) | exact flops | \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) | error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 615 | 666.7 | −7.8% |
| 100 | 661,650 | 666,667 | −0.75% |
| 1,000 | 666,166,500 | 666,666,667 | −0.075% |

By \(n=100\) the leading term is within 1%; by \(n=1000\) within a thousandth. Nobody carries the lower-order terms in practice: **dense LU costs \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) flops** is the working number, and it is the numerator of every Rmax figure on the TOP500 list.

**Why exactly one-third of a GEMM?** Multiplying two \(n \times n\) matrices costs \(2n^3\) flops; factoring one costs \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\). There is a clean geometric reason. Index GEMM's work by the triple \((i,j,k)\) — output entry, accumulation step — and you fill an \(n \times n \times n\) cube of multiply-accumulate cells. Elimination's update work lives in the index space \((k, i, j)\) with the constraint that both \(i > k\) and \(j > k\): step \(k\) only touches the trailing \((n-k) \times (n-k)\) square. As \(k\) sweeps from 1 to \(n\), those shrinking squares sweep out a **pyramid inscribed in the cube** — square cross-sections tapering to a point — and a pyramid has one-third the volume of its bounding cube. Same 2 flops per cell, one-third the cells: \(2n^3/3\). When you profile HPL and see the GEMM phase dominate, remember it is executing the pyramid, not the cube — an efficiency subtlety we will meet again as panel overhead (HPL-8.6, HPL-9.10).

**Work versus storage.** The factorization performs \(O(n^3)\) arithmetic on \(O(n^2)\) storage. Each matrix entry is read and rewritten roughly \(n\) times across the whole sweep. That ratio — arithmetic per byte *amortized over the whole algorithm* — grows linearly with \(n\), which is precisely why dense LU is the canonical compute-bound benchmark and why larger problems run at *higher* efficiency, up to memory and runtime limits (the practical N-choosing problem is HPL-13.1). Contrast this with the per-step view: a single rank-1 update moves \((n-k)^2\) words to do \(2(n-k)^2\) flops — an arithmetic intensity near one flop per word, hopelessly memory-bound *if executed in isolation*. The intensity is an emergent property of the whole sweep, not of any one step. This tension — memory-bound steps that are collectively compute-bound — is the fundamental argument for cache blocking (HPL-2.7, 8.1): keep the trailing matrix resident in fast memory across many steps so the hardware, not the DRAM, sees the reuse.

**Concrete scale.** Put numbers on it. \(N = 10^7\) (a realistic order of magnitude for a TOP500-class run): the matrix occupies \(N^2 \times 8\) bytes = 800 TB — which is why the problem must be distributed across a machine's entire aggregate memory — and the factorization costs \(\tfrac{2}{3} \times 10^{21} \approx 6.7 \times 10^{20}\) flops. Sustained perfectly at LineShine's measured 2.198 exaflop/s, that is about 300 seconds of full-machine execution; real submissions add margins for startup, residuals, and the tail of the run. The lesson stands: at exascale, "the benchmark" is minutes of the largest synchronized dense computation on Earth, and its size is set by the \(O(n^2)\)-memory-versus-\(O(n^3)\)-work scaling derived here.

**The solve is noise.** Forward and backward substitution (Section 1.4) cost \(2n^2 - n\) flops together. At \(n = 10^7\), the factorization-to-solve flop ratio is about \(10^7 : 3\): the triangular solves are a rounding error on the total. This is why HPL times the *system solve* but is, in performance terms, a pure factorization benchmark — and why reusing a factorization across right-hand sides (next two sections) is so valuable.

### 1.3 Data dependencies (HPL-5.7)

**The question.** Complexity tells us how much work exists; dependencies tell us how much of it can run *at the same time*. With 13.7 million cores (LineShine), the difference between "there are \(6.7 \times 10^{20}\) flops of work" and "here is the order they can execute in" is the difference between a benchmark and a slide show.

**Anatomy of one step.** Fix step \(k\). Three distinct activities:

1. **Pivot identification.** Finding \(a^{(k-1)}_{kk}\) — trivial without pivoting, a max-reduction across a column with pivoting (HPL-7.x).
2. **Multiplier computation.** The \(n-k\) divisions \(\ell_{ik} = a_{ik}/a_{kk}\). Each is independent of every other; all need the pivot. Embarrassingly parallel, but tiny.
3. **Trailing update.** The \((n-k)^2\) updates \(a_{ij} \leftarrow a_{ij} - \ell_{ik} a_{kj}\). Every one is independent of every other: each reads one multiplier, one pivot-row entry, and its own accumulator. This is the \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) bulk — and it is a parallel free-for-all, limited only by memory bandwidth and the need to *broadcast* row \(k\) and the multipliers to wherever the trailing matrix lives.

So within a step, parallelism is abundant. The interesting structure is *between* steps.

**The serial spine.** Entry \((k+1,k+1)\) — the next pivot — is not final until step \(k\)'s update of that single entry completes. The next multiplier column \(\ell_{k+1}\) is not final until step \(k\)'s update of column \(k+1\) completes. Chaining this observation across all \(k\): elimination has a **critical path** that runs pivot → update-of-next-pivot's-row-and-column → next pivot → …, and everything else — the vast bulk of the trailing updates — has *slack*. A tiny DAG makes this concrete for three columns:

```
   eliminate       eliminate        eliminate
   column 1        column 2         column 3
      │               │                │
      ▼               ▼                ▼
   [pivot 1] ──▶ [pivot 2] ──────▶ [pivot 3]
      │               │
      │ update of     │ update of
      │ row 2 & col 2 │ row 3 & col 3          ← on the critical path
      │               │
      └─── update of rows/cols ≥ 3 ───┘         ← slack: no pivot waits for it
```

The next panel needs only **row \(k+1\) and column \(k+1\)** of the trailing matrix to be current. The remaining \((n-k-1)^2\) entries can still be in flight. On our \(3\times3\) example: after step 1, step 2 requires \(a_{22}, a_{32}\) (column 2) and \(a_{23}\) (row 2) to be final — entries updated by exactly one operation each in step 1 — while \(a_{33}\) is needed only *at the moment* step 2 applies its update. Exploiting exactly this slack is **look-ahead** (HPL-8.9, 12.9): start factoring column \(k+1\) as soon as its row/column arrive, while the rest of the rank-1 update for step \(k\) is still executing. At \(n = 10^7\), the difference between "wait for the whole trailing update" and "wait for one row and one column" is the difference between the panel pipeline flowing and the machine starving (HPL-20.4–20.6).

**What this structure costs without blocking.** In the unblocked algorithm, the critical path executes \(n\) sequential steps, each a small memory-bound rank-1 update plus a broadcast. Synchronization per step, \(n\) times: at scale this is where whole seconds go, which is why the *serial spine* — not the flop count — sets the achievable parallel efficiency of naive GE. Blocking (HPL-8.x) attacks this two ways at once: it batches \(NB\) rank-1 updates into one GEMM (fewer, fatter, more parallel operations) and makes the spine \(n/NB\) panels long instead of \(n\) columns long. The dependency analysis above is the *reason* those later topics exist; the DAG is the same, just viewed at block granularity.

**One more dependency class: the solve.** Once \(U\) exists, back substitution (HPL-5.4) has its own dependency story — \(x_i\) depends on all \(x_j\), \(j > i\) — a chain of length \(n\), but with only \(O(n^2)\) total work it never dominates. Note the contrast with the factorization: within-step parallelism is *rectangular* (an entire submatrix updates at once), while solve parallelism is *triangular* (row \(i\) can only use \(x_j\) for \(j>i\)). This shape difference is why the two phases parallelize differently on a process grid — a thread picked up again in HPL-11.x.

### 1.4 What LU factorization is (HPL-6.1)

**The definition, stated on its own terms.** An LU factorization of \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is a decomposition

\[
A = LU,
\]

where \(L\) is **unit lower triangular** (ones on the diagonal, zeros above) and \(U\) is upper triangular. The "unit" convention is a normalization choice, not a mathematical necessity — but without it the factorization is never unique: for any nonsingular diagonal \(D\), \(A = (LD)(D^{-1}U)\) is another valid split, so the diagonal scale has to be assigned to one side. Fixing \(L\)'s diagonal at 1 hands all of it to \(U\), and with that convention the factorization, when it exists, is *unique*. Higham's survey states the precise condition: an LU factorization with unit lower triangular \(L\) exists and is unique if and only if the leading principal submatrices \(A_k = A(1{:}k,1{:}k)\) are nonsingular for \(k = 1, \dots, n-1\).

**When it fails to exist.** The condition is real, not pedantic. The innocent-looking matrix

\[
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\]

is nonsingular (its determinant is \(-1\)) but has \(A_1 = 0\) — singular — so no \(A = LU\) exists: Gaussian elimination dies on the very first pivot, before any arithmetic happens. The fix is to reorder rows first, \(PA = LU\) with \(P\) a permutation matrix, which is Gaussian elimination *with pivoting* — the entire subject of the next module (HPL-7.x). For today we assume the pivots arrive nonzero; the algebra does not care where the matrix came from.

**Why factor rather than eliminate?** This is the conceptual pivot of the whole module, and it is worth stating as an engineering argument. Elimination-as-recipe solves *one* system: append \(b\), sweep, back-substitute, discard everything but \(x\). Factorization splits the work into a **reusable object** plus a cheap application:

\[
\underbrace{A = LU}_{\text{factor once: } \tfrac{2}{3}n^3}
\qquad\text{then, for each } b:\qquad
\underbrace{Ly = b}_{\text{forward: } n^2}
\;,\;
\underbrace{Ux = y}_{\text{backward: } n^2}.
\]

Solving the pair of triangular systems costs \(2n^2 - n\) flops (forward substitution with a unit diagonal costs \(n^2 - n\): row \(i\) does \(i-1\) multiply-subtracts; backward substitution costs \(n^2\): row \(i\) does \(n-i\) multiply-subtracts plus a division). So the first right-hand side costs \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3 + 2n^2\), and every subsequent one costs \(2n^2\) — three orders of magnitude cheaper at \(n = 10^3\), five at \(n = 10^5\). Any workload with multiple right-hand sides, repeated solves against a fixed operator (implicit time-stepping, Newton iterations on the same Jacobian, parameter sweeps) is *only* viable because the factorization is saved, not consumed.

The factorization view also unlocks things elimination-as-recipe cannot express cleanly: \(\det A = \prod_i u_{ii}\) (from Section 1.1); transposed systems \(A^T y = c\) solved as \(U^T z = c\), \(L^T y = z\) using the *same* factors; and the residual-based correctness checks that HPL itself runs after timing the solve (HPL-12.10). In production software the split is literal: LAPACK exposes `dgetrf` (factor) and `dgetrs` (apply factors) as separate calls, and ScaLAPACK and HPL mirror that separation in distributed form. The interface *is* the factor-and-apply pattern.

**Storage: nothing extra.** A preview of HPL-6.4: \(L\)'s strict lower triangle, \(U\)'s upper triangle including its diagonal, and \(A\) itself all occupy the same \(n^2\) array, and the union of \(L\setminus I\) and \(U\) exactly tiles the matrix. The factorization is created *in place* — no extra \(O(n^2)\) buffer is required. (In exact arithmetic the multipliers land precisely in the holes that elimination zeros out; why that is not a miracle is the subject of the next section.)

### 1.5 Relationship between Gaussian elimination and LU (HPL-6.2)

**The theorem hiding in the algorithm.** From Section 1.1 we have the chain

\[
U = M_{n-1} \cdots M_2 M_1 A
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A = M_1^{-1} M_2^{-1} \cdots M_{n-1}^{-1}\, U,
\]

so \(A = LU\) with \(L = M_1^{-1} M_2^{-1} \cdots M_{n-1}^{-1}\), a product of unit lower triangular matrices — hence unit lower triangular. The remaining question is what \(L\) actually *is*, and the answer is the single most quotable fact in dense linear algebra: **\(L\) is the multipliers, and nothing but the multipliers.**

\[
\boxed{\,L \;=\; I + \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \ell_k e_k^T\,}
\]

Each Gauss-transform inverse \(M_k^{-1} = I + \ell_k e_k^T\) contributes its multiplier vector into column \(k\) below the diagonal, and — critically — **nothing else**. Why don't the \(n-1\) matrix products interfere with each other? Expand any cross term that arises when multiplying out:

\[
(I + \ell_i e_i^T)(I + \ell_j e_j^T) = I + \ell_i e_i^T + \ell_j e_j^T + \ell_i \underbrace{(e_i^T \ell_j)}_{=\,0} e_j^T \quad \text{for } i < j.
\]

The ghost term dies because \(e_i^T \ell_j = 0\) whenever \(i < j\): multiplier vector \(j\) has zeros in its first \(j\) positions, and \(i < j\) means position \(i\) is one of them. The multipliers of later steps live strictly below the rows that earlier steps' inverses touch. Every step writes into a region of \(L\) that no other step writes to — the columns fill independently, like contractors who never share a wall. That is the entire proof: the product of inverses telescopes into the sum of multiplier columns.

**On the running example.** We computed \(\ell_1 = (0,2,4)^T\), \(\ell_2 = (0,0,3)^T\), \(U = \begin{pmatrix} 2&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&2 \end{pmatrix}\). Then

\[
L = I + \ell_1 e_1^T + \ell_2 e_2^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 2&1&0 \\ 4&3&1 \end{pmatrix},
\]

and multiplying back: row 2 of \(LU\) is \(2\cdot(2,1,1) + (0,1,1) = (4,3,3)\) ✓, row 3 is \(4\cdot(2,1,1) + 3\cdot(0,1,1) + (0,0,2) = (8,7,9)\) ✓. \(LU = A\) exactly. Note also *where* those numbers came from: nothing was computed twice. The 2, 4, 3 in \(L\) are the multipliers recorded during elimination; \(U\) is the matrix elimination ended with. Gaussian elimination does not merely *produce* an \(LU\)-solvable system — it computes the factorization as a byproduct, storing \(L\) in the very subdiagonal cells it zeros out.

**How to read \(L\).** Row \(i\) of the identity \(A = LU\) says: original row \(i\) equals \(\ell_{i1}\times\)row \(1(U)\) \(+\;\ell_{i2}\times\)row \(2(U)\) \(+\cdots+\) row \(i(U)\). So \(L\) is the *recipe for rebuilding each original row out of final rows*, and its entries measure how much of earlier pivot rows got mixed into each row during elimination. This reading makes the growth-factor stability analysis of HPL-7.5 tangible: if the multipliers are huge, the final rows must be huge canceling combinations of the originals — which is exactly the numerical danger that pivoting exists to prevent.

**Three ways to solve \(Ax=b\), and why nobody picks two of them.**

| approach | first solve | each later \(b\) | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eliminate augmented \([A\,|\,b]\) | \(\approx \tfrac{2}{3}n^3 + O(n^2)\) | \(\approx \tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) again | mixes concerns; the \(O(n^3)\) work is *consumed*, not saved |
| **Factor, then solve (\(A=LU\))** | \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3 + 2n^2\) | \(2n^2\) | the standard: LAPACK `dgetrf` + `dgetrs`, ScaLAPACK, HPL |
| Explicit inverse, then \(x = A^{-1}b\) | \(\approx 2n^3 + 2n^2\) | \(2n^2\) | ~3× the factorization cost, and numerically less attractive |

The inverse column deserves its blunt evaluation. Computing \(A^{-1}\) costs about \(2n^3\) flops — three LU factorizations' worth — and Gauss–Jordan-style routes to it, as Higham notes, require more computation *and* carry more numerical instability than Gaussian elimination, without producing anything a solver needs that the factors don't already provide. The explicit inverse is a pedagogical object; the factorization is the engineering one. (When someone writes "invert the matrix" in a performance-critical context, they almost always mean "factor it and do triangular solves.")

**What this means for HPL.** The benchmark's measured quantity — Rmax — is computed by timing a program whose computational core is exactly this algorithm: distributed LU factorization of a randomly generated \(n \times n\) matrix, with the known flop count (dominated by the \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) derived in Section 1.2) divided by the wall-clock time. Today's algebra is not adjacent to the benchmark; it is the benchmark. And the practical HPL story — blocking the rank-1 updates into GEMM, pipelining panels along the critical path of Section 1.3, distributing the factors across a process grid — is precisely the next three modules of this theme (HPL-8 through HPL-11).

### 2.1 LineShine leads the TOP500: the list your \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) formula built

**What happened.** The June 2026 TOP500 (the 67th edition, and still the standing list) opened what TOP500's own release calls a new global exascale era: **LineShine**, built by the Shenzhen Cloud Computing Center for the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, debuted at No. 1 with an HPL result of **2.198 exaflop/s** — the first China-based system to lead the list since Sunway TaihuLight in 2017, and the fifth exascale-class system ever listed. It displaced El Capitan (1.809 exaflop/s; Frontier sits third at 1.353; Europe's JUPITER Booster holds fifth at exactly 1.000). The machine is CPU-only: 13,789,440 cores on the custom LingKun platform, 304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz, the proprietary LingQi interconnect, Kylin OS.

**What it means technically — read the numbers through today's curriculum.** An HPL Rmax is nothing but the fixed operation count of distributed LU — the \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\)-dominated total from Section 1.2, evaluated at the submission's \(N\) — divided by measured wall-clock time. LineShine's Rpeak is 2,735.8 petaflop/s, so its 2,198.4 Rmax is **80.4% sustained HPL efficiency** across a 13.7-million-core CPU machine — strikingly high for FP64 LINPACK at that scale, and a direct endorsement of the machine's balance between per-core FP64 throughput and LingQi's collective-communication capability (panel broadcasts and pivot exchanges are the serial spine from Section 1.3, and they are what usually drags large HPL efficiencies down; El Capitan runs at ~64%, Frontier at ~66%). Two more list facts connect to the road ahead: LineShine also tops the **HPCG** list at just 22 petaflop/s — one percent of its HPL figure — a vivid reminder that irregular, dependency-choked kernels cannot exploit the rectangular, embarrassingly-parallel trailing updates that make dense LU run near peak. And on **HPL-MxP** (mixed precision), LineShine debuted fourth at 7.92 exaflop/s, a 3.6× speedup over its HPL result — modest, as TOP500 notes, "consistent with its CPU-only design," because HPL-MxP's leverage comes from low-precision matrix engines of exactly the kind Theme-1 and the upcoming mixed-precision modules (HPL-14–17) are about.

**Why it matters / what to watch.** The list that SC26 will inherit is led by a system whose claim to fame is executing today's algorithm, at scale, more *efficiently* than any GPU machine on it. The next edition of the list is announced **November 17 at SC26 in Chicago** (the conference runs November 15–20) — watch whether El Capitan-class systems re-tune, and whether any of China's other exascale-class machines (see below) appear.

### 2.2 Sugon 8000 "Dengfeng" debuts: a fourth Chinese exascale-class system, measured by the same yardstick

**What happened.** Two days ago (November 2), HPCwire reported the public debut of **Sugon 8000**, also called "Dengfeng," at the National Supercomputing Center in Zhengzhou (Henan Supercomputing Center). It is a **100,000-card supercluster** on Hygon silicon, linked by Sugon's new **scaleFabric** interconnect — an "InfiniBand-like" 400G/800G RDMA network — with flash-based ParaStor storage that recently took first place in the IO500's full-node and 10-node rankings, and immersion phase-change liquid cooling supporting megawatt-class cabinets. With LineShine, Sunway Oceanlite (Wuxi), and Tianhe-3 (Guangzhou), China is now believed to have at least four exascale-class systems. Whether Sugon 8000 will be submitted to the TOP500 at SC26 on November 17 is unknown.

**What it means technically.** "Exascale-class" is a claim about *this theme's algorithm*: it asserts that a distributed \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) LU factorization on the machine would sustain above \(10^{18}\) flop/s — which is a statement simultaneously about FP64 core throughput (Theme 1), about the interconnect's ability to feed panel broadcasts and pivot exchanges along the serial spine of Section 1.3 at 100,000-card scale (Theme 2), and about the software stack's ability to batch rank-1 updates into GEMM (Theme 4, and HPL-8 ahead). The scaleFabric detail matters in that light: RDMA semantics are exactly what distributed LU's broadcast-and-update pattern wants, and a 400G/800G "InfiniBand-like" fabric is a direct answer to the communication pressure that faster GEMM creates — the bottleneck-migration story of HPL-16.7 and 20.8 in embryo. Sugon also positions the machine across the precision spectrum, "from scientific codes requiring full FP64 … to AI models in INT8 mode" — the same precision-versus-work split that separates HPL from HPL-MxP and that the curriculum formalizes in HPL-14.

**Why it matters / what to watch.** What is *not* disclosed is as instructive as what is: no core count, no memory capacity, no power figure — hence no Rpeak, and no way to guess an HPL efficiency from outside. The only universally comparable, verifiable number in exascale discourse remains a LINPACK submission. November 17 will tell whether Dengfeng's owners choose to convert "exascale-class" from press-release claim into a measured \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) / time figure on the public list.
