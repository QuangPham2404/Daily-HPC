## 0. Concise Summary

Last Thursday's PERF volume ended with the three raw metrics — wall time, throughput, latency — and pointed forward to utilization. Today we take the next five topics, and they turn out to be one lesson in careful bookkeeping: before any performance number means anything, you must answer four questions. *Utilized* — busy doing what, on which resource, measured at which scope? *Efficient* — relative to which reference: theoretical peak, another benchmark, a watt, a core? *Bottlenecked* — limited by which resource or dependency, and would fixing it actually shorten the critical path? And *per what unit of work* — FLOPs, samples, tokens, cell updates, or "accelerators kept fed"?

Utilization (PERF-1.6) is a fraction of available hardware doing work — but NVIDIA's own `utilization.gpu` metric is a pure time-share flag that reads 100% when a single one-thread kernel is resident, so a whole hierarchy (device activity → SM activity → warp occupancy) is needed before "the GPU is 87% utilized" says anything. Efficiency (PERF-1.7) is achieved performance over a reference, and the entire skill is choosing and declaring the denominator: the June 2026 TOP500 lets us compute HPL efficiency for the top systems (LineShine 80.3%, El Capitan 64.1%, Fugaku 82.3%) and see how the *same machine* earns wildly different efficiencies under different references — 1.0% on HPCG versus HPL, 3.6× on HPL-MxP versus HPL. Bottlenecks (PERF-1.8) are the resource or dependency whose improvement — and only whose improvement — shortens total runtime; the symptom (an idle GPU) routinely appears far from the cause (slow storage or a dependency-heavy panel factorization). The unit of work (PERF-2.1) is the countable thing whose completion constitutes progress, and every rate or time is silently "per" that unit; benchmarks legislate it precisely because it is where comparisons live or die. Finally, time-per-operation and operations-per-second (PERF-2.2) are reciprocal views of the same measurement, and each has a natural algebra: rates add across parallel workers, times add along serial chains.

The developments today are a matched set. The June 2026 TOP500 (announced at ISC Hamburg) sees China's CPU-only **LineShine** debut at No. 1 with 2.198 Exaflop/s — 80.3% of its theoretical peak — and its cross-benchmark profile (HPCG 22 PF, HPL-MxP 3.6× HPL) is a machine-sized exercise in reading efficiencies against different denominators. The **Green500** crowns KAIROS, a compact Grace-Hopper system ranked 445th on the TOP500, at 73.28 GFLOP/s/W — proof that the efficiency leaderboard and the capability leaderboard are different axes. And MLCommons' **MLPerf Storage v3.0** (September 1, 2026) makes utilization a *validity gate*: a storage result only counts if simulated accelerators stayed at ≥90% utilization, and the benchmark reports throughput in two units at once — samples/s and MB/s — the unit-of-work lesson written into a production benchmark.

Why this matters: every optimization argument you will ever have — with a vendor, a colleague, or yourself — is an argument about one of these five concepts stated imprecisely. Today makes them precise.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Utilization: busy is not the same as productive (PERF-1.6)

Utilization is the fraction of an available resource that is doing work, over some measurement window:

```text
U(resource) = time (or space) the resource spends busy / total time (or capacity) available
```

It exists as a concept because hardware is bought, not earned. Throughput (PERF-1.4) tells you what you got; utilization tells you what fraction of what you paid for was ever touched. A cluster that averages 60% utilization is a machine of which you bought 40% too much — or, more interestingly, a machine whose software starves it. Utilization is the bridge between the economics of the purchase and the physics of the workload.

The subtlety is that "busy" must be defined per resource, per scope, and per measurement method — and the definitions matter enormously. The canonical example is the one every GPU programmer meets first: `nvidia-smi`'s `utilization.gpu`. NVIDIA's definition is *"the percent of time over the past sample period during which one or more kernels was executing on the GPU."* Read that carefully: it is a **time-share flag**, not a work measurement. One tiny kernel running on one thread of one SM out of an H100's 132 SMs reports 100% utilization. The other 131 SMs can be completely idle and the dashboard says the GPU is fully busy.

NVIDIA's own monitoring stack recognizes this and exposes a hierarchy of progressively stricter utilization metrics, which the DCGM team has described publicly in terms of three levels:

| Metric | Question it answers | What reads 100% |
|---|---|---|
| Device activity (`gr_activity`) | Was *any* kernel running anywhere? | 1 block, 1 thread, 1 SM |
| SM activity (`sm_activity`) | What fraction of SMs had any work? | `numSMs` blocks of 1 thread each |
| Warp occupancy (`sm_occupancy`) | What fraction of each SM's warp slots held warps? | blocks with substantial thread counts |

Each level is a different definition of "the GPU," and a healthy workload separates them only mildly while a sick one separates them violently. Consider two kernels on a 132-SM H100:

```text
Kernel A: large GEMM, all 132 SMs, ~128 warps each
  device activity ≈ 100%   SM activity ≈ 100%   occupancy ≈ high   FP64 efficiency ≈ 70–80% of peak

Kernel B: sequential reduction, 1 block of 256 threads
  device activity ≈ 100%   SM activity ≈ 0.76%  occupancy ≈ ~0.4%  FP64 efficiency ≈ ~0.003% of peak
```

Both read "100%" in `nvidia-smi`. This is not a bug — the metric is answering the question "is anything resident?" honestly — but it means a single utilization number is a **screening signal, not a diagnosis**. Low utilization is highly informative (something is starving the hardware: gaps, dependencies, insufficient work, host-side orchestration). High utilization is weakly informative: the resource may be doing useful work, or it may be spinning.

Two further traps. First, utilization cannot distinguish *useful* busy from *wasteful* busy. An MPI rank in a busy-wait inside `MPI_Wait` shows 100% CPU utilization while contributing zero progress; only an efficiency metric (next section) can see the difference, because efficiency counts *work delivered*, not *time occupied*. Second, utilization is per-resource: at any instant an application presents a vector — SMs 100%, DRAM 22%, NVLink 5%, tensor pipe 40%, copy engines 0% — and each component of that vector is a different story. "The GPU is utilized" is a sentence that hides at least five numbers. (Scope matters too: with MIG partitions, `nvidia-smi` reports per physical GPU while the work runs inside instances; DCGM's per-instance metrics are normalized to the *allocated* slice of SMs, so the same activity reads differently at different scopes.)

The practical discipline: always state utilized *what*, measured *how*, over *which scope* — "SM activity averaged 34% while device activity read 100%" is a diagnosis; "GPU utilization was 100%" is a tautology.

### 1.2 Efficiency: achieved performance divided by a declared reference (PERF-1.7)

Efficiency is a ratio:

```text
η = achieved performance / reference performance
```

It looks trivial; the entire intellectual content is in the denominator. Unlike utilization, which measures occupation, efficiency measures *adequacy* — how close what you got is to what is (in some declared sense) gettable. And because "gettable" can be defined many ways, a machine has not one efficiency but a family of them. The June 2026 TOP500 list (see Section 2.1) provides a full zoo, all computed from the same public Rmax/Rpeak/power fields:

| System | HPL efficiency (Rmax/Rpeak) | Energy efficiency (Rmax/power) | HPCG as % of HPL | HPL-MxP / HPL |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LineShine (CPU-only, China) | 80.3% | 52.07 GF/W | 1.0% | 3.6× |
| El Capitan (MI300A, LLNL) | 64.1% | 60.94 GF/W | 0.96% | 9.2× |
| Fugaku (A64FX, RIKEN) | 82.3% | 14.8 GF/W | 3.6% | — |
| JUPITER Booster (GH200, FZJ) | 81.6% | 63.3 GF/W | not submitted | — |

Four different efficiencies, four different stories about the *same* machines. Note what each denominator is:

- **Rpeak** — a *convention*, not a physical constant. TOP500 computes it from the advertised (base) clock rate and an ideal issue model (2 FLOPs per FP64 FMA lane per cycle); TOP500 itself notes that for turbo-capable parts you should account for turbo clocks when judging efficiency. Change the clock convention and every efficiency number shifts.
- **Watts** — the Green500 reference. LineShine leads the TOP500 but is *mid-pack* on GF/W (52.07) while El Capitan, 18% slower on HPL, is 17% better per watt. Performance and efficiency are genuinely different axes.
- **Another benchmark's result** — HPCG as a fraction of HPL measures architectural balance: Fugaku's 3.6% says its memory system feeds its (relatively modest) FLOPs well; LineShine's 1.0% says a machine built to saturate enormous vector units with dense GEMM has its FLOP capacity largely irrelevant to sparse, bandwidth-bound kernels.
- **The same benchmark at different precision** — HPL-MxP/HPL ratios (El Capitan 9.2×, Aurora 11.5×, LineShine 3.6×) measure how much extra capability the silicon exposes when the work is allowed to run in low precision. LineShine's small ratio is a direct consequence of having no matrix-engine accelerators: there is little low-precision headroom to unlock.

Even the numerator is a convention, which is the deeper lesson. HPL-MxP reports "FLOP/s" while executing most operations in FP16/BF16 — the count of operations is the FP64-equivalent reference workload, the execution is not. The ratio above 1.0 is only meaningful because you know exactly what is being counted and what is being executed. This is why stating both numerator and denominator is not pedantry: it is the difference between a measurement and a slogan.

Why is HPL efficiency ~60–80% and not 100%? Because Rpeak assumes dual-issue FMA at the advertised clock on every core, forever. Real runs pay for: memory traffic (even HPL's O(n²) panel and swap traffic), communication (the row/column broadcasts from Theme 3), panel factorization (dependency-heavy, poorly overlappable at small NB), DVFS pulling clocks down under sustained power draw, and the plain arithmetic of the ⅔n³ − ½n² work formula never perfectly matching idealized GEMM shapes. That CPU-only wide-vector systems (Fugaku 82.3%, LineShine 80.3%) currently beat accelerator-based ones (El Capitan 64.1%, Aurora 51.1%) on this ratio is itself informative: a CPU's FP64 peak is set by pipes a dense, regular benchmark can genuinely keep full, while an accelerator's FP64 peak is set by units that only very well-tuned code (and only some phases of it) can approach.

One more denominator, per-"core", shows the convention problem at its worst: dividing Rmax by TOP500's core counts gives LineShine 159.4 GF/s per core and El Capitan 159.5 — numerically almost identical, and almost meaningless, because a "core" is counted differently by different vendors (a CPU core and a compute unit inside an APU are not comparable units of anything). Efficiency statements inherit every ambiguity of their denominators.

### 1.3 Bottleneck: the constraint that actually sets your runtime (PERF-1.8)

A bottleneck is the resource or dependency whose current rate limits overall performance — equivalently, the one whose improvement shortens total runtime while improvements elsewhere change nothing. That second phrasing is the operational definition, and it is worth internalizing because it converts "bottleneck" from a vibe into a test:

```text
bottleneck ⇔ argmax over candidate resources of (∂T_total / ∂ resource speedup)
```

The simplest possible example is a two-stage pipeline. A GPU computes a training batch in 100 ms; the storage system delivers the next batch in 150 ms. With no overlap, the pipeline period is max(100, 150) = 150 ms, and the GPU is busy only 100/150 ≈ 67% of the time. Now watch the asymmetry that defines bottleneck-hood: cut the compute time in half (50 ms) and the period is *still* 150 ms — total runtime unchanged, expensive optimization wasted. Cut the feed time in half (75 ms) and the period drops to 100 ms. The storage was the bottleneck even though the *symptom* — idle time — showed up on the GPU, the most expensive and most visible component in the rack. Symptom location and cause location are routinely different, a pattern that will return as rank-asymmetry analysis (PERF-18.7): waiting workers are rarely the guilty ones.

Bottlenecks come in two structurally different flavors:

- **Resource bottlenecks** — a physical unit is saturated: DRAM bandwidth, SM issue slots, NVLink, PCIe, a metadata server, a license. The fix is more of the resource, better use of it, or less demand on it.
- **Dependency (synchronization) bottlenecks** — nothing is saturated; the program *waits*: a serial chain of small kernels, a barrier for a straggler, a latency-bound loop where each operation needs the previous one's result. HPL's panel factorization is the classic case from Theme 3: at small NB, the panel (a small, recursion-heavy, latency-dominated factorization) executes frequently relative to the big trailing GEMM updates, and the GPUs idle between panels. No unit is "saturated" — the bottleneck is the dependency structure itself.

Both flavors can hide from naive utilization reading. A memory-bound kernel can show DRAM at 100% utilization while delivering only a few useful bytes per 32-byte transaction — the resource is busy but the *useful* bandwidth is low, so the real constraint is access efficiency, not raw DRAM capacity. Conversely, a host CPU can show 30% package utilization while one core is pegged at 100% issuing kernel launches — that single core is the bottleneck, and 30% is a scope artifact. And once you fix a bottleneck, the constraint migrates: in the pipeline above, after storage is improved to 80 ms, compute (100 ms) becomes the new limiter. Optimization is therefore not a checklist but a loop — measure, identify the current constraint, fix it, re-measure (this is the PERF-1.1 cycle, and bottleneck migration gets its own topic later as PERF-19.5).

The full taxonomy — compute-, memory-, latency-, communication-, and synchronization-bound behavior — is coming in Module 4 (PERF-4.4–4.8), and the formal tool for *finding* the bottleneck along a dependency chain is critical-path analysis (Module 19). Today's takeaway is the discipline: a bottleneck claim is a prediction ("improving X will reduce T_total; improving Y will not"), and predictions are testable. If your bottleneck hypothesis survives the test, act on it; if not, you learned more from the failed test than from any dashboard.

### 1.4 Define the unit of work: what counts as "one" (PERF-2.1)

Every rate and every per-operation time in this field is silently divided by a unit of work: iterations, FLOPs, cells, elements, samples, requests, tokens, timesteps. If the unit is ambiguous, every downstream number is ambiguous. Defining it is the first act of measurement, not an afterthought.

HPL makes the definition explicit and mathematical: the unit of work is the factorization of an n×n dense matrix, and its canonical operation count is ⅔n³ − ½n² FLOPs, so the reported rate is that count divided by the timed region. The definition extends to the problem size itself: TOP500 records not just Rmax but Nmax (the problem size at which the best score was achieved) and N½ (the size at which the machine achieves half of it), because performance is a *function of* problem size, not a number. LineShine's Nmax of 37,647,359 is instructive arithmetically: a dense FP64 matrix of that order occupies 8n² ≈ 11.3 PB, and with HPL's customary target of filling most of system RAM, that implies a machine with on the order of 14 PB of memory. The unit of work determines the memory footprint determines the achievable size determines the score — the definition is load-bearing end to end.

HPCG demonstrates the opposite move: same metric name (FLOP/s), completely different unit of work. Its work is a fixed sparse problem — a discretized 3D Poisson equation solved with a preconditioned conjugate gradient, built from SpMV, Gauss–Seidel smoothing, dot products, and multigrid — and its rules *legislate* the unit: the problem must fill at least a quarter of memory, and official runs must last at least 1800 seconds. The legislators (Dongarra, Heroux, and the HPCG community) wrote those rules precisely so that the reported number means the same thing across machines: that a run can't game the benchmark with a cache-resident problem or a two-second timing window. And yet the number means something *completely different* from HPL's, because the operation mix has ~100× lower arithmetic intensity. LineShine posts 2198 PFlop/s on one unit and 22 PFlop/s on the other — a 100× gap produced not by the hardware but by the definition of work.

The newest example is in today's third development (Section 2.3): MLPerf Storage reports each result in **two units simultaneously** — samples/second *and* MB/s — because the same measured run is consumed by two audiences: ML engineers think in samples, storage engineers think in bytes. Same numerator, different unit, both correct.

Five pitfalls to carry forward:

1. **Optimizing the denominator.** An algorithmic improvement (a better preconditioner halving iterations, a faster factorization reducing FLOPs) *reduces* the work per solution. FLOP/s will drop while the program gets faster. Always pair rate metrics with time-to-solution for a fixed problem.
2. **Mixed-precision accounting.** HPL-MxP counts the FP64-equivalent operation count while executing in low precision. "FLOP" is a convention there, not a physical event; say which you mean.
3. **Useful vs. busy work.** A spin-wait completes zero units of work. Units of work must be *deliverables* (a correct sample, a converged iteration, a timestep accepted), not activity.
4. **Normalization scope.** Per-node, per-rank, per-GPU, per-watt, per-dollar: the same total work divided by different scopes answers different questions, and comparing numbers normalized to different scopes is meaningless (the per-"core" trap from 1.2).
5. **Comparability across sizes.** Doubling the problem per rank (weak scaling) and doubling ranks at fixed problem (strong scaling) change what "one unit" costs; the scaling laws in Module 6 exist precisely to reason about this.

### 1.5 Time per operation versus operations per second: reciprocal views, different algebras (PERF-2.2)

For a fixed unit of work, rate and per-unit time are exact reciprocals: a kernel sustaining 159.4 GFLOP/s per core is spending 6.27 ns per FP64 operation. Same measurement, two views — so why does the choice matter? Because the two views have different, and complementary, algebra.

**Rates add across independent parallel resources.** Eight GPUs each sustaining 45 samples/s deliver 360 samples/s; 45,360 processors each sustaining 60 TFLOP/s deliver 2.7 EFLOP/s. Capacity planning, cost analysis (performance per watt, per dollar), and resource sizing are all naturally rate arithmetic — you multiply and add rates. This is why rates dominate procurement documents, marketing, and dashboards.

**Times add along serial chains.** Total runtime is the sum of per-stage times along the critical path: T = t₁ + t₂ + … . Latency budgets, dependency chains, step-by-step profiling (kernel timelines report durations in μs and ms — a profiler's native unit is time, because timelines are sums), and user-perceived latency are all naturally time arithmetic. This is why profilers and debuggers speak in durations.

The two algebras meet in one warning: **never average rates arithmetically when the measurements cover different durations.** Suppose phase A runs for 10 s at 100 samples/s and phase B runs for 50 s at 20 samples/s. The true aggregate is (100×10 + 20×50) / 60 = 2000/60 ≈ **33.3 samples/s**. The arithmetic mean of the two rates — (100+20)/2 = 60 samples/s — overstates it by nearly 2×, because the slow phase occupied most of the wall time. The correct aggregate is always total work divided by total time: a duration-weighted mean. The arithmetic mean of rates is valid only when every measurement covers the *same duration*; dually, averaging per-item times is safe only when every measurement covers the same *count* of items. Getting this wrong inflates expected throughput whenever phase rates differ, which they always do.

Per-operation times also expose hardware constants in a way rates do not. LineShine's per-core sustained HPL rate of 159.4 GF/s is, inverted, 6.27 ns per FP64 op; its per-core peak (198.4 GF/s) is 5.04 ns per op. The 1.23 ns/op gap is where everything imperfect — memory stalls, panel time, communication, clock droop — lives, and 6.27 ns is ~9.7 cycles at 1.55 GHz, so the core averages roughly one completed FP64 result per ten cycles *per core*, sustained, across 13.8 million cores. Compare a single off-chip memory access, on the order of 100 ns: one DRAM/HBM round trip costs the time of ~16 of those FLOPs. That is why a sparse kernel that misses cache cannot be rescued by FLOPs (the HPCG story again), and why "ns per event" is the natural language for reasoning about latency-bound loops (PERF-1.5's dependency chains, and PERF-4.6 ahead).

The two views also answer to the two constituitions from PERF-1.4/1.5: throughput is what an *owner* buys (rates), latency is what a *user* feels (times). Molecular dynamics wraps the duality into a single metric — "ns of simulated physical time per day of wall time" — a time-per-work figure, because the community's question is "how much physics do I get by Tuesday."

The rule to keep: **choose the view that makes the arithmetic additive for what you are studying.** Aggregating parallel capacity → rates. Reasoning about serial chains, stalls, and latency budgets → times. And whichever you use, carry the unit of work (1.4) and the denominator (1.2) with it, or the number is not portable.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 The June 2026 TOP500: LineShine debuts at No. 1 — and its cross-benchmark profile is an efficiency lesson

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 in Hamburg (June 23, 2026), the 67th TOP500 list crowned a new No. 1: **LineShine**, installed at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, China, built by the Shenzhen Cloud Computing Center on the domestic LingKun platform. It scored 2.198 Exaflop/s on HPL — more than 20% ahead of El Capitan (1.809 EF/s), displacing it after its long run, and becoming the first Chinese system to lead the list since Sunway TaihuLight in 2017 and the first system of any kind to pass 2 EF/s on CPUs alone: 13,789,440 cores in 304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz (exactly 45,360 chips), linked by the proprietary LingQi interconnect, drawing 42.22 MW.

**What technically changed/demonstrated.** Read through today's concepts, the announcement is a five-topic exam. Its HPL efficiency is 2198.4/2735.8 = **80.3% of Rpeak** — exceptional at leadership scale, and achieved with a striking per-core implication: Rpeak/cores/clock = 198.4 GF/s ÷ 1.55 GHz ≈ **128 FP64 FLOPs per cycle per core**, i.e., the equivalent of 64 FP64 FMAs per cycle — enormously wide vector machinery (for contrast, Fugaku's A64FX issues 32 FLOPs/cycle/core via two 512-bit SVE pipes, and sustains 82.3% HPL efficiency). A CPU-only design posting 80% HPL efficiency says: dense, regular, perfectly prefetchable GEMM is exactly the workload wide vector units can keep fed. But the *same machine* tells a completely different story under other definitions of work: **HPCG 22.0 PFlop/s — 1.0% of its HPL score** — because the sparse, bandwidth-bound unit of work (SpMV, smoothers, halo exchange) never touches those vector pipes at width; the bottleneck resource is the memory system, and FLOP capacity is nearly irrelevant. And on **HPL-MxP, 7.92 EF/s — only 3.6× its FP64 score**, versus El Capitan's 9.2×, Aurora's 11.5×, and Frontier's 8.4× (SoftBank's CHIE-4 posted the round's largest gain at 24.4×). The small ratio is a clean architectural signature: without matrix-engine accelerators there is little low-precision headroom, while for accelerator-based systems the mixed-precision benchmark exposes an entirely additional layer of capability that FP64 Rpeak conceals. LineShine also took No. 1 on HPCG outright (22.00 PF vs El Capitan 17.41, Fugaku 16.00) — it is a genuinely balanced large machine by HPCG standards, not merely an HPL monster.

**Why it matters and what to watch.** One machine, one week, four efficiencies (80.3% of peak, 52.07 GF/W, 1.0% of HPL on HPCG, 3.6× on HPL-MxP) — each correct, each answering a different question. For a student of performance engineering the list is a free, fully documented dataset of denominator discipline. Watch: whether November 2026 brings tuned HPL-MxP submissions for LingKun (its 3.6× could improve with software, not silicon), and whether the HPCG/HPL ratios of the exascale cohort move as compilers mature for LX2 (listed as Lclang 1.0.0 with OpenBLAS-local — a young stack).

### 2.2 The Green500: the most efficient machine in the world is ranked 445th

**What happened.** The June 2026 Green500 (ranked on HPL performance per watt) kept an unchanged podium: **KAIROS** at CALMIP/University of Toulouse–CNRS retains No. 1 at **73.28 GFLOP/s/W** (3.046 PFlop/s on HPL), followed by **ROMEO-2025** (70.91 GF/W, 9.863 PF) and the **Levante GPU extension** at DKRZ (69.43 GF/W, 6.747 PF). All three are the same technology — Eviden BullSequana XH3000 with Grace Hopper GH200 superchips and quad-rail NVIDIA InfiniBand NDR200 — and their order is system size: as TOP500's own analysis notes, smaller installations of identical technology consistently beat larger ones on efficiency.

**What it demonstrates.** KAIROS sits at **rank 445 on the TOP500** while being No. 1 on the Green500: the capability axis and the efficiency axis are simply different axes, and this is the cleanest possible demonstration that "fastest" and "best" require a declared reference. The size effect is mechanism, not accident: a small system pays less fixed overhead (interconnect scale, idle-node power, conversion losses), and — crucially — a compact HPL run is less likely to pull clocks down under power capping (DVFS, the Theme-1 mechanism), so the same silicon runs nearer its advertised clock. LineShine at 52.07 GF/W and El Capitan at 60.94 GF/W trail KAIROS precisely because leadership-scale HPL runs are sustained power-draw events; the denominator "watt" is being measured under much harsher conditions at the top of the list. Note also that the efficiency comparison inherits every numerator convention: all these GF/W figures are FP64 HPL work per watt — an HPCG-work-per-watt ranking would reorder the field entirely.

**What to watch.** Whether GH200-class systems can hold the podium as Blackwell-generation (B200/GB200) systems — now entering the list in volume — mature their HPL tuning; first-generation deployments historically underperform their hardware until the software stack catches up.

### 2.3 MLPerf Storage v3.0: utilization as a validity gate, and a benchmark that reports two units of work

**What happened.** On September 1, 2026, MLCommons published **MLPerf Storage v3.0**, extending the benchmark from AI *training* I/O to inference-era storage: KV-cache and vector-database workloads, and S3 object-storage access alongside POSIX. Nineteen organizations submitted, eleven for the first time (including Azure, NVIDIA, Nebius, and ZettaLane).

**What technically changed.** The benchmark's figure of merit is not storage bandwidth but **Accelerator Utilization (AU)**: accelerators are *simulated* (an NVIDIA B200 or AMD MI355 in v3.0) — the data path through storage, network, and client DRAM is fully real, and each simulated accelerator "computes" by sleeping for the measured per-batch compute time of the real device. A result is valid **only if AU stayed at or above a threshold — 90% for the Unet3D workload, 85% for Retina** — and the reported number is the throughput sustained *at that utilization*. This is utilization promoted from a dashboard metric to a *benchmark validity criterion*: the benchmark's definition of a correct measurement is "the accelerators were kept busy," because a storage system that lets GPUs idle is failing at its job no matter how many MB/s it posts. The workload spread encodes bottleneck taxonomy in the benchmark design itself: the most bandwidth-hungry workload requires ≈5.9 GiB/s of read bandwidth per simulated B200 at full utilization, while another averages a modest ≈155 MiB/s per accelerator but ~500 file-opens per second — one stresses raw link bandwidth, the other metadata and small-object latency. Two different bottlenecks, two different storage designs that can win.

**Why it matters and the connection.** This is today's whole lesson industrialized: utilization (PERF-1.6) as the gate, the storage system as the bottleneck whose fix is the only one that matters while GPUs idle (PERF-1.8), the GPU-feeding framing as a capacity unit ("how many accelerators can this system keep busy"), and dual reporting in samples/s and MB/s as a deliberate, simultaneous choice of two units of work for two audiences (PERF-2.1/2.2). The original MLPerf Storage goal, as its working group has described, was "keeping the GPUs running as close to 100% utilization as possible" — our Section 1.1 caveats apply (this AU is a time-share measurement on a *simulated* device), but as a *system-level* utilization target it is exactly right: the question is whether the expensive resource is ever starved. **Watch:** whether the AU-gate pattern spreads to other benchmark suites, and how KV-cache workloads change storage architectures (they read and write at Llama-3.1-70B KV-block granularity — 2.5× the object size of the 8B model's — which favors different caching tiers than training's sequential streams).
