## 0. Concise Summary

Today's volume completes the curriculum's "data movement" module and opens the floating-point module — and the two are more deeply connected than they first appear.

We start with **surface-to-volume intuition** (HPL-2.8): the geometric fact that a *b×b* matrix block has *O(b²)* boundary (data that must cross a boundary) but *O(b³)* interior work (multiplying it with other blocks). The ratio of work to boundary grows linearly with *b*, which is the single mathematical fact behind blocking, BLAS-3, cache tiling, and the block size *NB* you have already tuned in HPL runs.

We then descend from geometry to bits. Real numbers are uncountable; a 64-bit word is one of 2⁶⁴ values. **Floating-point representation** (HPL-3.1) is the engineered compromise: a nonuniform grid of representable numbers that is dense near zero and sparse far away, buying nearly constant *relative* error. The **format structure** (HPL-3.2) — sign, biased exponent, significand with an implicit leading bit — is the machinery that makes that grid cheap to encode and fast to compare. **HPC precisions** (HPL-3.3) form a ladder — FP64, FP32, TF32, BF16, FP16, FP8 — where every rung trades mantissa bits (precision) against exponent bits (dynamic range) and roughly doubles matrix-engine throughput per step down. **Machine epsilon** (HPL-3.4) is the one number that prices each rung: the gap between 1.0 and the next representable value, which bounds the best possible per-operation rounding error.

The connection between the halves: blocking decides *how few times* data moves; precision decides *how wide* each datum is and *how fast* the arithmetic on it runs. Together they define the design space of modern dense linear algebra — and of HPL-MxP, which runs the bulk of its FLOPs at a coarse grid (low precision, fast) and repairs the result on a fine grid (high precision, slow).

In developments: the June 2026 TOP500 saw China's CPU-only **LineShine** debut at No. 1 on HPL (2.198 EFlop/s) while **El Capitan** held No. 1 on the mixed-precision **HPL-MxP** list at 16.7 EFlop/s — a 9.2× speedup over its own HPL score, versus only 3.6× for the CPU-only machine, a live demonstration of the precision ladder at exascale scale. We also look at the definitive **HPL-MxP benchmark paper** by Dongarra and Luszczek (2025), and at **NVFP4**, NVIDIA's 4-bit microscaling format, which shows what happens to the exponent/mantissa budget at the bottom of the ladder.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 The geometry of blocking (HPL-2.8)

**The problem.** HPL-2.7 established *data reuse*: matrix multiplication can reuse operands many times if you stage blocks of them in fast memory. The natural next question is quantitative: *how much* does reuse improve as the block grows, and why does the improvement have the specific shape it has?

**The mechanism: a cube versus its faces.** Take a *b×b* block of each operand A, B and C, and consider the core operation of blocked multiplication — the update C ← C + A·B on tiles. The arithmetic in one tile-tile-tile multiply is:

$$\text{work} = 2b^3 \ \text{FLOPs}$$

(each of *b²* output elements is a dot product of length *b*, at 2 FLOPs per multiply-add). The data that must be *available* to do it is three *b×b* tiles:

$$\text{data} = 3b^2 \ \text{elements}$$

The FLOP-per-element-loaded ratio is therefore

$$\frac{2b^3}{3b^2} = \frac{2}{3}\,b \ \text{FLOPs/element} \quad\propto\quad b.$$

This is the surface-to-volume ratio: the tile's *work* scales with its volume (*b²·b = b³*), while the data it needs scales with its *surface* (*b²*). Doubling the tile side roughly halves the data movement per FLOP. In floating-point terms, work lives in the interior (every pairing of a row-fragment and column-fragment is a new dot product), while data movement lives on the boundary — you only need to *fetch* each tile once and it participates in *b* dot products per output element.

**A concrete FP64 example.** In FP64 each element is 8 bytes, so streaming three tiles costs 24*b²* bytes for 2*b³* FLOPs:

| Tile side *b* | FLOP/byte (tile streaming) | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 0.67 | memory-bound, barely worth blocking |
| 32 | 2.7 | typical shared-memory tile |
| 64 | 5.3 | typical register/L2 blocking unit |
| 128 | 10.7 | |
| 256 | 21.3 | large-block GEMM territory |

Now put machine numbers on it. An A100 GPU peaks at 19.5 TFLOP/s of FP64 Tensor Core throughput against ~1.55 TB/s of HBM bandwidth — a **machine balance** of 19.5/1.55 ≈ **12.5 FLOP/byte**. For the tile update to be compute-bound rather than bandwidth-bound, its intensity must exceed 12.5 FLOP/byte, which (from the table, with the simple streaming model) requires *b* ≳ 150. This is why real GEMM kernels and HPL block sizes *NB* typically live in the 100–300 range, not 16 or 32: below that, the trailing-matrix update — the phase that owns ~80–90% of HPL's FLOPs — becomes memory-bound and the GPU starves. (The streaming model is optimistic — it ignores output write-back and cache conflicts — but the scaling conclusion is exactly right.)

**The same geometry at every scale.** Surface-to-volume is scale-free; it reappears at each level of the hierarchy with the same *linear-in-b* cure:

- **Registers/shared memory:** larger register tiles reuse each loaded fragment more (this is the register-tiling you met conceptually in HPL-2.7, and formally in HPL-9.3/9.4).
- **MPI messages:** a rank owning an *n/√P × n/√P* patch of the matrix communicates along its edges. Irony, Toledo and Tiskin proved (2004) that any "2D" algorithm using *O(n²/P)* memory per processor must communicate Ω(*n²/√P*) words — communication is governed by the *perimeter* of each processor's share of the matrix, and you cannot beat geometry by cleverness. Words moved per FLOP scales like √*P*/(2*n*): bigger problems amortize communication, bigger processor counts intensify it.
- **Network bisection:** splitting the machine in half cuts through the matrix; the boundary of that cut is *O(n²)* worth of data that may need to cross it.

**Why not just make *b* huge?** Because the trade-offs bend the other way. Larger blocks mean: fewer, larger messages (good for bandwidth, but latency-hiding and pipeline overlap get coarser); less parallelism in panel-like phases where only a few blocks are active (you will meet this as the panel bottleneck in module 8); worse load balance at the end of the factorization when the trailing matrix shrinks to a few blocks; and more fast-memory real estate consumed per stage. Surface-to-volume tells you the *benefit* curve of *b*; the costs, and the point where they cross, is the tuning problem — which is exactly what HPL's *NB* parameter (HPL-12.4, HPL-13.3) exposes to you.

This closes the data-movement module: an algorithm is efficient when its work scales like volume while its communication scales like surface, and the block size is the dial that sets both.

### 1.2 From real numbers to a finite grid (HPL-3.1)

**The problem.** Everything in section 1.1 assumed arithmetic operates on "numbers." But the real line ℝ is uncountable — between any two distinct reals lie infinitely many others — while a 64-bit machine word selects from finitely many (2⁶⁴) bit patterns. No finite representation can store even the interval [0,1] exactly. Every non-integer datum in an HPC run is therefore already an *approximation* the moment it is stored, before any arithmetic happens.

**The mechanism: binary scientific notation.** Floating point borrows the structure of scientific notation. Any nonzero real *x* can be written uniquely as

$$x = \pm m \times 2^{e}, \qquad 1 \le m < 2$$

(for example, 85.125 = 1.010101001₂ × 2⁶). The digit string *m* (the **significand** or mantissa) carries the *precision*; the integer *e* (the **exponent**) carries the *magnitude*. A floating-point format stores a *truncated* version of *m* (t binary digits) and a bounded integer *e*, plus a sign. What survives is a finite subset of ℝ.

**What that subset looks like.** The crucial property: within each **binade** [2^e, 2^(e+1)) the format provides exactly 2^t evenly spaced representable numbers, with spacing 2^(e−t). Consequences:

- **The grid is nonuniform — logarithmic, not linear.** Near 1.0, FP64 numbers are packed 2⁻⁵² ≈ 2.2×10⁻¹⁶ apart; near 2⁵⁰ they are 2⁻² ≈ 0.25 apart; near 2⁶⁰ they are 64 apart. The same 52 fraction bits stretch to cover whatever binade the exponent selects.
- **Relative spacing is (nearly) constant.** The spacing between neighbors within a binade is 2^(e−t) ≈ x·2^(−t): proportional to *x* itself. This is the design decision that separates floating point from fixed point. A fixed-point grid with spacing δ has *absolute* error ≤ δ/2 everywhere — useless for quantities that range over many orders of magnitude. A floating-point grid delivers *relative* error ≤ 2^(−t−1) everywhere in its range, at the cost of that error growing with magnitude.
- **Gaps are real behavior, not an abstraction.** In FP32 (t = 23), 1 + 10⁻⁸ rounds back to exactly 1.0, because 10⁻⁸ is smaller than half the local spacing (2⁻²⁴ ≈ 6×10⁻⁸). At 8192 = 2¹³, the FP32 spacing is 2^(13−23) = 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 9.8×10⁻⁴ — adding 0.001 to 8192.0 does nothing. A dot product that sums 10⁵ terms *in the wrong order* can repeatedly add tiny corrections to a large partial sum and lose every one of them; the corrections fall below the local grid spacing. (This failure mode gets its own treatment as non-associativity in HPL-3.7 and error accumulation in HPL-3.8.)

**Why HPC cares specifically.** Dense linear algebra lives on long chains of multiply-adds — a single HPL run on *n* = 500,000 performs ~8×10¹⁶ FLOPs, each of which rounds its result to the local grid. The representable set is the terrain on which all of that rounding plays out; you cannot reason about pivoting strategies, residual checks, or mixed-precision recovery (all later in this module) without first seeing the grid itself. And the grid's logarithmic spacing is *why* the errors that matter in linear algebra are analyzed in relative, norm-scaled terms — the vocabulary of backward error and condition numbers (module 21) is built for a number system whose error is relative.

One more framing worth keeping: a floating-point format is a **budget**. It has a fixed word width; every bit given to the significand is a bit taken from the exponent (or the sign, but one sign bit is non-negotiable). Significand bits buy precision; exponent bits buy dynamic range. Hold that thought — the entire modern precision ladder in section 1.4 is this one budget drawn at different points.

### 1.3 Anatomy of a floating-point format (HPL-3.2)

**The mechanism.** An IEEE 754 binary format packs three fields into a word:

```
FP32 (32 bits):  | s | exponent (8) | fraction (23) |
                 | 1 |      8       |      23        |

FP64 (64 bits):  | s | exponent (11) | fraction (52) |
                 | 1 |      11        |      52        |
```

For a **normal** number, the value is

$$x = (-1)^s \times \left(1.f\right)_2 \times 2^{\,E - \text{bias}}$$

where *f* is the fraction bit string and *E* is the unsigned integer stored in the exponent field. Three design decisions deserve mechanism-level attention:

**1. The implicit leading bit.** Because normalization forces the significand into [1, 2), its leading binary digit is always 1 — so why spend a bit storing it? IEEE formats simply *omit* it: the stored fraction *f* is interpreted as 1.*f*. FP64 thus delivers 53 bits of precision while storing 52. (The trick fails only for zero and the smallest magnitudes, which is where subnormals come in below.) This is a small but genuine example of HPC formats squeezing representation for all it's worth — the same instinct that later gives you TF32's 19 bits inside a 32-bit datum.

**2. The biased exponent.** *e* ranges over negative and positive values, but a sign-magnitude or two's-complement exponent field would order badly in hardware (the classic problem: −1 as sign-magnitude is 1000…1, which sorts *above* +1). Instead the stored field is *E = e + bias*, with bias = 2^(w−1) − 1 (127 for FP32's 8 exponent bits, 1023 for FP64's 11). Now magnitude order in the encoded word matches numeric order for positive numbers, and range comparisons reduce to integer compares — cheap in hardware, and the foundation of tricks like subtracting floats by integer manipulation of their encodings.

**3. Reserved exponent values.** The two extreme exponent fields are not used for normal numbers. For FP32: *E* = 255 encodes ±∞ (fraction 0) and NaN (fraction ≠ 0); *E* = 0 encodes zero (fraction 0) and **subnormals** (fraction ≠ 0) — values of the form ±0.*f*₂ × 2^(−126) that give up the implicit leading 1 so the format can *gradually underflow* toward zero instead of jumping from 2⁻¹²⁶ straight to 0. Without subnormals, any algorithm producing a sequence shrinking to zero would hit a cliff; with them, the grid densifies all the way down. (Subnormal arithmetic can be slower on some processors — a real performance consideration in low-precision codes, worth remembering when we reach scaling in HPL-14.7.)

**A worked encoding.** Encode 85.125 in FP32.

1. 85.125 = 1010101.001₂ (85 = 1010101₂; 0.125 = 001₂).
2. Normalize: 1.010101001₂ × 2⁶, so *f* = 010101001 (padded to 23 bits with zeros), *e* = 6.
3. Biased exponent: 127 + 6 = 133 = 10000101₂.
4. Sign 0. The word:

```
0 10000101 01010100100000000000000  →  0x42AA4000
```

Decoding is the reverse: (1 + 0.010101001₂) × 2⁶ = 1.33...×64 = 85.125. Every FP64 constant in your HPL binary went through this exact procedure.

**The two formats that define HPC.** FP32 (binary32): 8 exponent bits give ~±3.4×10³⁸ of range; 23 fraction bits give ~7 decimal digits. FP64 (binary64): 11 exponent bits give ~±1.8×10³⁰⁸; 52 fraction bits give ~15–16 decimal digits. HPL is *defined* as an FP64 benchmark — its reported rate counts FP64 FLOPs regardless of what the hardware internally does — and until the mixed-precision era this made FP64 throughput the gold metric of supercomputing (TOP500 ranks by it). The reason FP64 exists at all in GPUs is precisely the dense-linear-algebra constituency: NVIDIA's matrix engines carry FP64 paths (19.5 TFLOP/s on A100, 2.5× its CUDA-core FP64) for this workload class.

Everything in later topics — rounding (3.5), overflow/underflow (3.6), FMA (3.9), and the entire mixed-precision module — is a statement about *these fields*: what happens at their edges (exponent extremes), how much resolution *t* fraction bits buy, and what it costs to change *t*.

### 1.4 The precision ladder of modern HPC (HPL-3.3)

**The problem.** Matrix hardware (Tensor Cores and equivalents) multiplies faster when operands are narrower. But "narrower" can mean fewer *mantissa* bits or fewer *exponent* bits — and the two fail differently. The HPC precision zoo is the set of points where different architects have drawn that line.

**The budget split.** Every format below FP64 is the same 1.3-anatomy with a different (exponent, mantissa) allocation:

| Format | Total bits | Exponent | Mantissa | ε (spacing at 1) | Max normal | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP64 | 64 | 11 | 52 | 2⁻⁵² ≈ 2.2×10⁻¹⁶ | ~1.8×10³⁰⁸ | reference, HPL currency |
| FP32 | 32 | 8 | 23 | 2⁻²³ ≈ 1.2×10⁻⁷ | ~3.4×10³⁸ | legacy HPC default |
| TF32 | 19 (in FP32) | 8 | 10 | 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 9.8×10⁻⁴ | FP32's | FP32 range, FP16 precision |
| BF16 | 16 | 8 | 7 | 2⁻⁷ ≈ 7.8×10⁻³ | FP32's | range-first 16-bit |
| FP16 | 16 | 5 | 10 | 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 9.8×10⁻⁴ | 65,504 | precision-first 16-bit |
| FP8 E4M3 | 8 | 4 | 3 | 2⁻³ = 0.125 | ±448 | precision-first 8-bit |
| FP8 E5M2 | 8 | 5 | 2 | 2⁻² = 0.25 | ±57,344 | range-first 8-bit |

Read the table as three design decisions:

**FP64 → FP32** keeps the *shape* of the number (wide range, decent precision) and just narrows both fields. Memory halves; on matrix hardware, throughput does *not* double (FP32 CUDA cores ≈ FP64 Tensor Cores on A100: 19.5 vs 19.5 TFLOP/s) — FP32 is not where the matrix-engine payoff lives.

**FP32 → TF32 → BF16/FP16** is where the ladder gets steep. **TF32** (introduced with NVIDIA Ampere/A100, 2020) is the cleanest expression of the budget idea: keep FP32's 8 exponent bits (full FP32 dynamic range — no rescaling needed for existing FP32 data) but truncate the mantissa to 10 bits *inside the Tensor Core*. It is explicitly **not a storage format**: memory still holds FP32; the hardware rounds operands to 19 bits on the way into the matrix unit and accumulates in FP32. The payoff on A100: 156 TFLOP/s TF32 Tensor Core versus 19.5 TFLOP/s FP32 — **8×** for the cost of ~3 decimal digits of input precision. This is the template for all "drop mantissa bits, keep the exponent" designs; BF16 is the same idea at 16 bits (FP32's top 16 bits verbatim — conversion is literally truncation), giving 312 TFLOP/s on A100.

FP16 takes the opposite bet for the same 16 bits: 10 mantissa bits (like TF32) but only 5 exponent bits. Precision survives; range collapses to ±65,504 with minimum normal 2⁻¹⁴ ≈ 6.1×10⁻⁵. In deep learning that narrowness is manageable; in linear algebra, intermediate quantities in a factorization can easily leave that window, which is why mixed-precision solvers pair FP16 compute with **scaling** (HPL-14.7) and FP32 accumulation.

**FP8** splits again into two dialects, standardized by the NVIDIA/Arm/Intel FP8 paper (Micikevicius et al., 2022): **E4M3** (3 mantissa bits, max ±448, no infinities, one NaN pattern — range sacrificed to squeeze out one more mantissa bit) for weights and activations, and **E5M2** (2 mantissa bits, ±57,344, IEEE-style specials) for gradients, whose magnitudes swing wildly. Native on Hopper-class matrix engines. With only 3 mantissa bits, E4M3's spacing at 1.0 is 0.125 — the grid is appallingly coarse by FP64 standards, and yet (as the HPL-MxP literature shows) FP8-class compute can drive the bulk of an LU factorization if a higher-precision correction pass follows.

**The throughput pattern.** On A100's dense (non-sparse) Tensor Cores: FP64 19.5 → TF32 156 → FP16/BF16 312 TFLOP/s. Each step removes ~half the mantissa bits and roughly doubles throughput. The ladder is not an accident of marketing; it is the datapath-width economics of section 1.2's budget made silicon. And its top-to-bottom span — 19.5 to 312, sixteen-fold — *is* the headroom that HPL-MxP was created to expose and that iterative refinement (module 15) exists to harvest safely: factorize on a cheap rung, correct on an expensive one, spend most FLOPs where they are cheap.

Two honest caveats. First, the table's throughputs are vendor peak numbers at boost clock — sustained rates depend on shapes, occupancy, and the memory system (HPL-9.10 will quantify this). Second, "HPC precision" is not just these: INT8/INT4 exist on the same engines, and 4-bit microscaling formats (see Development 3) now extend the ladder below FP8 — but the same exponent/mantissa budget logic governs all of them.

### 1.5 Machine epsilon: one number that prices precision (HPL-3.4)

**The definition.** **Machine epsilon** (ε_mach) is the gap between 1.0 and the next larger representable floating-point number. From section 1.2's grid picture, 1.0 sits at the bottom of binade [2⁰, 2¹), whose spacing is 2^(0−t); hence

$$\varepsilon_{\text{FP64}} = 2^{-52} \approx 2.22\times10^{-16}, \qquad \varepsilon_{\text{FP32}} = 2^{-23} \approx 1.19\times10^{-7}.$$

Equivalently: ε is the weight of the *last* mantissa bit. One number per format, and it is the format's price tag — see the ε column in the section 1.4 table, where it collapses the whole ladder: 2.2×10⁻¹⁶ (FP64) → 9.8×10⁻⁴ (TF32/FP16) → 0.25 (E5M2). Sixteen orders of magnitude from top to bottom of the ladder, in one column.

**Unit roundoff.** A closely related and more operational quantity is the **unit roundoff** u = ½ε (under round-to-nearest), the worst-case *relative* error of a single correctly-rounded operation. With round-to-nearest, every basic operation ★ satisfies the fundamental rounding model

$$\text{fl}(a \star b) = (a \star b)(1+\delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u.$$

(The nomenclature is unfortunately not universal — some texts call u itself "machine epsilon," others use ε for the spacing and u for the bound. What is invariant is the pair of values 2^(−t) and 2^(−t−1); when a paper says "machine epsilon," check which it means.) The spacing at an arbitrary magnitude x is the **unit in the last place**, ulp(x) ≈ x·ε; it doubles at every power of two — the staircase you can already picture from section 1.2.

**Why ε is the right lens for HPL.** Three concrete payoffs:

1. **It bounds what any algorithm can achieve.** No sequence of FP64 operations can return a result with relative error below ~u ≈ 1.1×10⁻¹⁶ for generic data. A solver claiming FP64-accurate output from an FP16-precision factorization is not cheating physics — it is *correcting* error — but it can never do better than the precision of its final correction steps. ε is the floor.
2. **It is built into benchmark acceptance criteria.** HPL does not ask whether the computed solution x̂ equals the true x — with ε-sized arithmetic noise over ~10¹⁶ operations, nothing could pass that test. Instead it checks a *backward-error*-style scaled residual, normalized by machine epsilon, the problem size, and norms of the problem data, against a small threshold (the shipped default is 16). The validation is therefore calibrated exactly to ε: "is the answer as good as FP64 arithmetic could plausibly deliver?" — a theme that becomes formal in module 21.
3. **It quantifies the cost of the ladder.** A TF32 multiply-add introduces input rounding errors up to ~2⁻¹¹ (rounding two operands to 10-bit mantissas, then rounding the product) — roughly 2⁴⁻⁵ *billion* times coarser than FP64's u. That is the numerical debt HPL-MxP's refinement loop exists to pay down; convergence analysis of that loop (module 15) is literally a race between ε-sized correction gains and the coarser ε of the factorization precision.

**A closing example that ties the whole volume together.** Sum a length-*n* dot product in FP32. Each add can lose up to half an ulp of the running sum; in the worst ordering the total rounding error is bounded by roughly (n−1)·u·Σ|xᵢ| — with n = 10⁵ and u ≈ 6×10⁻⁸, worst-case relative corruption reaches the percent scale, and pairwise/tree summation (which is what blocked, surface-to-volume-friendly kernels naturally do!) reduces the bound to log₂(n)·u — about 10⁻⁶. The geometry of section 1.1 and the grid of sections 1.2–1.4 are not separate concerns: *blocking improves both the communication pattern and the error-accumulation pattern of the same computation*. That double dividend is one of the quiet reasons dense linear algebra scales as well as it does.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 June 2026 TOP500: a CPU-only machine wins HPL, but the precision ladder rules HPL-MxP

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 (Hamburg, June 23, 2026), the 67th TOP500 list debuted **LineShine**, installed at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, China, at No. 1 with **2.198 EFlop/s on HPL** — about 80% of its 2.736 EFlop/s theoretical peak, and the first system to sustain two exaflops of FP64. It is a CPU-only machine: a claimed 13.79 million cores of the custom LingKun platform (304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz, proprietary LingQi interconnect), with no GPU/accelerator matrix engines. El Capitan (LLNL) dropped to No. 2 at 1.809 EFlop/s; five systems now exceed 1 EFlop/s on HPL (LineShine, El Capitan, Frontier, Aurora, JUPITER Booster).

**What changed technically.** The more interesting split is on the **HPL-MxP** list, where El Capitan remains No. 1 at **16.7 EFlop/s — a 9.2× speedup over its own FP64 HPL score** — followed by Aurora (11.6 EFlop/s, 11.5×) and Frontier (11.4 EFlop/s, 8.4×). LineShine debuts on HPL-MxP at only No. 4 (7.92 EFlop/s), with a **3.6×** speedup over its HPL result. Further down, SoftBank's CHIE-4 posted the field's largest mixed-precision gain at **24.4×**.

**Why it matters, in today's terms.** That speedup column is the precision ladder of section 1.4 measured on real exascale iron. The accelerator-based systems (MI300A, Intel Data Center GPU Max, GH200-class) have wide, low-precision matrix datapaths, so dropping from FP64 to FP16/FP8-class compute multiplies throughput by ~8–11×. A CPU design with wide vector FP units has no comparably fast low-precision path, so its mixed-precision gain is a modest 3.6× — LineShine can win the FP64 race and still trail by 2× on the mixed-precision one. Same lesson from the other end: CHIE-4's 24.4× shows that the achievable speedup depends not just on hardware but on how much of the algorithm (panel work, refinement, FP64 corrections) can be dragged onto the fast rungs — which is exactly the coupling this curriculum builds toward in modules 14–16.

**What to watch.** Whether CPU vendors respond with matrix engines or block-FP vector extensions; and whether future HPL-MxP leaders emerge from FP8/FP4-class hardware, where the raw ladder is steeper still but the ε-priced numerical debt (section 1.5) is correspondingly larger.

### 2.2 The HPL-MxP benchmark paper: mixed precision, GMRES refinement, and the ε argument (Dongarra & Luszczek, 2025)

**What it is.** "HPL-MxP Benchmark: Mixed-Precision Algorithms, Iterative Refinement, and Scalable Data Generation" (Jack Dongarra and Piotr Luszczek, arXiv:2509.19618, September 2025; published in the *International Journal of High Performance Computing Applications*) is the benchmark authors' own definitive description of what HPL-MxP measures and why it is numerically legitimate.

**What it establishes (paper claims).** The benchmark factors the matrix with a lower-precision LU and then recovers accuracy with a *non-stationary* iterative refinement based on GMRES — a stronger correction scheme than classical refinement, needed because the factorization precision (FP16/FP32-class ε, per section 1.5) is too coarse for plain refinement to converge from a cold start. The paper evaluates the numerical stability of the benchmark's scalable input-matrix generation and shows how its diagonal scaling affects solution quality measured in backward error. It also reports exascale-level throughput results on TOP500-class installations, demonstrating the benchmark's viability for evaluating machines that mix HPC and AI hardware.

**Connection to today's topics.** This paper is the destination of the road you started today: the precision ladder (1.4) supplies the fast rung for the factorization; machine epsilon (1.5) supplies the vocabulary for *why* the correction pass works — the refinement drives the *backward* error down toward the FP64 grid even though most FLOPs were computed on a grid a billion times coarser; and the surface-to-volume geometry (1.1) is what keeps the low-precision phase running at matrix-engine speed in the first place. When the curriculum reaches modules 14–16, this paper is the primary reference to read in full.

**What to verify as you read it later.** Which precision combination each reported result actually used (implementations vary), and how the backward-error acceptance criterion compares with HPL's ε-scaled residual check.

### 2.3 NVFP4: the exponent/mantissa budget at four bits (NVIDIA Blackwell)

**What it is.** With Blackwell (2024), NVIDIA's Tensor Cores added 4-bit floating-point matrix arithmetic. Plain FP4 (E2M1: 1 sign, 2 exponent, 1 mantissa) can represent only ±{0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6} — a format with ε = 0.5 and a 24× range, unusable alone for real tensors. NVIDIA's **NVFP4** makes it usable with **microscaling**: tensors are divided into blocks of 16 elements, and each block shares an FP8 **E4M3 scale factor**; the community **MXFP4** variant (OCP-defined) uses 32-element blocks with power-of-two E8M0 scales.

**The mechanism worth studying.** This is section 1.4's budget argument taken to its limit — and then *escaped* by adding a second level. When 2 exponent bits cannot buy enough dynamic range, you borrow it from a per-block shared exponent carried at FP8 precision. The effective number system is no longer one grid but a family of grids, re-anchored every 16 elements. Everything you learned today still applies, just twice: E2M1 is the inner budget (almost all mantissa, minimal range), E4M3 the outer one. Per NVIDIA's technical blog, Blackwell's Transformer Engine handles grouping, dynamic scaling, and the 4-bit matrix ops in hardware, and NVFP4 pretraining results (arXiv 2509.25149) indicate it can match BF16 quality on large LLM runs.

**Relevance to this curriculum.** HPL-MxP does not use FP4 today. But the architectural trend is unambiguous: matrix engines keep widening the ladder downward, which keeps widening the throughput gap between the cheap rungs and FP64 — and therefore keeps raising the value of the algorithmic machinery (scaling, refinement, error analysis) that this module is building toward. Watch this space at the HPL-MxP level: the first credible FP4-class HPL-MxP result would make the 24.4× speedups of today look conservative.
