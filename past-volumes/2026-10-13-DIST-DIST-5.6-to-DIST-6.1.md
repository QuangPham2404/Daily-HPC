## 0. Concise Summary

Modules 1–4 gave us the vocabulary of messages and collectives; DIST-5.1–5.5 (October 6) gave us the arithmetic of a *single* transfer — latency, bandwidth, software overhead, injection rate — as if each message had the network to itself. It doesn't. Today's first four topics complete Module 5 by asking what happens when the fabric is a **shared** resource: many flows competing (contention), topology deciding which paths exist and how long they are (hop count, bisection bandwidth), and the application-level consequence of all of it (why communication becomes more dominant as you scale out). The fifth topic then opens Module 6, the GPU-specific part of the curriculum, with the **host-staging path** — the default way a GPU's data reached the network for the first decade of GPU computing, and the baseline every modern optimization (GPUDirect RDMA, NCCL, NVSHMEM) is measured against.

The mechanical core of the day is a chain of cause and effect. **Contention** (DIST-5.6) arises because several flows must share one link or buffer; in lossless fabrics like InfiniBand, credit-based flow control converts local overload into *backpressure* that can spread backwards through switches as a congestion tree, throttling even flows that had nothing to do with the original hotspot. **Hop count** (DIST-5.7) matters because every switch a message crosses adds pipeline delay, buffering, and arbitration variance — and because the topology's diameter (three hops for a dragonfly, five-to-six switch crossings for a three-tier fat-tree, growing with machine size for a torus) is a design decision with cost consequences. **Bisection bandwidth** (DIST-5.8) is the honest aggregate metric: the minimum capacity across any half-and-half cut of the machine. A 3D torus's bisection grows only as N^(2/3); a fat-tree buys full bisection with more switches and cables; a dragonfly trades some of it away (Frontier's global links are tapered to 57% of local bandwidth) in exchange for a ~30% cheaper network. **Scaling** (DIST-5.9) is where this meets your code: under strong scaling, each rank's subdomain shrinks, so its halo — the surface it must exchange — grows *relative* to the volume it computes (ratio ∝ p^(1/3) in 3D), while fixed-size global operations stop shrinking at all; weak scaling is kind to nearest-neighbor patterns but hostile to all-to-all ones.

Finally, **host staging** (DIST-6.1) traces the four-copy path a naive GPU-to-GPU message takes — GPU → PCIe → pinned host buffer → NIC → network → NIC → host buffer → PCIe → GPU — and counts what it costs: doubled PCIe traffic, doubled DRAM traffic, CPU involvement, and serialization at the slowest stage.

Three developments anchor the lesson: NVIDIA's **MRC transport**, open-sourced through OCP in May 2026, spreads a single RDMA connection across many network paths — an endpoint-side attack on the path-collision problem; the **OLCF evaluation of Frontier's tapered dragonfly versus Summit's fat-tree**, the first production-scale measurement of what trading bisection bandwidth for cost actually costs applications; and a 2026 **multi-GPU FDTD study** measuring host staging against direct GPU-to-GPU exchange — a 2.46–2.76× difference on the exact path DIST-6.1 describes.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

Notation carries over from October 6: *p* ranks, *m* message size, **α** per-message startup latency, **β = 1/B** per-byte cost. All of Module 5's models so far assume the transfer owns its path. Today we let other traffic in, then let the topology constrain the paths, then let the application's scaling behavior determine how much traffic there is — and we end by descending into the node, where the GPU-era communication path begins.

### 1.0 From a private pipe to a shared fabric

Keep one picture in mind for the whole issue. A network is a graph of links and switches, and at any instant many messages are traversing it simultaneously. Every model from DIST-5.1–5.5 still holds *locally* — each flow obeys *T(m) = α + mβ* on whatever share of the resources it actually gets. What changes today is the word **share**. When eight flows converge on one switch uplink, each gets roughly B/8 (DIST-5.6). Which uplinks exist, and how many of them there are across the machine, is topology (DIST-5.7, 5.8). How many flows your application generates per rank, and how big each is, is scaling behavior (DIST-5.9). And whether the first hop of the journey is even efficient is the host-staging question (DIST-6.1).

### 1.1 Contention (DIST-5.6)

**The problem.** Contention is multiple flows competing for a shared resource: an output link, a switch buffer, a NIC's transmit queue, a DMA engine, or host memory bandwidth. Distinct from congestion on a single flow's path (a transient queue), contention is a *structural* collision: aggregate demand exceeds a resource's capacity. The simplest model: if *k* equal flows share one bottleneck link of bandwidth *B*, each flow realizes ~*B/k*, no matter what the link rate is. That single sentence explains a large fraction of "my 200 Gb/s fabric runs at 25 Gb/s" reports.

**Where contention lives.** Four layers, in increasing order of blame-worthiness:

1. **Endpoint contention** — ranks on a node sharing one NIC or one rail. This is the injection-bandwidth arithmetic of DIST-5.5 run in reverse: eight processes pushing into one ConnectX port each get an eighth of it if their flows collide.
2. **Switch contention** — flows whose paths share an output port queue behind one another.
3. **Fabric-wide path collisions** — deterministic routing (hash-based ECMP on Ethernet, static LID-based routing on InfiniBand) assigns each flow one path; two heavy flows that hash to the same link throttle each other while parallel links sit idle. The October 6 volume quoted measurements of fat-trees realizing only ~25–40% of available bandwidth under synchronized collectives for exactly this reason.
4. **Memory-system contention** — NIC DMA engines, MPI internal copies, and application kernels all drawing on the same host memory bandwidth; also GPU-side, copy engines contending with compute for PCIe.

**The mechanism that makes HPC contention special: lossless backpressure.** Ethernet's answer to overload is to drop packets and let TCP retransmit. HPC fabrics cannot afford that — a dropped packet costs a retransmission timeout that dwarfs a microsecond-scale MPI latency — so InfiniBand and RoCEv2-with-PFC are **lossless**: a receiver grants **credits** (each credit = 64 bytes of receive buffer in InfiniBand) and the sender may not transmit beyond the granted ceiling. Losslessness is bought with backpressure: when a downstream buffer fills, the upstream port simply stops receiving credits and stops sending.

The consequence is the **congestion tree**. When one output port becomes a hotspot (the *root*), its input-side buffers fill; the switches feeding those inputs exhaust their credits and stall; their upstream buffers fill in turn, and the stall propagates backwards toward the sources. A flow that shares a stalled queue with hotspot traffic but is headed somewhere else entirely — a **victim flow** — is blocked behind it. This is **head-of-line (HOL) blocking**, and in an input-queued switch it is structural: the blocked packet is at the head of a queue that other packets must pass through.

This is not a theoretical hazard. A 23-day measurement study of the production 72K-core InfiniBand cluster *Yellowstone* (TMA 2017) read switch port counters every 100 ms and found that **ToR uplink ports were stalled waiting for flow-control credits in ~60% of observed intervals** — usually briefly, but sometimes for 60–100% of an interval. Those stalls directly stretch the MPI ranks behind them, and one slow rank stretches every collective the job runs.

**InfiniBand congestion control.** The architecture specifies a rate-based feedback loop: a congested switch output port (crossing a buffer threshold, with credits still available — the *root* condition) marks packets with a **FECN** bit; the destination reflects it back as a **BECN**; the source's channel adapter consults a Congestion Control Table and inserts an **injection rate delay** between packets, throttling the contributor at the root. The mechanism is deliberately rate-based rather than TCP-window-based: in a low-latency fabric the bandwidth-delay product is so small that any useful window already saturates the network, so the control variable must be injection *rate*, not bytes-in-flight. The parameters (thresholds, marking rate, CCTI step sizes) are notoriously coupled — one setting that is optimal for one topology and traffic mix degrades another — which is why production deployments often run with it disabled (Yellowstone did) and why research alternatives (e.g., RGBCC) aim for self-tuning.

**The Ethernet mirror.** RoCEv2 achieves losslessness by reaction instead of construction: Priority Flow Control fires PAUSE frames upstream *after* buffers approach full, with reserved headroom to catch in-flight bytes. That is a race rather than a guarantee — hence the PAUSE-storm pathologies and the ECN/DCQCN tuning burden that RoCE operators know well, and ultimately the motivation for both Ultra Ethernet's and NVIDIA MRC's redesigns (Section 2).

**What an engineer does about contention.** The levers are structural, not magical: spread flows across paths (adaptive routing, packet spraying, MRC); avoid synchronized traffic where possible (skewed collective start, chunking — Module 11); place ranks to match traffic to topology (Module 12); and on RoCE, tune or replace the lossless machinery. Diagnostically: per-flow bandwidth far below link rate with idle parallel links is a collision signature; stalls visible in `PortXmitWait`-style counters are backpressure.

### 1.2 Hop count (DIST-5.7)

**Why each hop costs.** A switch is not a wire. Crossing one involves receiving and decoding the link-layer packet, parsing the routing header, looking up the output port, arbitrating for the crossbar among competing packets, traversing the crossbar, and queuing at the output — each stage consuming tens of nanometers of pipeline and, under load, variable queueing time. Modern HPC switches add a few hundred nanoseconds per hop (order-of-magnitude; exact figures are vendor- and generation-specific), and per-hop *variance* matters as much as the mean: every arbitration is a lottery your latency-sensitive small message can lose. A message crossing *H* switches also consumes *H* buffers' worth of fabric memory and *H* arbitration slots that other flows wanted — hop count is a *shared-resource multiplier*, which is why it lives next to contention.

**Diameter versus average distance.** The **diameter** (worst-case hop count) and the average hop count are topology properties. Comparing the three canonical families at real scale:

| Topology | Switch hops (typical worst case) | How it scales |
|---|---|---|
| 3D torus / mesh | up to 3k/2 per dimension (k nodes per edge) | average distance **grows** with machine size (~N^(1/3) in 3D) |
| 3-tier fat-tree (folded Clos) | 5 switch crossings cross-pod (L0→L1→L2→L1′→L0′) | constant for a given tier count; tiers grow with radix limits |
| Dragonfly | 3 (local → global → local) | constant by construction |

The torus is the cautionary tale: it is cheap, wire-efficient, and *wonderful* for nearest-neighbor physics codes — but its average hop count grows with the machine, so latency- and hop-sensitive global operations degrade as the system scales. This is precisely the observation that motivated the low-diameter era: if pin bandwidth lets you build wide switches, spend it on *short* paths.

**The dragonfly's move (Kim et al., ISCA 2008).** The dragonfly attacks hop count directly: group *a* routers plus their endpoint ports into a **group** wired internally (e.g., as a flattened butterfly) so that the group acts as one **virtual router** of effective radix *k′ = a(p + h)* — far beyond any physical switch. Groups connect to each other with a full all-to-all of **global links**; a minimally-routed packet crosses at most one global link, so the network **diameter is three**: source group (local hop) → destination group (global hop) → destination router (local hop). With radix-64 routers the construction scales beyond 256K endpoints at diameter three, and the paper's cost analysis showed ~20% savings versus a flattened butterfly and ~52% versus a folded Clos at ≥16K nodes — mostly by minimizing the *long, expensive optical* cables.

**The catch that ties 5.6 and 5.7 together.** Minimal hop count is not minimal time under contention. A minimal dragonfly route has few global-link alternatives between a hot source/destination group pair; under adversarial traffic those global links congest while indirect paths sit idle. So production dragonflies (Cray/Slingshot, and its academic ancestors) run **adaptive, sometimes non-minimal routing**: an overloaded flow is *deliberately sent through an intermediate group* — more hops, less contention, faster arrival. The principle generalizes: **hop count is a cost you pay per packet; contention is a cost you pay per contending byte.** A good fabric spends the first to avoid the second. (Frontier's production configuration biases 5% of traffic away from minimal routes to pre-spread load — Section 2.2.)

### 1.3 Bisection bandwidth (DIST-5.8)

**Definition.** Split the machine's endpoints into two equal halves — *any* way you like — and sum the bandwidth of the links crossing the cut. The **bisection bandwidth** is the *minimum* over all possible splits. The minimum is the point: it measures the network's capacity under the *adversarial* partition, i.e., the worst-case half-to-half traffic pattern the machine can carry at full rate. Bisection bandwidth is to aggregate marketing numbers what effective bandwidth (DIST-5.4) was to link rate: the honest metric.

**Why it is the right abstraction for collectives and all-to-all.** A ring all-reduce (DIST-4.10) needs only neighbor links — it ignores bisection entirely. But an all-to-all (transpose, FFT remap), a large all-gather, or a broadcast tree's root links must push a large fraction of the machine's traffic *across some cut*. The slowest cut is the ceiling. Concretely: for p ranks each injecting B, an all-to-all where every rank sends m bytes to every other rank pushes p²m/4 bytes across the bisection (the half of traffic whose source and destination are on opposite sides); the achievable time is bounded below by that volume divided by the bisection bandwidth.

**How the canonical topologies compare.**

- **3D torus:** a k×k×k torus (N = k³) has bisection 2k² links — growing as **N^(2/3)**, strictly sublinear. As a *fraction* of full bisection (N/2 links' worth), it is 4/N^(1/3): ~40% at N = 1,000, ~4% at N = 1,000,000. This is the mathematical statement of "torus networks are cheap and local-friendly but globally starved."
- **Fat-tree (folded Clos):** full bisection *by construction* — that was Leiserson's 1985 point of making links "fatter" toward the root, realized today as a Clos network where every pod has enough uplinks that any half-to-half permutation can run at injection rate. The price is switches and cables: since no compute hangs off aggregation/core tiers, the switch and cable counts dominate the network budget.
- **Dragonfly:** global-link count per group pair determines bisection. A *balanced* dragonfly (a = 2p = 2h in Kim et al.'s notation) can in principle deliver full bisection for benign traffic, but the *paths* are the weak point — few minimal global routes per pair — so *effective* bisection depends on adaptive routing doing its job (again 5.6 and 5.7 intertwined).

**Measured, deployed reality: the taper.** Real leadership systems do not buy full bisection. **Frontier** (ORNL, Slingshot dragonfly) has a global-to-local **taper ratio of 57%**: the bandwidth leaving a group toward the rest of the machine is 57% of the bandwidth its nodes can inject locally. Frontier's designers pay that tax deliberately; the OLCF evaluation (Section 2.2) is the first production-scale measurement of who actually got billed.

**A worked comparison.** Take N = 32,768 endpoints, per the OLCF cost model: a non-blocking 3-level fat-tree needs ~2,560 radix-64 switches and 2× more optical cables than a dragonfly organizing the same endpoints into 64 groups of 512 (64,512 electrical + 16,128 optical cables, 2,048 switches). The fat-tree buys: any permutation at full rate, deterministic routes, no adaptive-routing requirements. The dragonfly buys: ~30% lower network cost (~3% of total system cost), diameter three, and the *obligation* to run adaptive non-minimal routing to keep its global links balanced. Neither is "better"; they are different points on the cost–bisection–complexity surface, chosen by matching the expected workload mix (nearest-neighbor and stencil-heavy → the dragonfly's taper is nearly free; global-communication-heavy → you will feel it).

**Bisection and the earlier models.** Bisection bandwidth is the fabric-level β in an α-β model of *global* operations: time for a bisection-crossing phase ≈ α-term + (volume across cut)·(1/bisection). When a vendor quotes switch capacity, ask for the bisection of the *as-built* fabric *with the routing it actually runs* — that number, like all effective-bandwidth numbers, is lower.

### 1.4 Strong and weak scaling communication effects (DIST-5.9)

**The problem.** Everything so far prices one message or one collective. Applications run billions of them, and the *ratio* of communication to computation — not either quantity alone — determines whether scaling pays. DIST-5.9 is the topic where the α-β arithmetic meets Amdahl's and Gustafson's laws.

**Setup: the halo exchange as the canonical local pattern.** Decompose a 3D grid of N³ points over p ranks as a cube of cubes: each rank owns a subdomain of side L = N/p^(1/3). A stencil update of radius h needs h layers of neighbor data; each time step, every rank exchanges its **halo** — six faces of area L² — with six neighbors. Per-rank communication volume ∝ **surface** (6hL²); per-rank computation ∝ **volume** (L³). The surface-to-volume ratio is

```
comm/compute ∝ 6hL² / L³ = 6h / L = 6h·p^(1/3) / N
```

This one expression carries the entire strong-scaling story for local codes.

**Strong scaling (fixed problem, more ranks).** Amdahl: with serial fraction f, speedup ≤ 1/(f + (1−f)/p); overheads make reality worse than that. Communication is an overhead that does not just fail to shrink — it *grows*:

1. *Halo fraction grows as p^(1/3).* Fixed N, double p per dimension: L halves, the 6h/L ratio doubles. On N = 1000³: at p = 1,000 (L = 100) the halo-to-volume ratio is 6h/100; at p = 8,000 (L = 50) it is 6h/50 — double, while compute per rank *halved*. The crossover where halo exchange time equals compute time is the practical strong-scaling limit of the code, long before Amdahl's serial-fraction wall.
2. *Fixed-size global operations stop shrinking.* A residual-norm all-reduce costs ~α·log p (latency-dominated tree) regardless of N. Compute per rank ∝ 1/p. The ratio grows as p·log p — eventually every rank's timeline is nothing but all-reduce latency. This is the Amdahl wall wearing a different hat: the "serial fraction" is not sequential code but *latency-bound communication*.
3. *HPL/HPL-MxP is the same math in matrix form.* Flops ∝ (2/3)N³; panel broadcasts and swaps cost O(N² · log p) data movement; ratio ∝ log p / N. That ratio is why HPL runs the largest N that memory allows (and why HPL-MxP's even larger arithmetic intensity is what makes mixed-precision benchmarks scale further): growing N shrinks the ratio, growing p grows it. Strong-scaling HPL on a fixed matrix is the classic way to watch communication win.

**Weak scaling (problem grows with ranks).** Gustafson's re-interpretation: hold L constant (N ∝ p^(1/3) per rank... in practice N³ ∝ p), and the per-rank halo volume 6hL² is *constant* — perfect weak scaling for local patterns, in principle. But two structural effects still bite:

1. *Global latency grows.* Even with per-rank work constant, collectives over p ranks cost ≥ α·log p; over a million ranks that is real time per step, and it does not amortize.
2. *All-to-all weak scaling is impossible.* In a transpose/FFT-style pattern, every rank sends data to every other rank: per-rank volume ∝ p·(per-pair share). Double the machine at constant per-rank domain and per-rank all-to-all traffic *doubles*. Weak scaling is kind to surface-like patterns and structurally hostile to global ones — the application's communication *pattern* decides which law applies, not the word "weak."

**The countermeasure preview.** Temporal blocking (advance T local steps inside an enlarged halo before exchanging) cuts exchange *frequency* ~T-fold at the price of redundant computation in the enlarged ghost zone — exactly the trade-off measured in Section 2.3. Pipelining and overlap (Module 11) attack the exposed part of communication; the surface-to-volume expression above is what they are fighting.

**The takeaway table.**

| | Strong (fixed N, ↑p) | Weak (N ∝ p) |
|---|---|---|
| Halo (surface) volume per rank | grows ∝ p^(1/3) | constant |
| Global-reduce latency per rank | grows ∝ log p | grows ∝ log p |
| All-to-all volume per rank | constant-ish | grows ∝ p |
| Governing law | Amdahl + surface/volume | Gustafson + global terms |

### 1.5 The traditional host-staging path (DIST-6.1)

Modules 1–5 treated "the network" as if the interesting design space ended at the NIC. It doesn't: GPU clusters add a *second* boundary inside every node — GPU memory is a separate device memory behind PCIe, and for most of GPU computing's history the NIC could not touch it. Module 6 starts at that boundary, and DIST-6.1 documents the default solution every later topic optimizes away.

**The path.** Rank A's GPU holds the data; rank B's GPU needs it. Without GPU-aware communication:

```
GPU A (HBM) --PCIe--> host A staging buffer (DRAM) --DMA--> NIC A
      --> network --> NIC B --DMA--> host B staging buffer (DRAM)
      --PCIe--> GPU B (HBM)
```

Concretely, an MPI library that does not know about GPUs forces the application to `cudaMemcpy` device→host into a send buffer, call `MPI_Send` on the host buffer, then on the receiving side `MPI_Recv` into a host buffer and `cudaMemcpy` host→device. Even a "GPU-aware" library that silently stages does the same thing internally.

**Counting the costs.** Enumerate what that path pays versus a hypothetical direct one:

1. **Two extra PCIe crossings per direction.** The data crosses the GPU's PCIe link twice on each side (out of GPU A's HBM, later into GPU B's HBM). On a Gen4 x16 link (~32 GB/s per direction effective), 1 GiB costs ~32 ms of PCIe each way — before the network has moved a byte. Worse, those crossings often share the link with compute's own traffic (kernel launches, other transfers).
2. **Two extra DRAM round-trips.** The staging buffer is written and read on each host (NIC DMA in, cudaMemcpy out): roughly 4× the payload in host DRAM traffic per message end-to-end — bandwidth that the application's own host-side work wanted.
3. **CPU involvement.** The CPU issues the memcpys, manages the staging buffers, and synchronizes — cycles and latency on every message. Measured small-message costs are brutal: OSU/MVAPICH2 GTC material reports ~20 µs of overhead for a 1-byte cudaMemcpy-staged operation, versus single-digit microseconds once direct paths are used — because the *fixed* cost of two kernel-mediated copies and their synchronization dwarfs the payload.
4. **Serialization at the slowest stage.** The end-to-end time is the *sum* of stage times (GPU→host, host→NIC, network, NIC→host, host→GPU); effective bandwidth is the *minimum* over stages (DIST-5.4/5.5). If PCIe delivers 32 GB/s and the fabric 50 GB/s, the staged path runs at roughly 1/(1/32 + 1/50)⁻¹-style sums per hop — in practice, well under the network's rate, and the NIC idles while copies run.

**One subtlety: pageable versus pinned staging buffers.** Host memory a NIC can DMA into must be *pinned* (page-locked) and registered. Buffers allocated with plain `malloc`/`cudaMalloc`-side `cudaMemcpy` to pageable memory force the CUDA runtime to stage *again* through an internal pinned buffer — a hidden extra copy. `cudaMallocHost` (or `cudaHostRegister`) allocates pinned memory directly and removes it. This is why "use pinned buffers" is the first law of GPU communication performance, before any exotic technique.

**Why it was ever built this way.** Not incompetence — architecture. A NIC's DMA engine addresses *host* memory (what it can register and reach through the root complex). GPU memory sits behind a PCIe endpoint that, historically, exposed no addressable window to other devices' DMA engines. The staging path is the composition of two separately-correct mechanisms (GPU↔host copy; NIC↔host DMA). The history of GPU communication since ~2010 is the history of deleting pieces of this path: GPUDirect 1.0 (CUDA 3.1) let GPUs and NICs share one pinned host region, deleting the CPU's inter-region copy; GPUDirect P2P (CUDA 4.0, with unified virtual addressing) let GPUs on the same root complex copy directly over PCIe; GPUDirect RDMA (CUDA 5.0) exposed GPU memory through the PCIe BAR so NICs can DMA it directly — the fast path whose prerequisites (registration, topology, drivers) occupy Modules 6–7.

**Measurement preview.** The taxonomy is not nostalgia: a 2026 multi-GPU FDTD study (Section 2.3) finds that switching a stencil halo exchange from host-staged to direct GPU-to-GPU peer exchange — deleting exactly the path above — yields a **2.46–2.76× speedup**, the dominant optimization in the entire study, while clever halo-width tricks yielded little. When Module 7 reaches GPUDirect RDMA, that is the size of the prize on the table.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 NVIDIA's MRC: multipathing an RDMA connection, then open-sourcing it

**What happened.** On May 6, 2026, NVIDIA released **Multipath Reliable Connection (MRC)** — an RDMA transport protocol proven first on Spectrum-X Ethernet in production clusters at OpenAI, Microsoft, and Oracle — as an open specification through the Open Compute Project, co-developed with AMD, Broadcom, Intel, Microsoft, and OpenAI.

**What technically changed.** Standard RoCEv2 binds a reliable-connection queue pair to a single network path (per-flow ECMP hashing); one congested or failed link stalls or breaks every connection hashed onto it, and in synchronized collective traffic — thousands of GPUs all-reducing at once — a single straggler path throttles the job. MRC decouples the RDMA connection from path assignment: a single connection sprays traffic across many (hundreds of) paths simultaneously, balances load dynamically, retransmits precisely rather than stalling the flow, and detects and bypasses failed paths **in hardware at microsecond speed**. It runs natively on ConnectX SuperNICs and Spectrum-X switches at 800 Gb/s, alongside Spectrum-X Adaptive RDMA as a per-workload choice.

**Why it matters.** Endpoint-driven multipathing is a structural answer to the path-collision problem (DIST-5.6's fourth cause): instead of the switch fabric trying to balance flows it cannot see, the endpoints — which know what a connection is — spread it themselves. And unlike Ultra Ethernet (covered October 6), which replaces the transport wholesale, MRC *extends* the existing RoCE RC semantics, which is why it could drop into running Blackwell-generation training clusters.

**Connection to today's concepts.** MRC is a direct attack on contention-as-path-collision (5.6) and on effective-versus-nominal bisection utilization (5.8): a fabric's worst cut only throttles you if traffic is pinned to a few of the paths crossing it. Watch how it complements, rather than competes with, UET's packet spraying — same diagnosis, different layer of the stack.

### 2.2 Frontier versus Summit: what a tapered dragonfly actually costs

**What happened.** OLCF researchers published the first production-scale evaluation of a **dragonfly network on an exascale system** — Frontier (Slingshot, tapered dragonfly) — against the previous generation's **non-blocking fat-tree** on Summit (EDR InfiniBand), using synthetic microbenchmarks, mini-apps, and full applications.

**What was measured and found.** The topology economics: the dragonfly's reduced switch, cable, and optical-cable counts make it **~30% cheaper** than an equivalent fat-tree — about 3% of a full exascale system's cost. The performance bill for that saving: Frontier's global links are tapered to **57%** of local injection bandwidth, and the fabric runs adaptive routing with a **5% bias away from minimal paths** to pre-spread global-link load; measured all-to-all bandwidth reaches roughly **30 GB/s of the ~100 GB/s** per-node injection. The impact is real but concentrated: applications with global communication — all-to-all-heavy, FFT-based algorithms — and even some nearest-neighbor codes sensitive to *performance variability* pay measurably; the paper's overall conclusion is that the trade-offs are "not as broad as initially thought" but fall exactly where topology theory predicts.

**Why it matters.** This is DIST-5.7 and 5.8 resolved into deployed hardware: a diameter-3 network with a deliberately bought-down bisection, measured against a full-bisection fat-tree on real leadership workloads. The 30%-cost / 57%-taper / 30-of-100-GB/s triad is now the reference point for every "why doesn't my cluster's all-to-all run at line rate" investigation — sometimes the answer is not a bug, it is a purchase decision.

**Connection to today's concepts.** The 5% non-minimal bias is Section 1.2's "spend hops to avoid contention" in production configuration; the taper is Section 1.3's cost–bisection trade-off with a measured price tag; and the workload-dependence of the damage is Section 1.4's pattern taxonomy (surface-like vs global) deciding who pays.

### 2.3 A 2026 multi-GPU FDTD study: the host-staging tax, measured

**What happened.** A 2026 arXiv study (Obieke, *Communication Strategy Selection for Multi-GPU 3D FDTD with CPML*) built a CUDA 3D finite-difference time-domain solver with convolutional perfectly matched layers and systematically compared communication strategies across GPUs: decomposition layouts, **host-staged versus direct GPU-to-GPU peer exchange**, and enlarged ghost regions.

**What was demonstrated.** Direct GPU-to-GPU peer exchange was **the dominant optimization, 2.46–2.76× faster than host staging** — deleting the two-extra-PCIe-crossings path of Section 1.5 was worth more than every other communication decision combined. Enlarged ghost regions (temporal blocking, DIST-5.9's countermeasure) gave **only modest** additional benefit, because the reduced exchange frequency is partly offset by redundant ghost-zone computation and extra memory traffic. Strong scaling itself topped out around 1.51× on two GPUs — the surface-to-volume and fixed-cost effects of Section 1.4 visible in the small-p regime, where they bite hardest.

**Why it matters and what to watch.** It is a clean, modern replication of the Module-6 thesis with numbers attached: the *path* your data takes (staged vs direct) can matter more than the *algorithm* of your exchanges. As the curriculum moves into GPU-aware MPI and GPUDirect RDMA (Modules 6–7), keep this paper's ratio in mind — it is the size of the lever.

- Gran et al., [Exploring the Scope of the InfiniBand Congestion Control Mechanism (IEEE)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6267917) — congestion trees, credit-based flow control, FECN/BECN loop, rate- versus window-based control rationale.
- [First experiences with InfiniBand congestion control in hardware (Simula)](https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/Simula.nd.417.pdf) — CC parameter behavior, the parking-lot fairness problem.
- [A Measurement Study of Congestion in an InfiniBand Network (TMA 2017)](https://dl.ifip.org/index.html/db/conf/tma/tma2017/tma2017_paper18.pdf) — the Yellowstone study: 23 days of port counters, ~60% of ToR-uplink 100-ms intervals credit-stalled, PortXmitWait/FITF methodology.
- [Implementation of PFC and RCM for RoCEv2 Simulation in OMNeT++ (arXiv 1509.03559)](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.03559) — credit unit = 64 bytes; RoCEv2's PFC contrast.
- Zhang et al., [RGBCC: A New Congestion Control Mechanism for InfiniBand (IEEE)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7445318) — root/victim definitions; parameter-coupling problems; a self-tuning alternative.

**Topology, hop count, bisection**

- Kim, Dally, Scott, Abts, [Technology-Driven, Highly-Scalable Dragonfly Topology (ISCA 2008)](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/34926.pdf) — the dragonfly paper: groups as virtual routers, diameter three, balanced a=2p=2h, cost vs flattened butterfly/Clos.
- Sem-Jacobsen et al., [Dragonfly vs fat-tree comparison (HPCAC 2013 slides)](https://www.bsc.es/sites/default/files/public/mare_nostrum/2013hpcac-07.pdf) — CBB ratios, the ~75% traffic-crossing-bisection crossing point, practical utilization figures.
- Jain et al., [Analyzing Cost-Performance Tradeoffs of HPC Network Topologies (OSTI)](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1597618) — iso-node/router/link comparisons across fat-tree, dragonfly, express mesh; scaling equations.
- Bhatele et al., [Maximizing Throughput on a Dragonfly Network (ICS 2014)](https://charm.cs.illinois.edu/newPapers/14-04/paper.pdf) — routing strategies and job placement on a Cascade-style dragonfly.
- [An Evaluation of the Effect of Network Cost Optimization for Leadership Class Supercomputers (OLCF, OSTI 2538101)](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2538101) — the Frontier-vs-Summit study (Section 2.2): 57% taper, 5% non-minimal bias, ~30 GB/s all-to-all, ~30% cost saving.

**Scaling laws and surface-to-volume**

- [HPC Wiki: Benchmarking & Scaling Tutorial](https://hpc-wiki.info/hpc/Benchmarking_&_Scaling_Tutorial/Introduction) — Amdahl and Gustafson derivations with overhead terms.
- Gustafson, [Reevaluating Amdahl's Law (CACM 1988)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/42411.42415) — the original scaled-speedup argument.
- [Rice CSCADS 2009: surface-to-volume cost formulas](http://cscads.rice.edu/granlibakern-2009.pdf) — 1D/2D/3D halo-surface costs as functions of p.
- Shafi et al., [Node-Aware Stencil Communication for Heterogeneous Supercomputers (IEEE)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9150372) — surface-to-volume minimization, placement, and strategy selection for GPU stencil halo exchange.

**Host staging and the GPU communication path**

- [The Landscape of GPU-Centric Communication (arXiv 2409.09874)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09874v2) — pageable-vs-pinned staging, the GPUDirect 1.0 → P2P → RDMA history, BAR1 exposure, GPU-aware MPI lineage.
- Kandalla et al., [GPU multicast designs (GTC 2016 slides, OSU nowlab)](https://nowlab.cse.ohio-state.edu/static/media/talks/slide/dk-gtc16-mcast_gdr.pdf) — host-staged multicast; ~20 µs for 1-byte cudaMemcpy-staged operations vs single-digit-µs direct schemes.
- [NVIDIA GPUDirect Storage Design Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/gpudirect-storage/pdf/design-guide.pdf) — the bounce-buffer framing of staged paths (two copies vs one) and BAR1 as the DMA aperture.
- Obieke, [Communication Strategy Selection for Multi-GPU 3D FDTD with CPML (arXiv 2606.06910)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06910) — Section 2.3's study: 2.46–2.76× direct-peer over host-staged exchange; enlarged-ghost trade-offs.

**Developments**

- NVIDIA, [Spectrum-X with MRC announcement](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/spectrum-x-ethernet-mrc) and the [OCP MRC 1.0 specification](https://www.opencompute.org/documents/ocp-mrc-1-0-pdf); OpenAI's engineering note: [MRC supercomputer networking](https://openai.com/index/mrc-supercomputer-networking).

### Further Reading

- **Kim et al. ISCA 2008 (above) — 45 min.** The single paper that explains why modern leadership networks are shaped the way they are; read for the virtual-router idea, not just the results.
- **HPC Wiki scaling tutorial (above) — 20 min.** Work through the Amdahl/Gustafson derivations with a specific halo-exchange code in mind.
- **arXiv 2409.09874, The Landscape of GPU-Centric Communication — deep dive.** The best single map of every GPU communication mechanism this curriculum's Modules 6–10 will cover; Sections 2–4 are this module's material.
- **OLCF Frontier-vs-Summit paper (above) — 60 min.** The methodology (synthetic patterns → mini-apps → full apps on two production machines) is a masterclass in how to evaluate a topology decision honestly.
- **Hands-on: run `osu_allreduce` and `osu_alltoall` at two sizes on any cluster you can reach, and estimate the fabric's effective bisection from the large-message all-to-all rate — then check whether the answer matches the machine's advertised topology.**
