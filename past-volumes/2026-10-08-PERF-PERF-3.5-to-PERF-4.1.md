# 0. Concise Summary

Today's volume closes Module 3 (Variability and Reproducibility) and opens Module 4 (Performance Limits), and the five topics turn out to be one lesson. A performance number is never just a value — it is a tuple: **(value, uncertainty, environment, record, reference)**. *How many measurements* (PERF-3.5) sets the uncertainty; *environmental control* (PERF-3.6) shrinks it; *the experiment record* (PERF-3.7) preserves everything a future reader needs to re-interpret the number; *the baseline* (PERF-3.8) fixes what the number is compared against; and *the theoretical peak* (PERF-4.1) is the one reference no code can ever beat. These are coupled, not independent: controlling the environment reduces the coefficient of variation, and required repetitions scale with CoV² — halving noise quarters your measurement budget.

The key technical connection is that **the theoretical peak — the anchor of every "X% of peak" claim — is itself a stack of conventions**: which functional units you count (vector pipes or matrix engines), which clock you assume (base or boost, CPU spec or GPU spec), and which operations you count as FLOPs. Working El Capitan's published Rpeak backwards reproduces it to within 0.02% from AMD's *vector* FP64 rate (61.3 TF per MI300A) plus the CPU at 32 FLOP/cycle — but the same chip also publishes a 122.6 TF FP64 *matrix* rate, and against that denominator the very same HPL run's "efficiency" halves from 64% to ~32%.

Three developments anchor this. **El Capitan's remeasurement** from 1.742 to 1.809 exaflop/s (November 2025, holding through June 2026) is a baseline story at machine scale: +2.7% more APUs plus ~1.1% genuine tuning, with the TOP500 entry doubling as the experiment record that makes the delta auditable. **ReFrame 4.10.3** (August 27, 2026) shows records and baselines being industrialized into continuous, automated regression testing at HPC centers. And the **one-chip-many-peaks** arithmetic above shows why "state your denominator" is the first law of performance reporting.

Why this matters: every headline number in HPC — TOP500 scores, vendor speedup claims, your own "the optimization worked" — is only as good as these five disciplines underneath it.

# 1. Learn the Concepts

## 1.1 How many measurements? (PERF-3.5)

Last volume ended with the algebra; today it becomes an experiment-design decision. If you want the 99% confidence interval of your reported mean to be within 5% of the mean itself, you need

$$N \;\ge\; \left(\frac{z_{99}\,\cdot\,\text{CoV}}{0.05}\right)^2 \;=\; \left(\frac{2.576\,\cdot\,\text{CoV}}{0.05}\right)^2 \;=\; (51.5\,\cdot\,\text{CoV})^2,$$

where CoV is the coefficient of variation of a single run.

**A correction to last week's arithmetic.** The 2026-10-01 volume stated "At CoV = 1%, that's N ≈ 27; at CoV = 10%, N ≈ 2,658." Both pairings are off by a factor of ten in CoV. The correct mapping is:

| Single-run CoV | Required N (99% CI within 5%) |
|---|---|
| 1% | 1 (a single run already satisfies the criterion) |
| 5% | 7 |
| 10% | 27 |
| 20% | 107 |
| 30% | 239 |
| 50% | 664 |
| 100% | ≈2,655 |

So N ≈ 27 corresponds to CoV = 10%, and N ≈ 2,655 to CoV = 100%. The qualitative conclusion from last week not only survives but strengthens: with a well-controlled environment (CoV ≤ 5%), single-digit repetitions suffice; with a noisy one, the required count explodes past any reasonable budget. (Caveat: at N=1 you cannot compute a confidence interval at all — "CoV ≤ 1% → N=1" means *if you have external evidence the measurement is near-deterministic*, one run meets the precision criterion, though you would still run three to confirm that premise.)

**Sequential stopping beats fixed N.** The formula has a chicken-and-egg problem: you need CoV to compute N, but you need data to estimate CoV. Hoefler and Belli's protocol resolves this by making the rule *operational* rather than a priori: "We collected measurements until the 99% confidence interval was within 5% of our reported means." You measure a few runs, compute the CI, and continue if it is still too wide. For non-normal data (and timing data usually is — skewed with a right tail), the same loop works with a bootstrap CI around the median instead of the normal-theory CI around the mean.

**The cost model is what makes this interesting.** On the four-GPU HPL testbed we have used since the first PERF volume, a 9-second run makes 30 repetitions a five-minute affair — you should simply do them. At the other extreme, a full-machine HPL run on El Capitan occupies 45,000 MI300A APUs for hours; a national lab's measurement campaign is budgeted in node-hours and machine availability windows, and N is 1 to 3 *by policy, not by statistics*. This is why TOP500 submissions are single verified runs, not means of thirty — and why, at that scale, the disciplines of today's other four topics (environmental control, records, baselines) have to substitute for repetition statistics. You cannot average your way out of a noisy exascale run; you have to make the single run trustworthy.

**Repetition design details that matter:**

- **Independent launches vs. in-run loops.** Timing the same kernel 1000 times inside one process (the OSU Micro-Benchmarks pattern, with its `-x` warm-up and `-i` measurement iterations) captures steady-state kernel variance cheaply — but it *misses* variance that lives between process launches: daemon interference, allocation placement, cold caches. Whichever structure you choose decides which part of the noise distribution you are sampling.
- **Warm-up runs are not repetitions.** Runs discarded for warm-up (JIT compilation, cache population — PERF-2.3/2.4) do not count toward N; N counts steady-state samples only.
- **Interleave, don't block.** When comparing variant A against baseline B, run A/B/A/B rather than AAAA/BBBB. Slow drifts — thermal buildup, clock decay over a long session, filesystem cache growth — then affect both arms equally instead of masquerading as a difference between them. This is elementary experimental design (counterbalancing), and it is exactly the error a naive benchmark script makes.

The takeaway: N is not a constant of nature. It is set by the CoV your environment produces (next section) and by how much the claim matters. A 2% tuning effect claimed from single runs in a 10%-CoV environment is not a result; it is noise with a narrative attached.

## 1.2 Environmental control (PERF-3.6)

Last week's position was: variance you cannot remove, you must report. Today's is the complementary half: **much of the variance is environmental, and removable**. The controllable levers, with the mechanism each one neutralizes:

| Lever | Mechanism it removes | Typical tool |
|---|---|---|
| CPU frequency governor | DVFS ramping between idle/boost | `cpupower frequency-set -g performance` |
| CPU turbo/boost | clock excursions above base (invalidates peak arithmetic — §1.5) | disable boost in BIOS / `cpupower` |
| GPU clock locking | driver-boosted, thermally/power-throttled clocks | `nvidia-smi -lgc` / `rocm-smi --setperfdeterminism` |
| Thermal warm-up | cold-start clocks, fan ramp | run until temperatures plateau |
| NUMA placement | remote-socket memory latency/bandwidth | `numactl --cpunodebind --membind` |
| Background daemons | ASCI-Q-style periodic disturbance | exclusive node allocation |
| Shared resources | other tenants' network/filesystem traffic | exclusive job / quiet partition |
| Page cache state | cold vs. warm file I/O | pre-read input files; report which |

**GPU clock locking deserves the detail, because it is where vendor guidance is clearest.** By default, GPU clocks float: idle-low when no kernel is resident, boost under load, throttle when power or temperature limits bind. NVIDIA's benchmarking guidance (GTC 2019, "Best Practices when Benchmarking CUDA Applications") is explicit: unrestricted clock fluctuation makes results unstable; lock to the *default application clocks* with `nvidia-smi -ac` (or `-lgc` on Volta+, with persistence mode enabled so the setting survives idle). Their worked example: a kernel averaging 5.05 ms with a **relative standard deviation of 0.39%** after locking. NVIDIA's TensorRT performance documentation adds the subtleties: floating clocks add nondeterminism not just to timing but to *tactic selection* (the kernel-choice heuristic itself becomes a random variable); power throttling plus gaps between inferences inflates measured throughput (the GPU idles between requests, draws less average power, boosts higher, and reports numbers the fully-loaded production workload will never hit — which is why `trtexec` is designed to leave no gaps); and thermal throttling engages around 85 °C on most GPUs, visible as a clock drop in `nvidia-smi dmon` while temperature climbs.

**The representativeness tension.** Locked clocks give stability but lower average performance — the TensorRT docs state flatly that there is "no definite recommendation" on whether to lock, because it depends on whether you want *deterministic and stable* or *best average* numbers. The clean resolution is to name your mode. In **characterization mode** (isolating a mechanism: "is this kernel memory-bound?"), lock everything — you are measuring the kernel, not the power management. In **production mode** (what will users actually experience), let clocks float and accept the variance — reporting it per §3.1–3.4 of last week. Mixing the two modes in one comparison is how papers end up claiming improvements that evaporate in production.

**The coupling back to §1.1 is the punchline.** N scales as CoV². Halving your single-run CoV — say from 20% to 10% by locking clocks, warming up, and grabbing exclusive nodes — cuts the required repetitions from 106 to 27, a 4× reduction in measurement budget. Environmental control is not hygiene; it is the highest-leverage term in the repetition-cost equation. That is also why last week's "reduce the environment's variance first, then measure" is not just a slogan but arithmetic.

**What you cannot control at scale.** On a leadership machine, some of the environment is observational, not experimental: DVFS responding to building-wide power draw, other jobs' interconnect traffic on shared links, RAS events, cooling-system behavior. Note that El Capitan's 29,684.62 kW in the TOP500 record is a *measured* quantity (power measurement level 2), not a spec — the machine's power state during a run is part of the measurement context. At machine scale you control what you can, measure what you cannot, and record both.

(Placement and affinity — which node, which NUMA domain, which GPU — are themselves environmental controls, but they are deep enough to deserve Module 15 later in this theme.)

## 1.3 Reproducible experiment records (PERF-3.7)

A performance number without its context decays into anecdote. Six months after the fact, "we got 74.1 TF/s" is unanswerable: which compiler? which BLAS? locked clocks? which nodes? how many runs, and what spread? PERF-3.7 is the discipline of making the answer automatic by recording it at measurement time — not reconstructing it from memory later, when reconstruction is guesswork.

A minimal record that makes any HPC measurement re-interpretable:

```text
date/time + timezone:        2026-10-08 14:02 +08:00
hardware:                    4× MI300A node (Slingshot-11), nodes n017–n020,
                             exclusive allocation, topology snapshot attached
software:                    ROCm 6.4.2, rocBLAS 6.4.2, Cray MPI, gcc 8.5.0 / hipcc 6.4.2
environment variables:       OMP_NUM_THREADS, OMP_PROC_BIND, ... (full dump)
launch command:              exact srun/mpirun line, including binding flags
input:                       HPL.dat contents + hash (N, NB, P×Q)
clock/governor state:        governor=performance, boost off, GPU determinism mode
result:                      74.1 TF/s = mean of 10 steady-state runs
                             (σ, min, max, raw per-run timings attached)
```

The last line matters as much as the rest: store the **raw per-run data**, not only the summary. The summary is a claim; the raw data is the evidence, and it is what lets you recompute a different statistic later (a median, a bootstrap CI) without re-running anything.

Three levels of institutionalization are worth knowing:

1. **Ad hoc:** the block above, kept in a version-controlled directory next to the scripts that produced it. Git history then *is* the experiment log — every number traceable to a commit.
2. **Framework:** ReFrame, which turns tests into code and runs them through a managed pipeline (build → submit → sanity check → performance check), storing results in a database for later comparison — covered as today's second development.
3. **Public record:** TOP500's per-system pages. Look at what the El Capitan entry (system 180307) considers necessary to publish alongside the headline number: operating system (TOSS), compilers (g++ 8.5.0 and hipcc 6.4.2), math library (AMD rocBLAS 6.4.2), MPI (HPE Cray MPI), Nmax (24,768,000), core count, interconnect, and measured power with its measurement level. That is a *leaderboard* deciding, correctly, that a bare Rmax is meaningless without the software stack that produced it. Note what is still missing even there: the HPL.dat block sizes and process grid, the node allocation, the run date. The gap between a leaderboard record and a full experiment record is exactly the gap ReFrame-style frameworks close for your own work.

The record is also what makes baselines legitimate — which is the next topic.

## 1.4 Baselines (PERF-3.8)

A baseline is the fixed comparison point that converts "it is fast" into "it is 1.12× faster than X, ±u." Without one, no optimization claim is even well-formed. The taxonomy of baselines you will use in practice:

- **Previous self** — the last version of your own code. The regression baseline; what "did I make it better?" means.
- **Reference implementation** — a naive or standardized version (triple-loop GEMM vs. blocked BLAS). Establishes how much headroom optimization has captured.
- **Vendor library** — cuBLAS/rocBLAS/oneMKL. The "why not just call the library?" baseline.
- **Best-known result** — the record: a published number, a leaderboard entry, a prior campaign's best.
- **Theoretical peak** — the reference no code can beat; Module 4's subject, starting today.

Four validity conditions make a baseline defensible:

1. **Same problem and same validity check.** The baseline must solve the identical problem and pass the identical correctness test. A baseline that skipped the residual check is not a baseline; it is a rumor.
2. **Same environment — or re-measured in the new one.** The classic error is copying last month's baseline number into today's comparison. The software stack changed since then (new driver, new MPI, retuned node), and stack-dependence is real (Module 25 formalizes it). The baseline must be *re-measured* under the current environment, using the same measurement protocol and the same summary statistic — comparing your median against its mean is a subtle, common fraud.
3. **Known spread.** Last week's rule: a baseline without a variance estimate cannot support any later claim of improvement. The comparison statistic inherits uncertainty from *both* arms.
4. **Non-overlapping intervals, or no claim.** Hoefler–Belli Rule 7: if the confidence intervals of the two arms overlap, you have not demonstrated a difference, whatever your intuition says.

A machine-scale worked example — one that also exercises §1.3 and §1.5 — is El Capitan's remeasurement on the TOP500:

| | Nov 2024 / Jun 2025 entry | Nov 2025 / Jun 2026 entry | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cores (228 CU + 24 CPU per APU) | 11,039,616 | 11,340,000 | +2.72% |
| Implied MI300A APUs | 43,808 | 45,000 | +2.72% |
| Rmax (PFlop/s) | 1,742.00 | 1,809.00 | **+3.85%** |
| Rpeak (PFlop/s) | 2,746.38 | 2,821.10 | +2.72% |
| HPL efficiency (Rmax/Rpeak) | 63.4% | 64.1% | +0.7 pt |
| Measured power (kW) | 29,580.98 | 29,684.62 | +0.35% |
| Efficiency per watt (GF/W) | 58.9 | 60.9 | +3.5% |

The decomposition is the lesson. Of the +3.85% Rmax gain, +2.72% is simply *more hardware* (43,808 → 45,000 APUs; the Rpeak moved identically), and only the remaining ~1.1% is software/tuning — visible as the efficiency bump from 63.4% to 64.1%. If you had compared only the two Rmax numbers and attributed the whole 3.85% to tuning, you would have overclaimed by 3.5×. Every "we improved X%" claim should be auditable exactly this way: separate the hardware delta from the software delta, and check the ratio-to-peak moved. Note also what the TOP500 record enables here: because each entry lists cores, software stack, and power, this decomposition can be done by anyone, a year later, from public data. That is PERF-3.7 making PERF-3.8 possible.

A distinction worth keeping sharp: the **baseline** is the comparison point; the **control** is the environment held constant between the two arms. "Same compiler, same clocks, same nodes, only the block size changed" — that sentence is a controlled experiment. "We upgraded the driver and the block size" is two experiments braided into one, and proves nothing about either.

## 1.5 Theoretical peak (PERF-4.1)

Module 4 opens with the reference limit. The theoretical peak of a machine is the throughput it would sustain if every relevant functional unit executed its maximal operation on every cycle, forever, at the nominal clock:

$$R_{\text{peak}} \;=\; \sum_{\text{units}} (\text{count}) \times (\text{clock}) \times (\text{FLOPs per cycle per unit}).$$

Two conventions hide inside that formula, and both matter more than the arithmetic. First, **FMA counts as two FLOPs** — a fused multiply-add does a·b+c in one instruction but is counted as two operations, so peak assumes every FMA slot is filled with a genuine multiply-add every cycle. Second, **which clock**: TOP500 states that Rpeak values "are calculated using the advertised clock rate of the CPU" — the base clock, not turbo. A machine spending part of its run at boost clocks invalidates its own denominator; a machine power-throttling below base never reaches it. This is one more reason §1.2's clock discipline exists: your *measurements* should be interpretable against the same clock your *peak* assumes.

The deeper convention is **which functional units count** — and the best way to see it is to assemble a real machine's Rpeak from its parts.

**El Capitan.** Its TOP500 core count (11,340,000) is consistent with 45,000 MI300A APUs, each contributing 252 listed "cores" (228 compute units + 24 Zen 4 CPU cores). AMD's published MI300A specification gives the FP64 rates: **61.3 TF/s vector** and **122.6 TF/s matrix** — two peaks for the *same silicon at the same precision*, because the CDNA 3 chip has both conventional vector FP64 pipelines and matrix cores with double the FP64 rate. Assemble the machine with the vector number plus the CPU at 32 FLOP/cycle:

| Component | Count | Per-unit peak | Subtotal |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPU FP64 (vector convention) | 45,000 APUs | 61.3 TF/s | 2,758.5 PF/s |
| CPU FP64 (Zen 4, 32 FLOP/cycle @ 1.8 GHz) | 1,080,000 cores | 57.6 GF/s | 62.2 PF/s |
| **Total** | | | **2,820.7 PF/s** |

The published Rpeak is 2,821.10 PF/s — the reconstruction lands within 0.02% (the residue is clock rounding). So the published number counts the *vector* FP64 rate, and the GPU side owns 97.8% of the machine's peak. El Capitan's HPL efficiency is therefore 1,809.00/2,821.10 = **64.1%**. But swap in AMD's matrix FP64 rate — 122.6 TF per APU, which is what the matrix cores can actually sustain, and what rocBLAS-class DGEMM kernels on CDNA are built to target — and the same machine's peak becomes 5,579 PF/s, making the very same run **32.4% efficient**. Same hardware, same run, same benchmark: two defensible peaks, a factor-of-two difference in the headline ratio. The lesson is not that one convention is wrong; it is that *"X% of peak" is never a raw fact — it is (measurement, convention), and the convention must be stated.* (This deepens the efficiency discussion from the 2026-09-17 volume, which used the 64.1% figure; today we ask where its denominator came from, and find it is a choice.)

**The CPU-side convention is just as constructed.** The 32 FLOP/cycle per Zen 4 core implied by El Capitan's Rpeak is the "2× 512-bit FMA" counting convention. But Zen 4 physically implements AVX-512 on **256-bit datapaths**: per AMD's Hot Chips 35 presentation, most 512-bit operations execute over two consecutive cycles ("double-pumped"), which is precisely what makes the implementation power-efficient — and power, not instruction issue, is what limits sustained clock rates. AMD's own slide quantifies the payoff: using AVX-512 instead of AVX2 yields a **+17% effective frequency gain for HPL** on power-limited systems, because wider instructions mean ~50% fewer instructions to fetch, decode, and track for the same FLOPs, leaving power headroom to spend on clocks. Peak FLOP/cycle and achievable clock are not independent knobs; the convention stack treats them as if they were.

**LineShine makes the same point from the CPU-only direction.** Its Rpeak back-computes exactly: 13,789,440 cores × 1.55 GHz × **128 FLOP/cycle per core** = 2,735.8 PF/s, matching the published 2,735.82. A 128-FLOP/cycle core is a very wide vector engine (64 FP64 FMAs per cycle), and the whole machine is nothing but these cores — which is why its HPL efficiency, 2,198.40/2,735.82 = **80.3%**, sits near the top of the historical band: a CPU's FP64 peak is set by vector pipes that a dense, regular benchmark like HPL can genuinely keep full, week after week, while an accelerator's peak is set by specialized units that only well-tuned phases of well-tuned code approach (El Capitan 64.1%, Aurora ~51%). We noted this pattern on 2026-09-17; today you can *derive* both denominators yourself.

**Why peak is unreachable by construction:** it assumes every FMA slot issued every cycle; zero memory traffic (no loads, no stores, no cache misses); zero communication; zero branches; nominal clocks forever. Real HPL pays for panel factorization, broadcasts, DVFS, and imperfect GEMM shapes — hence the 60–80% band, not 95%. The peak is not a target; it is a *ruler* — a fixed, conventionally-defined ceiling that makes machines and optimizations comparable.

One forward-looking extension: **each precision has its own peak.** The MI300A ladder is 61.3 TF (FP64 vector) → 122.6 (FP64 matrix / FP32) → 490.3 (TF32 matrix) → 980.6 (FP16) → 1,960 (FP8) — each rung roughly a factor of two — five rungs spanning a factor of 32 from FP64 vector to FP8. El Capitan's HPL-MxP result of 16.7 exaflop/s should be read against the FP16 rung: 45,000 × 980.6 TF = 44.1 exaflop/s, so the mixed-precision run achieves ~38% of *that* peak. Same machine, third ruler. Module 4 continues next week with sustained peak (PERF-4.2) and application efficiency (PERF-4.3), which formalize which ruler to use when; Module 5's Roofline then adds the second axis — bandwidth — that pure FLOP/s peaks ignore.

# 2. Key Developments / News

## 2.1 El Capitan remeasured: 1.742 → 1.809 exaflop/s — a baseline story at exascale

**What happened.** At SC25 (November 2025), LLNL's El Capitan was remeasured on HPL at 1.809 exaflop/s, up from its debut 1.742 exaflop/s (November 2024), and completed the "triple crown" — No. 1 on HPL (1.809 EF/s), HPCG (17.41 PF/s), and HPL-MxP (16.7 EF/s). The numbers held through the June 2026 list, where El Capitan sits at No. 2 behind LineShine's debut 2.198 EF/s.

**What technically changed.** The table in §1.4 tells it: core count rose 2.72% (consistent with 43,808 → 45,000 MI300A APUs), Rpeak rose identically, and Rmax rose 3.85% — so roughly 2.7 points of the gain are hardware and ~1.1 points are software/tuning, visible as HPL efficiency moving from 63.4% to 64.1% and efficiency-per-watt from 58.9 to 60.9 GF/W at nearly constant power.

**Why it matters.** This is today's whole curriculum in one public event. The *previous submission* functioned as the baseline (PERF-3.8); the TOP500 entry — compilers, rocBLAS version, MPI, OS, Nmax, measured power (PERF-3.7) — is what makes the decomposition auditable by anyone, months later; the run is a *single verified measurement* at machine scale, where the repetition statistics of PERF-3.5 are unaffordable and environment/record discipline substitutes for them; and the efficiency ratios are meaningful only against the Rpeak conventions dissected in §1.5. A ~1% efficiency improvement on this machine is ~28 PF/s — an entire mid-size cluster — won not by new silicon but by the unglamorous work of re-tuning against your own baseline.

**What to watch.** The next remeasurement cycle (November 2026): whether the MI300A generation's efficiency ceiling sits near 65% or has more headroom; and whether the U.S. systems respond to LineShine's 2.198 EF/s on the November 2026 list.

## 2.2 ReFrame 4.10.x (2026): records and baselines as continuous infrastructure

**What happened.** ReFrame — the open-source framework for writing and running portable regression tests and benchmarks for HPC systems — released 4.10.3 on August 27, 2026, the latest in a steady 2026 cadence (4.9.1 January 30, 4.9.2 March 5, 4.9.3 April 16, 4.10.0 May 28, 4.10.1 June 25, 4.10.2 July 24, 4.10.3 August 27).

**What technically changed.** ReFrame tests are Python classes that describe *what* to test; the framework's pipeline handles *how*: programming-environment switching, compilation, job submission to the batch system, output parsing, sanity checking, and **performance assessment against per-system reference values** — baselines with tolerance bands, stored and compared automatically, with results written to a database "allowing for later inspection, basic analytics and performance comparisons" (the analytics direction was the subject of a FOSDEM 2025 talk). The 4.x line added configuration-file chaining (system definitions distributed across files) and reworked performance logging.

**Why it matters.** This is PERF-3.7 and PERF-3.8 industrialized. A performance check in ReFrame is literally today's topics compiled into executable form: same environment (the system configuration is versioned code), same protocol (the pipeline), compare against a stored reference with a threshold (the baseline with a tolerance), keep the result rows (the record). Centers like CSCS run their production systems this way — a nightly regression suite is what catches "the new driver made the fabric 8% slower" *before* users file tickets, and the result database is what turns "it feels slower lately" into a queryable time series. The reason a framework needs to exist at all is §1.1's economics: rigorous multi-point measurement against baselines is too tedious to do by hand every night — so it gets automated, exactly as the OSU Micro-Benchmarks unified launcher (covered 2026-10-01) automated multi-point microbenchmarking.

**What to watch.** CI-for-machines becoming standard practice at mid-size centers, not just leadership facilities; and the ReFrame Test Library (`hpctestlib`) accumulating community-maintained microbenchmark and application checks — the beginnings of a shared, executable baseline corpus.

## 2.3 One chip, several peaks: what El Capitan's Rpeak actually counts

**What happened.** Nothing new — which is the point. This development is a design fact worth internalizing: AMD publishes **two FP64 peaks for the same MI300A silicon** (61.3 TF/s vector, 122.6 TF/s matrix), and TOP500's Rpeak convention for El Capitan counts the vector one, as §1.5's reconstruction showed (within 0.02%). The matrix convention would double the machine's peak to ~5.58 exaflop/s and halve its apparent HPL efficiency.

**Why it matters.** Because the choice is not obviously wrong either way. rocBLAS — version 6.4.2 is named in El Capitan's TOP500 entry — ships DGEMM kernels that target CDNA's matrix units, so a GEMM-dominated benchmark is arguably better measured against the matrix rate; but the vector convention keeps CPU-only machines (Fugaku, LineShine) comparable on one scale. There is no universally right answer, only *declared* answers. The same construction sits underneath every peak: Zen 4's 32-FLOP/cycle counting convention versus its physically 256-bit datapaths (Hot Chips 35), where the +17% effective-frequency HPL result shows peak FLOP/cycle and sustainable clock trading against each other; LineShine's implied 128 FLOP/cycle per core; TOP500's advertised-base-clock rule, which boost clocks silently violate. And the precision ladder multiplies the choices: El Capitan's HPL-MxP 16.7 EF/s is 9.2× its HPL score but only ~38% of its FP16 peak (my computation), while LineShine's 7.92 EF/s is 3.6× HPL — the field's smallest ratio, because CPU-only designs have little low-precision headroom to unlock — and SoftBank's CHIE-4 posted the largest, 24.4×.

**What to watch/understand next.** Treat every "X% of peak" and every "Y× faster" as a pair (measurement, convention) and ask three questions: which units, which clock, which operations counted? Next week's topics — sustained peak (PERF-4.2) and application efficiency (PERF-4.3) — formalize the denominators; the FP8 rung (1.96 PF per MI300A) is where the AI-performance conversation now lives.
