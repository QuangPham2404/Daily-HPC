## 0. Concise Summary

The last DIST issue (2026-11-03) covered the fabric's building blocks: switches, InfiniBand, Ethernet alternatives, topology, and adaptive routing. Today we finish the network-hardware module with three concepts that decide what a deployed fabric actually delivers — **oversubscription** (DIST-8.7), **congestion** (DIST-8.8), and **network offload** (DIST-8.9) — and then cross into the software that sits on top of it, asking **why GPU collectives need a specialized library at all** (DIST-9.1) and how **NCCL's programming model** (DIST-9.2) is built.

The chain is causal. Nobody can afford a non-blocking network at scale, so real fabrics are deliberately oversubscribed: some set of links carries less capacity than the endpoints attached to them could demand. When offered load crosses those links faster than they drain, queues build and the fabric enters congestion — hot spots, head-of-line blocking, and in credit-based lossless fabrics, backpressure that spreads congestion even to traffic that shares nothing with the original victims. One structural cure is to remove the traffic itself: network offload moves collective operations into the switch, and SHARP-style in-network reduction shrinks data as it flows toward the aggregation root, attacking latency, bandwidth, and congestion simultaneously. The same idea reappears inside the node as NVSwitch reduction. With the fabric understood, the natural question is what software should drive it for GPUs: MPI was designed around host-resident data and host-driven progress, while GPU collectives want device-resident data, NVLink/NVSwitch paths, stream semantics, and per-message algorithm selection — which is exactly the niche NCCL occupies with a deliberately tiny API.

The two developments this issue sit at the two ends of that chain. **NCCL 2.28→2.32** (Device API, symmetric memory, GPU-Initiated Networking groundwork) is dissolving the "host launches a kernel per collective" assumption inside the NCCL programming model itself. And **SHARP's trajectory** — from the 2016 SwitchIB-2 paper to SHARPv4 in Quantum-X800 switches and NCCL-level integration through HPC-X — shows in-network computing graduating from an MPI accelerator to a first-class part of the GPU communication stack.

Why this matters: on a real cluster, the gap between the brochure bandwidth and what your all-reduce actually achieves is explained almost entirely by today's three network topics, and the library you call determines which of the hardware's escape hatches (rings, trees, NVLS, SHARP) get used.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Oversubscription (DIST-8.7)

**Problem.** A fully non-blocking network is one in which every endpoint can simultaneously inject at line rate toward any other endpoint — formally, a network whose bisection bandwidth equals the aggregate bandwidth of the smaller half of the machine. The problem is that bisection bandwidth is bought with ports, and ports cost money, power, and (worst of all) optics. A 10,000-node system at 400 Gb/s per node needs 4 Pb/s of bisection capacity; providing it with a folded-Clos (fat-tree) fabric consumes a large multiple of the endpoint ports in switch ports alone. So real deployments make an economic decision: **provision less network than the endpoints could demand**, and rely on the fact that not all traffic crosses the same links at the same time.

**Definition.** A fabric is *oversubscribed by ratio k* when the aggregate bandwidth of the endpoints below some cut exceeds the capacity crossing that cut by a factor k. The canonical case is the uplink group of a leaf (top-of-rack) switch: 32 nodes at 400 Gb/s each hanging off a leaf with only 16×400 Gb/s of uplinks is a 2:1 oversubscription at that tier. If all 32 nodes push toward the spine simultaneously, each gets at most 200 Gb/s — exactly line rate divided by k.

**Concrete arithmetic with a real switch.** The NVIDIA Quantum-X800 Q3400 is a 144-port, 800 Gb/s InfiniBand switch — 115.2 Tb/s of switching capacity, the first generation using 200 Gb/s-per-lane SerDes. NVIDIA's user manual states that its radix supports a **two-level fat tree connecting up to 10,368 NICs**. Decompose that number: 10,368 endpoints = 144 leaf switches × 72 NICs each; each leaf uses its other 72 ports as uplinks; 10,368 uplinks / 144 ports per spine = 72 spine switches. A 1:1 (non-blocking) two-level tree for ~10k endpoints costs 216 of these switches — and that is the *cheapest* full-bisection option, because a two-level topology is only possible while a single switch can hold all the uplinks of the leaf tier. Double the machine and you need three levels, more optics, and disproportionately more cost. This is why "fat tree" is named as it is: links get *fatter* (more parallel links) as you go up, to keep every tier non-blocking, and why the moment an operator thins those upper tiers, the fabric becomes oversubscribed.

**What oversubscription does to a collective.** Consider a ring all-reduce of S bytes over N GPUs (the algorithm NCCL leans on for bandwidth; we will build it properly in DIST-9.3). Each GPU sends and receives 2(N−1)/N·S bytes, and the ring — if its order is mapped contiguously onto the topology — crosses the bisection exactly twice per channel. A single, well-mapped collective on an otherwise empty oversubscribed fabric can therefore still run at near line rate; the ring was *designed* (Patarasuk & Yuan, 2009) to spread traffic perfectly evenly across links, which is precisely the property that makes it robust here. Oversubscription bites in three situations:

1. **Aggregate demand.** Many jobs, or many concurrent collectives, whose traffic sums to more than the oversubscribed cut can carry. Then per-flow throughput degrades toward 1/k of line rate and queues build — which is the entry point to congestion (§1.2).
2. **Concentrated patterns.** Rooted trees (reduce-to-root, broadcast-from-root), gather/all-to-all phases, and parameter-server traffic concentrate on the links near the root regardless of how fat the middle of the fabric is. These patterns can saturate a *non-blocking* fabric's hot links.
3. **Bad placement.** If communicating partners interleave across the cut (e.g., ranks alternate between two halves of the machine), traffic that could have stayed local crosses the bisection many times. This is why schedulers and launchers work hard on **job locality**: packing a job's ranks into as small a set of leaf switches as possible keeps its traffic below the oversubscribed tier. NVIDIA's own description of the 10,368-NIC fat tree advertises "optimal job locality" as a design goal, not an afterthought.

**System behavior and mitigations.** In production you see four responses. (a) *Scheduler locality* — batch systems place jobs to minimize cross-tier traffic. (b) *Multi-plane fabrics* — instead of one oversubscribed spine tier, build several independent non-blocking (or less-oversubscribed) planes, one NIC per GPU per plane, and keep each job's traffic inside one plane with rail-optimized placement (this returns in DIST-9.10). (c) *Hierarchical collectives* — reduce inside the NVLink domain first, so the network only ever sees the node-aggregated result; this divides cross-fabric demand by the GPUs per node. (d) *Accept it* — many HPC workloads (stencil codes with halo exchange, communication-avoiding algorithms) are local by nature, and the exercised bisection is far below provisioned capacity. The change of the last decade is that synchronous distributed training made "every GPU communicates with every other GPU every step" a mainstream workload, which is why AI-factory fabrics are built non-blocking or multi-plane at costs HPC centers historically refused.

**Trade-off in one line:** oversubscription trades worst-case cross-fabric bandwidth for switch count, optics, power, and dollars — and it is safe exactly to the extent that workload locality and scheduling keep the offered load below the cut's capacity.

### 1.2 Congestion (DIST-8.8)

**Problem.** Oversubscription is a *provisioning* statement; congestion is the *runtime* phenomenon that occurs whenever offered load exceeds link capacity at some point in the fabric — regardless of whether the fabric is oversubscribed. A lossless, credit-based fabric like InfiniBand (covered in DIST-8.3) cannot drop packets to shed load; it must buffer and backpressure. That choice creates the distinctive pathology of HPC fabrics: congestion does not stay where it starts.

**Mechanism 1: hot spots and queue buildup.** The simplest congestion is incast. Eight nodes each send a 1 MB message to node 0. Each sender transmits at 400 Gb/s (50 GB/s); node 0's single NIC can drain 400 Gb/s. Offered 400 GB/s into a 50 GB/s drain: queues at the switches feeding node 0 grow at ~350 GB/s. The 8 MB total drains in 8 MB ÷ 50 GB/s = 160 µs, so the last sender's message completes in ~160 µs rather than the 20 µs a lone 1 MB message would take — an 8× slowdown that is *entirely queueing delay*, not transmission. The switch buffers absorbed ~7 MB at the peak. Scale the fan-in to hundreds of GPUs (an all-gather toward one root, a straggler synchronization, a checkpoint to one target) and either the buffers are enormous or something upstream must slow the senders down.

**Mechanism 2: congestion spreading through backpressure.** InfiniBand's link-level flow control is credit-based *per virtual lane*: a switch port may only transmit on a VL if the downstream port has granted credits for that VL's buffers. When a hot output port's buffers fill, the switch stops returning credits upstream, and the *upstream* port's buffers fill too — with a mixture of packets destined for the congested port and packets destined elsewhere. A 2017 measurement study of a production InfiniBand cluster put it plainly: link-by-link flow control "causes the effects of a port's congestion to spread even to ports that have no shared flows with the congested port." The congestion tree grows downward from the hot spot, and victims are flows that merely share a queue with hot traffic.

**Mechanism 3: head-of-line (HoL) blocking.** The victim mechanism is HoL blocking, classically analyzed for InfiniBand fat trees by Gran et al.: consider three "hot" flows converging on one destination, sharing output queues with two "cold" flows headed elsewhere. Credit starvation from the hot destination stalls the shared queue; the cold flows — which did nothing wrong — are throttled to the same 33% of link rate as the hot ones. Input-buffered switches suffer the low-order variant (a blocked packet at the head of an input queue blocks everything behind it); shared output queues suffer the high-order variant. The architectural fixes are well understood: separate queues per destination or per class (virtual output queues in the switch), and in InfiniBand, map traffic classes to **service levels (SLs)** and SLs to **virtual lanes (VLs)** — since flow control is per-VL, separating hot and cold traffic into different VLs gives the cold traffic its own credits, so it cannot be starved by the hot.

```
hot flow ──▶ ┌──────────────┐
             │  shared VL   │──▶ congested port X (credits exhausted)
hot flow ──▶ │   queue      │
             │  ░░░blocked░░│      cold flow is stuck behind hot
cold flow ─▶ │  ██ready██   │──▶ free port Y  (HoL: cannot overtake)
             └──────────────┘
```

**Mechanism 4: InfiniBand's closed-loop congestion control (FECN/BECN).** The InfiniBand specification defines an explicit congestion notification loop. A switch detecting congestion on an output port (the detection criterion itself is vendor-defined) marks forward-traversing packets with a **FECN** bit, at a configurable *Marking_Rate*. The destination HCA, seeing FECNs on a flow, sets a **BECN** bit on packets returning toward the source. The source HCA then increases its inter-packet delay for that flow — throttling injection at the *sender*, before queues overflow. Vendor switches expose tuning knobs (a *Threshold* on buffer occupancy from 0=disabled to 15=most reactive, marking rates, timer scales), and the practical lesson from measurement studies is that the parameter space is delicate: too passive and congestion trees form; too aggressive and well-behaved flows oscillate. The `PortXmitCongTime` performance counter tells you how long a port has actually spent in the congested state — one of the first places to look when a cluster "goes slow."

**Connection to today's other topics.** Adaptive routing (DIST-8.6) attacks congestion by *spreading* traffic over multiple paths — but it cannot create bandwidth on a truly saturated cut (§1.1). Oversubscription determines whether congestion is a transient burst or a permanent condition. And the strongest structural attack on congestion is to make the bytes disappear before they reach the hot spot — which is precisely what in-network reduction (§1.3) does.

**Where an HPC engineer meets this.** Symptoms: an all-reduce whose measured time is 3× the model from DIST-5.x on a busy machine but on-time on an empty one; a job whose performance collapses when a neighbor job lands on the same leaf switches; `ibquerystate`/telemetry showing PortXmitCongTime climbing on one spine port. The diagnosis path (formalized later in DIST-16) is always: separate latency, bandwidth, and interference — then find the hot spot.

### 1.3 Network offload (DIST-8.9)

**Problem.** A collective operation like all-reduce, executed on hosts, costs three things: the data traverses the network multiple times (up a reduction tree and back down), the endpoints spend processor cycles doing the arithmetic and the copies, and the traffic pattern feeds the congestion pathologies of §1.2. All three costs share one root cause — the network is treated as a *dumb pipe*, and every byte must survive all the way to an endpoint before anything is computed with it.

**Core mechanism: in-network reduction (SHARP).** The Scalable Hierarchical Aggregation and Reduction Protocol (SHARP) makes the switch an *aggregation node*. Switch ASICs (starting with Mellanox SwitchIB-2, 2016) contain arithmetic units in the data path. A set of switches is programmed to form an **aggregation tree**: participating endpoints send their data up the leaves; at each switch, the hardware ALU *reduces* packets from multiple children into one partial result; the fully reduced value emerges at the root and is distributed back down (multicast). The original SHArP paper (Graham et al., HotI 2016) reports a factor **2.1 latency improvement for an 8-byte MPI_Allreduce over 128 hosts (6.01 µs → 2.83 µs)**, and **3.24× for a 4 KB all-reduce (46.93 µs → 14.48 µs)** with pipelining — paper measurements on the hardware of that era, but the mechanism explains the gain: the data crosses the fabric *once*, being consumed as it goes, instead of making multiple endpoint-to-endpoint traversals.

```
            [root switch AN: +]  ← fully reduced result
             /            \
      [switch AN: +]   [switch AN: +]   ← partial sums formed in hardware
       /   \   \        /   \   \
     GPU0 GPU1 GPU2  GPU3 GPU4 GPU5    ← each endpoint sends its value ONCE
```

**Why this also cures congestion.** Reduction is *data-decreasing*: each aggregation node emits fewer bytes than it receives. The hottest links in a fat tree — near the root, where a software tree concentrates traffic — are exactly the links where SHARP's aggregation shrinks the flow. In-network reduction is therefore simultaneously a latency optimization, a bandwidth optimization, and a congestion mitigation. It also frees host (and GPU) cycles: the arithmetic that a ring all-reduce spends SMs on is done by switch ALUs that would otherwise be idle, and progress is made by the network itself rather than by scheduled endpoint resources — the SHArP paper notes this makes collectives less exposed to system noise and skew (with the caveat that a skewed collective loses most of the benefit no matter where it is reduced).

**What it costs and where it is limited.** Nothing is free. The switch needs: reduction ALUs (limited data types and operations — integer and floating-point sums dominate); buffer space to hold partial results per active aggregation group (Quantum supports up to 126 aggregation trees per subnet, Quantum-2 up to 1023, per NVIDIA's SHARP release notes); and a management plane — an **Aggregation Manager** (part of UFM) computes and programs the trees into the switches before applications run. There are two operating modes: *low-latency* trees for small messages and *streaming aggregation* for large ones, with per-switch parallelism limits (on Quantum-2, one active streaming tree per switch port). The benefit is largest for small and medium messages — exactly the sizes where a software all-reduce is dominated by latency — and diminishes for bandwidth-saturating large messages. Modern generations scaled the hardware a long way: NVIDIA's Quantum-X800 platform (introduced at GTC 2024) carries what HPE's specification sheets describe as **fourth-generation SHARP**, with NVIDIA claiming 5× bandwidth capacity and 9× the in-network computing throughput (14.4 TFLOPS) of the previous generation — vendor claims, but the direction is consistent with the generational scaling of switch SerDes and ALU counts.

**How software reaches it.** Applications do not talk to switches; stacks do. In the MPI world, HPC-X's collective components (HCOLL, and since HPC-X 2.50 the UCC framework, now the default for Open MPI collectives) map MPI_Allreduce onto SHARP groups when the fabric and message size justify it. On the GPU side, NCCL can route all-reduce through SHARP via its network plugins — NVIDIA ships an NCCL RDMA/SHARP plugin, and the HPC-X 2.26 release added SHARP in-network aggregation support for NCCL on Quantum switches, including an optional overlap of the reduce-scatter and all-gather phases of all-reduce. The pattern to notice: the *same* offload hardware is being surfaced through every collective API in the stack, because the win is in the fabric, not in any one programming model.

**The same idea inside the node.** In-network reduction is not only a network trick: the third-generation NVSwitch (H100 generation) added multicast and SHARP-style reduction engines to the *intra-node* switch fabric, which NCCL exposes as the NVLS algorithm. NVIDIA's specifications for the 8-GPU H100 HGX platform list 3.6 TB/s of all-to-all bisection bandwidth but 450 GB/s *for reduction operations* — a hint that in-switch reduction, while sharing the SHARP lineage, has its own throughput envelope. The "network as compute component" idea has now scaled from a 36-port SwitchIB-2 to a 72-GPU NVL72 rack: in an idealized 16 GB per-GPU all-reduce on such a rack, ring all-reduce must move 2·(71/72)·16 GB ≈ 31.6 GB per GPU, while in-switch reduction moves each GPU's data essentially once (~16 GB) — nearly a 2× reduction in bytes moved, before counting the elimination of SM-side reduction work. (These are bandwidth-floor calculations from link rates, not measurements; real collectives achieve a fraction of link rate.)

**Trade-off in one line:** collective offload trades switch silicon (ALUs, buffers, management complexity) for fewer network traversals, lower endpoint CPU/GPU cost, and structurally less congestion — and it is worth exactly as much as your workload uses the collective types it accelerates (reduce, all-reduce, barrier) at the sizes where it wins.

### 1.4 Why GPU collectives need specialized libraries (DIST-9.1)

**Problem.** By 2014–2016 the pieces were all individually solved: MPI ran on clusters, GPUs were attached to hosts, GPU-aware MPI (DIST-6.3–6.4) could move device buffers, and Patarasuk & Yuan had shown ring all-reduce to be bandwidth-optimal. What was missing was a library whose *entire design center* is the multi-GPU machine. Four structural mismatches make a specialized library necessary rather than merely nice:

**1. The data lives on the device, and the fast paths to it are GPU-specific.** Between two GPUs in one node, the best path is a direct NVLink peer-to-peer load/store or CUDA IPC mapping — no kernel copies, no host. Across NVSwitch, the best path may be a *multicast* write that the switch fans out (or reduces) in hardware. Across nodes, it is GPUDirect RDMA straight from device memory (DIST-7.5). A library must discover the NVLink/PCIe graph (nvidia-smi topo -m output), classify each pair (NV, PIX, PXB, NODE, SYS), and pick the path per pair. MPI *can* do this — CUDA-aware MPI implementations do — but every MPI's coverage of NVSwitch multicast, copy-engine offload, and multi-rail NICs differs, and none of it is required by the standard.

**2. The collective must execute as a GPU program, in stream order.** A CUDA application is a DAG of kernels on streams. A collective inserted into that DAG must itself be enqueued on a stream, run as device code (or copy-engine work) in the same order, and complete without the host spinning. MPI's progress model — host threads driving protocol state machines — is architecturally wrong for this: every MPI collective would either burn a CPU core per GPU or force a host round-trip per operation. Deep learning training issues *thousands* of collectives per second (one all-reduce per gradient bucket, per step, per process); a per-collective host round-trip at even 5–10 µs of overhead is catastrophic at that frequency. NCCL's answer is that the collective *is* a kernel launch: no host involvement between enqueue and completion.

**3. Algorithm and protocol selection must be automatic and size-dependent.** The same all-reduce wants a latency-optimized tree for 64 KB and a bandwidth-saturated ring (or NVLS, or CollNet/SHARP) for 256 MB — with message-protocol variants (Simple vs. LL vs. LL128) layered on top. Recent measurement work on an H100 NVSwitch host illustrates the stakes: ring all-reduce peaked near 366 GB/s bus bandwidth while NVLS reached ~376 GB/s at 8 GB, and the LL protocol traded away more than half the bandwidth for low small-message latency. A library that benchmarks nothing and hardcodes one algorithm leaves most of the machine's capability unused. (The algorithm/protocol matrix itself — Ring, Tree, CollNet, NVLS × Simple, LL, LL128 — is documented in NCCL's design and analyzed in the "Demystifying NCCL" paper.)

**4. The consumers are frameworks, not humans.** PyTorch's DistributedDataParallel, TensorFlow, DeepSpeed, and Horovod all need a small, embeddable C/C++ library with a handful of calls, no daemon, no scheduler integration, and no assumptions about who owns the process layout. That is a different contract than MPI's full communication substrate (point-to-point, one-sided, I/O, dynamic processes...).

**The ecosystem answer.** NCCL (NVIDIA) is the dominant implementation; AMD's RCCL provides the same API and semantics on ROCm platforms, and Intel's oneCCL plays the analogous role for its GPUs — evidence that the *programming model* (§1.5) is the durable contribution, independent of any vendor's silicon. Note also the convergence from the other side: MPI stacks increasingly delegate GPU collectives (UCC's design explicitly spans MPI, NCCL, and RCCL backends), so the specialized library is becoming the shared substrate under everyone's API.

**What to remember:** MPI is a *general* communication substrate with GPU support added; NCCL is a *collective engine* built from the GPU outward — device-resident execution, topology-aware paths, stream semantics, and automatic algorithm choice. Neither replaces the other (a point DIST-9.11 develops).

### 1.5 The NCCL programming model (DIST-9.2)

**The API surface is deliberately tiny.** The entire model is: *communicators, collective calls, streams, and groups*.

**Communicators.** An `ncclComm_t` names a fixed set of processes (each with one GPU) that will communicate. Bootstrap is out-of-band: rank 0 generates an `ncclUniqueId` (`ncclGetUniqueId`), every rank broadcasts it through any available side channel (MPI, a file, sockets — NCCL does not care), and everyone calls `ncclCommInitRank`. Initialization is itself collective: internally it performs a topology survey (CUDA devices, NVLink/PCIe adjacency, NICs and rails), negotiates rings/trees/channels, and establishes the transports (P2P mappings, IPC handles, RDMA memory registrations). This is also why `ncclCommInitRank` synchronizes across ranks — calling it for several communicators from one thread requires wrapping the calls in `ncclGroupStart()`/`ncclGroupEnd()`, which turns a batch of blocking calls into one synchronized batch. Derived communicators exist (`ncclCommSplit` with MPI_Comm_split-style color/key semantics) for the sub-group patterns that data- and model-parallel training generates.

```c
// One process, one GPU, classic NCCL pattern (per-rank):
ncclUniqueId id;
if (rank == 0) ncclGetUniqueId(&id);
MPI_Bcast(&id, sizeof(id), MPI_BYTE, 0, mpi_comm);   // any side channel works

ncclCommInitRank(&comm, nranks, id, rank);
cudaSetDevice(local_gpu);

ncclAllReduce(sendbuff, recvbuff, count, ncclFloat, ncclSum,
              comm, stream);            // enqueued, asynchronous
```

**Collective operations.** Five classics define the model: `ncclAllReduce`, `ncclBroadcast`, `ncclReduce`, `ncclAllGather`, `ncclReduceScatter`. Their composition identities are worth internalizing because NCCL's algorithms exploit them: **Reduce + Broadcast = AllReduce**, and **ReduceScatter + AllGather = AllReduce** (the ring all-reduce of DIST-9.3 is exactly this composition, pipelined). Two semantic details trip up MPI users: `root` is a *rank in the communicator*, not a device number; and every rank must pass the same count, datatype, and operator — NCCL supports a fixed set of reduction ops (sum, max, min, and the bitwise/pointwise ops) and a fixed set of datatypes, not user-defined reductions. (NCCL 2.28 added host-side `ncclAlltoAll`, `ncclScatter`, and `ncclGather` — the set is growing, but the "fixed, hardware-friendly menu" philosophy is unchanged.)

**Stream semantics.** This is the load-bearing design decision. Every NCCL call takes a CUDA stream and behaves like any other stream operation: asynchronous with respect to the host, ordered with respect to other work on that stream, and observable through events and stream synchronization. No `ncclWait` — you wait on the *stream*. Correctness of a communication–computation overlap is expressed entirely in the stream DAG: enqueue the gradient-producing kernel, the all-reduce, and the consuming kernel on the same stream (or use events across streams), and the ordering is guaranteed without any host thread participating. This is what lets a training step express "compute gradients, reduce them, apply the update" as enqueued GPU work with zero host round-trips, and it is what makes NCCL operations capturable in CUDA graphs.

**Groups.** `ncclGroupStart()`/`ncclGroupEnd()` batch operations issued between them into a single collective step: multiple collectives across *multiple communicators* (e.g., an all-gather over the tensor-parallel group and an all-reduce over the data-parallel group, issued back-to-back) become one coordinated operation — necessary both for correctness of simultaneous init and for efficient execution of the multi-group collectives that modern parallelism (DP × TP × PP) generates on every step.

**What happens under the hood at call time.** NCCL's tuner selects algorithm (Ring/Tree/CollNet/NVLS) and protocol (Simple/LL/LL128 — plus symmetric-memory kernels where buffers are registered) from message size, rank count, and detected topology; the data is chunked across multiple *channels* (independent rings/paths); and a device kernel — a set of CTAs (thread blocks) — executes the send/receive/reduce loop, using NVLink loads/stores intra-node and RDMA (or the network plugin, including SHARP-capable ones) inter-node. The CTA budget is itself a tunable resource: NCCL 2.28 *halved* the maximum CTA count on Blackwell (32→16) because SMs spent on communication are SMs taken from the application — a direct statement that collectives compete with compute for the GPU's own execution resources.

**Evolving edges of the model.** Three newer pieces matter for where this is going. (1) **User buffer registration / symmetric memory**: applications pre-register buffers (`ncclCommRegister`, `ncclCommWindowRegister`) so NCCL can use one-shot, zero-copy kernels — the symmetric-memory kernels reach lower latencies than the classic LL/Simple paths and were reimplemented on the device API in 2.28, extended in 2.29 (abort support) and 2.30 (AVG reduce-scatter). (2) **Copy-engine collectives**: for all-gather/all-to-all/scatter/gather within an NVL domain, NCCL 2.28 can execute on copy engines instead of SMs, removing the SM cost entirely. (3) **The Device API** (experimental): device-side communicators and callable communication primitives — the subject of today's first development.

**The model in one sentence:** NCCL asks the application for the minimum a collective needs — *who* (communicator), *what* (buffer, count, type, op), *where in the order* (stream) — and takes full responsibility for *how*, including topology discovery, algorithm selection, and execution on the device.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 NCCL 2.28→2.32: collectives you can call from inside a kernel

**What happened.** NCCL 2.28.3 (released October 6, 2025) introduced the experimental **Device API**: device-side communicators (`ncclDevCommCreate`) and in-kernel communication primitives, including LSA (load/store access) for P2P over NVLink, `Multimem` operations that use NVLink SHARP multicast/reduction hardware, and the first scaffolding for **GPU-Initiated Networking (GIN)** — GPUs launching network transfers without the host. The same release reimplemented symmetric-memory kernels on the device API, added host-side `ncclAlltoAll`/`ncclScatter`/`ncclGather`, added copy-engine collectives for NVL domains, and introduced an "Inspector" plugin that emits structured JSON performance traces of every NCCL operation. The 2.29–2.32 series has continued extending this surface (symmetric-memory abort support, AVG reduce-scatter on symmetric kernels, ...), with current documentation at 2.32.3.

**What technically changed.** The programming model's central assumption — the host enqueues a collective as a stream operation — is being relaxed. Communication becomes callable *from device code*, so a kernel can, mid-execution, pull the next tile from a peer GPU or push partial results, instead of ending the kernel and launching a separate collective. Symmetric memory is the enabling contract: when all participants' buffers are registered at matching windows, one-shot kernels (and device-side primitives) can act on remote memory directly.

**Why it matters and the connection to today's concepts.** This is DIST-9.2's programming model evolving under pressure from the exact costs we identified in DIST-9.1: per-collective kernel-launch overhead and the SM tax. It also continues NCCL's decade-long pattern of absorbing whatever the hardware offers (P2P, NVLink, NVLS, copy engines) behind a stable API. The caveat is explicit in NVIDIA's own notes: the Device API is experimental, with no ABI stability — applications must recompile per release, and adoption is via frameworks (PyTorch's symmetric-memory paths and fused kernels are the main consumers) rather than hand-written code.

**What to watch.** Whether GIN matures into a general GPU-initiated network path (which would blur the NCCL/NVSHMEM boundary — foreshadowing DIST-10.8's device-initiated communication), and whether the Device API stabilizes enough to become the primary way frameworks express overlap.

### 2.2 SHARP, from a 2016 paper to a built-in assumption of AI fabrics

**What happened.** In-network aggregation has gone from a research feature to a checkbox item across three generations of fabric. SHARP debuted in Mellanox's SwitchIB-2 ASIC (Graham et al., HotI 2016: 2.1× lower 8-byte all-reduce latency over 128 hosts). It scaled through Quantum (126 aggregation trees per subnet) and Quantum-2 (1023 trees, parallel streaming-aggregation trees), and the Quantum-X800 generation (GTC 2024) carries fourth-generation SHARP, with NVIDIA claiming 9× the in-network computing throughput of the previous generation (14.4 TFLOPS) alongside 800 Gb/s-per-port, 115.2 Tb/s switches. On the software side, the trail that used to be MPI-only has reached the GPU stack: HPC-X 2.26 added SHARP in-network aggregation for NCCL on Quantum switches — including optional reduce-scatter ↔ all-gather overlap — via the Spectrum-X/Quantum NCCL plugin, and UCC (the default collective framework in HPC-X since 2.50) routes MPI collectives to the same hardware.

**What technically changed.** Two things: capacity (switch SerDes went 25→100+ Gb/s per lane, so aggregation ALUs feed from 8–16× fatter pipes than in 2016) and integration (the same aggregation trees are now addressable from MPI *and* NCCL, with the fabric's Aggregation Manager/UFM handling tree construction as fabric state rather than per-application setup).

**Why it matters and the connection to today's concepts.** This is DIST-8.9's trajectory drawn in the real world — and it loops back through the whole network module: in-network reduction shrinks traffic at exactly the hot links where oversubscription (DIST-8.7) and congestion (DIST-8.8) bite, and the Quantum-X800 platform pairs SHARP with the adaptive routing and telemetry-based congestion control that manage what traffic remains. For HPL-MxP-style workloads and large training runs, in-network aggregation is now one of the algorithm choices NCCL can silently make for you — which is a good reason to know, from first principles, when it should win.

**What to watch.** The boundary between switch-based aggregation (SHARP over InfiniBand) and in-node NVSwitch reduction (NVLS) — and how multi-vendor answers develop, since in-network collectives are also an explicit goal of the Ultra Ethernet specification work covered in the 2026-11-03 issue.
