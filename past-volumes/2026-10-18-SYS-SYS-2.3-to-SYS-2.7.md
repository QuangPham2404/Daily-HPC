## 0. Concise Summary

Two Sundays ago we finished at the level of building blocks: cabinets, nodes, switches, storage, power, and the management plane that keeps 10,000 machines alive, ending with the homogeneous-versus-heterogeneous fork. Today we ask the questions that decide whether those blocks become a balanced machine or an expensive pile of parts: **how tightly should compute be coupled, how big should each unit of allocation be, how do the subsystems scale relative to each other, and how should the wires be arranged?**

Five concepts carry the issue:

1. **Scale-up versus scale-out (SYS-2.3).** Scale-up means enlarging one shared, tightly coupled domain — one OS image, one memory space, latency measured in nanoseconds-to-microseconds. Scale-out means replicating smaller domains and connecting them with a network of message passing. Modern accelerator clusters are explicitly two-tier: an NVLink-class fabric *inside* the domain (~1.8 TB/s per GPU on NVLink 5) and an InfiniBand/Ethernet fabric *between* domains (400–800 Gb/s per GPU) — a wire-speed gap of roughly 35–40×, which dictates where parallelism strategies must cut.
2. **Fat versus thin nodes (SYS-2.4).** A "fat" node packs many accelerators and much memory behind one network attachment; a "thin" node replicates modest units. Fugaku is the extreme thin design (158,976 single-CPU nodes); Frontier, El Capitan, and Alps are fat-node designs; an NVL72 rack is a fat *node* at rack scale. Fatness trades per-accelerator communication cost against allocation granularity, failure blast radius, and utilization.
3. **System balance (SYS-2.5).** Compute, memory bandwidth, network bandwidth, I/O, and power must scale together; per-node arithmetic intensity ceilings (FLOP/s ÷ bytes/s) and per-node communication ratios decide whether the machine you bought can run the workload you have. We will derive Fugaku's and Frontier's balance numbers from published specs and see why HPL saturates both while HPCG does not.
4. **Machine topology (SYS-2.6).** How the wires are arranged: direct networks (mesh/torus, dragonfly) versus indirect networks (fat-tree/Clos), judged by diameter, bisection bandwidth, radix, and cable/switch cost. Fugaku's Tofu 6D mesh/torus — 24×23×24×2×3×2 over ~200,000 cables — is today's specimen, contrasted with the fat-tree/Clos of JUPITER's quad-rail InfiniBand and with reconfigurable optical circuit switching in Google's TPU pods.
5. **The case-study method (SYS-2.7).** A fixed checklist — node, accelerator, memory, interconnect, storage, power, workloads — applied to a real machine until reading an announcement becomes mechanical. Today's worked example: CSCS **Alps** (No. 10, June 2026), a fat-node, multi-tenant Grace-Hopper system that doubles as a preview of "supercomputing as a set of software-defined clusters."

The developments section is unusually well matched to the curriculum. Google published an **IEEE Micro paper on five generations of TPU supercomputers** detailing Ironwood's 9,216-chip pods: a 3D torus of direct ICI links inside cubes of 64 chips, optical circuit switches between cubes, 42.5 exaflops FP8 and 1.77 PB of directly addressable HBM in one scale-up domain — the largest coherent-ish domain ever built, and a live demonstration of SYS-2.3's two-tier split with torus economics inside. **NVIDIA's NVLink Fusion** (announced May 2025) turns the scale-up domain itself into a licensable product — NVLink 6 at 3.6 TB/s per XPU, 72-XPU domains today with a roadmap to 1,152 endpoints — opening NVIDIA's rack fabric to third-party CPUs and XPUs. And the **UALink 1.0 specification** (April 2025, 85+ members) is the industry's open answer on exactly the scale-up axis: 200G per lane, up to 1,024 accelerators, load/store semantics. Two of the three developments are about who owns the boundary between scale-up and scale-out — which is precisely SYS-2.3 turned into an industry battleground.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.0 The three design questions

Every supercomputer announcement answers, explicitly or implicitly, three questions. *How tightly are the compute units coupled?* (SYS-2.3) *How large is each replicating unit?* (SYS-2.4) *And how do the subsystems — compute, memory, network, storage, power — scale relative to one another, and along what wiring?* (SYS-2.5, SYS-2.6). None of these has a universally correct answer; each is a trade-off resolved against the target workloads and the budget. SYS-2.7 then packages the questions into a repeatable method. We take them in order, and today's developments — TPU pods, NVLink Fusion, UALink — will exercise all of them.

### 1.1 Scale-up versus scale-out (SYS-2.3)

**Problem.** Parallel work needs its pieces to exchange data. The cheapest place to exchange data is the place with the most bandwidth and the least latency — and physics makes bandwidth and latency get dramatically worse as you move outward from a single die. So the first architectural decision is: how large do we make the region inside which communication is *cheap*, and how much of the machine lives outside it?

**The two pure strategies.** *Scale-up* (vertical scaling) enlarges a single domain: more cores, more memory, more accelerators behind one shared interconnect, typically one OS image or a small set of coherent OS domains. Communication is shared-memory access or hardware-coherent load/store — no protocol stack, no message copy into buffers, the network interface is a cache-coherent fabric. Its historical form is the symmetric multiprocessor (SMP); its modern forms are the Grace-Hopper superchip (CPU and GPU coherent over NVLink-C2C), the 8-GPU NVLink island in an HGX server, and the 72-GPU NVL72 rack. *Scale-out* (horizontal scaling) replicates smaller domains — nodes — and connects them through a network over which the only communication primitive is message passing: MPI over InfiniBand/RoCE/Ethernet, with protocol stacks, network interface cards, and latency measured in microseconds.

**The latency-and-bandwidth ladder.** The reason the split exists is a steep physical hierarchy, roughly (orders of magnitude, per published system documentation and vendor interconnect specs):

| Communication | Mechanism | Typical latency | Typical per-device bandwidth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Register→HBM load | on-package HBM | ~100s ns | 3.3–8 TB/s per GPU |
| CPU↔GPU, same package | NVLink-C2C | sub-µs | 0.5–1.8 TB/s |
| GPU↔GPU, same NVLink domain | NVLink/NVSwitch | ~1–few µs | 1.8 TB/s per GPU (NVLink 5) |
| Node↔node, same switch | RDMA over IB/ethernet | ~2–5 µs | 50–100 GB/s per NIC |
| Across a large fabric | multiple switch hops | ~5–20+ µs | same wire, shared |

The single most important number in the table is the ratio between rows three and four: NVLink 5 gives each GPU 1.8 TB/s of all-to-all bandwidth inside the domain, while a 400 Gb/s ConnectX-7 NIC gives it 50 GB/s outside — a factor of 36. NVLink 6 (3.6 TB/s per XPU) against an 800 Gb/s ConnectX-8 (100 GB/s) preserves the same ~36× ratio. This ratio is not an accident; it is set by copper reach, port economics, and power, and it means *any parallelization strategy that must cross the domain boundary pays roughly 35–40× less wire bandwidth per accelerator than one that stays inside it.*

**A concrete communication cost.** Synchronous data-parallel training ends every step with an all-reduce of the gradient tensor; the bandwidth-optimal ring all-reduce moves ~2·(N−1)/N × S bytes per GPU, effectively 2S. For S = 1 GB: inside a 72-GPU NVLink 5 domain, time ≈ 2 GB ÷ 1.8 TB/s ≈ **1.1 ms**. Span 72 eight-GPU nodes over 400G IB rails instead (≈50 GB/s effective per GPU), and the same all-reduce takes ≈ **40 ms** — thirty-six times longer, per step, forever. That factor is why model/tensor parallelism (communication-heavy, latency-sensitive) is packed *inside* the NVLink domain and data parallelism (communication-light, tolerates latency) spans *across* domains — the two-tier strategy you have already met from the application side in the DIST theme, here seen from the architecture side.

**Why not just scale up everything?** Three structural costs. *Cost scaling:* an all-to-all switched domain of N endpoints needs N×(N−1) unidirectional links and switch capacity that grows roughly quadratically before oversubscription; a torus or a fat-tree of smaller groups is dramatically cheaper per endpoint. *Fault domain:* everything in one scale-up domain typically reboots, reinitializes, and fails *together* — a 72-GPU rack is one serviceable unit with one firmware set, one cooling loop, one power envelope. *Static partitioning:* scale-up resources are usually bound at boot or job start; you cannot dynamically grow a coherent domain the way a scheduler adds nodes to a job. Scale-out inverts every one of these: incremental purchase, per-node failure, dynamic membership — at the price of the 36× wire ratio and message-passing semantics.

**What changed recently.** For decades the scale-up domain was one node (or one 4–8 GPU server) and everything above it was scale-out. The 2020s accelerator era inflated the scale-up domain through the whole rack: GB200/GB300 NVL72 makes 72 GPUs one NVLink domain with 130 TB/s of aggregate all-to-all bandwidth, connected by a passive copper backplane — copper's reach (~1 m class) literally sets the rack's size. The industry boundary between the tiers is now the most contested real estate in system design, which is exactly what today's three developments (Sections 2.1–2.3) are about.

### 1.2 Fat nodes versus thin nodes (SYS-2.4)

**Problem.** Given a fixed budget of FLOP/s, you can build a few large nodes or many small ones. "Fat" and "thin" describe how much compute, memory, and network attachment each *replicating unit* carries. This choice is independent of scale-up/scale-out in principle (you can have thin nodes in a scale-out cluster, or a fat node that is itself one scale-up domain), but in practice the two decisions are coupled: fatter nodes push more of the machine's communication into the cheap intra-node tier.

**The trade space.**

| Dimension | Fat nodes (e.g., 4×GH200, 8-GPU HGX, NVL72) | Thin nodes (e.g., Fugaku's 1×A64FX) |
|---|---|---|
| Network attachment per FLOP | amortized over many devices — cheap per FLOP | every node needs its own NIC/router ports |
| Intra-node communication | fast, shared-memory or NVLink; often hidden entirely | none — *all* communication is inter-node |
| Allocation granularity | coarse: whole nodes/racks per job | fine: nodes can be doled out in small counts |
| Utilization & fragmentation | a job needing 3 of 8 GPUs strands 5 unless GPUs are individually schedulable | small jobs pack tightly |
| Failure blast radius | one node failure kills many devices' worth of work | one node ≈ 1/N of the machine |
| Programming model pressure | hybrid: few ranks, many threads/devices per rank (MPI+X) | pure distributed: one rank per node, MPI-first |

Fugaku is the cleanest large-scale thin design ever built: 158,976 nodes of exactly one A64FX CPU (48 compute cores, 32 GiB HBM2) each, with the network router *integrated into the processor die*. There are no fat nodes to fragment, no intra-node hierarchy to program around — and, correspondingly, all ~163 TB/s of the machine's aggregate memory bandwidth sits behind explicit inter-node communication when data must move. Frontier, El Capitan, and Alps sit at the opposite end: four MI250X or four GH200 modules per node, so a large fraction of a typical job's communication never touches the cluster network at all. An NVL72 rack is the logical extreme — a fat *node* the size of a rack, with the caveat that schedulers still carve it into per-GPU or per-tray shares for smaller jobs.

**Two mechanisms worth internalizing.** First, *amortization*: a fat node concentrates network ports, cooling manifolds, baseboards, and BMCs per FLOP; this is a large part of why per-GPU cost falls in NVL-class systems even as the rack's absolute price explodes. Second, *the programming-model consequence*: on thin nodes, the natural decomposition is one MPI rank per node; on fat nodes, the natural decomposition is few ranks × many devices, which is why hybrid MPI+OpenMP and MPI+CUDA/HIP models (SYS-12.9, upcoming) exist at all. The node shape you are handed as a user is already an architectural opinion about how your parallelism should be organized.

**Where you meet it.** As a user, node fatness determines your job script: `#SBATCH --nodes=64` on Fugaku means 64 CPUs' worth of work; the same flag on an 8-GPU cluster means 512 GPUs. It determines when you must think about NUMA and GPU affinity inside the node, and it determines the *minimum* allocation a scheduler can hand you — which is next module's subject.

### 1.3 System balance (SYS-2.5)

**Problem.** A supercomputer is several engines bolted to one workload: compute (FLOP/s), memory bandwidth (bytes/s from DRAM/HBM), network bandwidth (bytes/s between nodes), storage I/O, and a power budget (watts) that caps all of them. If any one engine is undersized relative to the rest, the surplus engines idle. "Balance" is the discipline of sizing them against *each other* — and ultimately against the workloads the machine is bought for.

**The core arithmetic.** Define a machine's *balance ratio* for any two subsystems as their throughput ratio. The most quoted is compute-to-memory-bandwidth, in bytes per FLOP: a node with 3.072 TF/s FP64 (Fugaku's A64FX at 2 GHz) and 1,024 GB/s of HBM2 has 1024/3072 ≈ **0.33 B/F** — it can move a third of a byte per double-precision flop, so any kernel needing more than 0.33 B/F runs memory-bound at 1/(0.33·AI) of peak. Invert it: the *ridge point* — the arithmetic intensity at which compute and memory saturate simultaneously — is 3 FLOP/B. Frontier's node (4× MI250X ≈ 191.6 TF/s FP64, ≈12.8 TB/s HBM) has 12,800/191,600 ≈ **0.067 B/F**, a ridge point of ~15 FLOP/B: five times more compute-hungry a workload is needed to saturate it. Neither number is "better"; they are balance points chosen for different workload mixes. (You will meet this ratio formally as the Roofline model later in the PERF theme.)

Now widen to the network: Fugaku's node injects 40.8 GB/s into Tofu against 3.072 TF/s — **75 FLOP per network byte**. A dense LU factorization (HPL) communicates O(N²) words for O(N³) flops; at machine scale its per-node communication need is ~N²/N_p^(2/3)-ish words per node — vanishingly small per flop, so 75 FLOP/B is comfortable, and Fugaku sustains 82.3% of Rpeak on HPL. HPCG — sparse mat-vecs, triangular solves, halo exchanges — needs multiple bytes per flop *from memory*, and Fugaku's 0.33 B/F memory balance caps it: 16.0 PF on HPCG, ~3% of its HPL result. Same machine, two balance ratios, two completely different stories. **This is the practical skill: when you see a benchmark result, ask which balance ratio it exercised.**

**Why imbalance happens.** Compute scales with device area × frequency × precision width — and in the accelerator era it scales *fastest* (tensor cores multiply peak FLOP/s per mm² several-fold). Memory bandwidth scales with pin count and signaling rate — slowly. Network bandwidth per endpoint scales with serdes rate and lane count — also slowly, and is further capped by switch radix economics. So left alone, every generation drifts *more* compute-bound and *more* network-starved per FLOP; system designers counteract by widening memory (HBM stacks per package: 8 on MI250X and H100-class parts) and multiplying network attachment (four NICs per Frontier node; four rails per JUPITER node). Balance is not a state; it is a maintenance activity against silicon economics.

**Power is the zeroth balance constraint.** Every subsystem above draws from the same wall allocation. Alps' GH200 cabinets are deliberately *underpopulated* — 7 blades per chassis where 8 fit — because CSCS could deliver more power per GPU by having fewer of them; 434.9 PF at 7.12 MW is 61 GF/W in HPL terms. Balance against power sometimes means buying fewer, better-fed devices — a counterintuitive but common outcome (SYS-19/20 will formalize power and cooling).

### 1.4 Machine topology (SYS-2.6)

**Problem.** You have (say) 150,000 endpoints and a per-endpoint link budget. How do you arrange the wires and switches so that (a) nearby processes talk fast, (b) the traffic your workloads actually generate has enough aggregate bandwidth, and (c) the cable plant, switch count, and power stay within budget? This is topology — and unlike node design, it is visible in *every* collective operation your code runs.

**Two families.** *Indirect* networks put intelligence in dedicated switches: endpoints connect to a switching fabric, and any two endpoints communicate *through* it. The canonical example is the **fat-tree/Clos** network — a multi-stage fabric whose link counts (and hence bandwidth) grow toward the root, which is how you make the bisection (the bandwidth across a minimal cut splitting the machine in half) proportional to machine size. Fat-trees give uniform all-to-all bandwidth and arbitrary routing, at the price of large switch counts: a non-blocking 2-level Clos for N endpoints needs switch port radix that grows with √N and a cable count in the multiple of N. **JUPITER's Booster** uses quad-rail InfiniBand NDR200 (four 200 Gb/s NICs per node, ~800 Gb/s injection) in a fat-tree — the standard HPC answer when you want every rank to be able to talk to every other rank at full rate.

*Direct* networks attach each endpoint's router to its neighbors' routers directly — no separate switch tier. The **mesh/torus** family wires each node to 2·d neighbors in d dimensions (a torus wraps around; a mesh does not). **Dragonfly** (Frontier's Slingshot; dissected in the October 4 volume) groups nodes into all-to-all-connected groups and connects groups with long links to cut diameter and cable length. Direct networks trade *uniform* bandwidth for *cheap, local* bandwidth and short wires: excellent for stencil and nearest-neighbor physics, weaker for global shuffles.

**Today's specimen: Fugaku's Tofu Interconnect D.** Each A64FX integrates the router on-die: 10 ports × 2 lanes × 28 Gbps, feeding **six TNI RDMA engines per node at 6.8 GB/s each — 40.8 GB/s of injection bandwidth**, with 0.49–0.54 µs latency for 8-byte messages and 6.35 GB/s throughput at 1 MiB (RIKEN/Fujitsu published figures). The full machine is wired as a **6D mesh/torus of dimensions 24 × 23 × 24 × 2 × 3 × 2** — three scalable dimensions (X, Y, Z) matched to the physical packaging (racks, shelves, water-cooled CMU boards) and three fixed small dimensions (A, B, C) that track intra-board and intra-CMU structure. The cable plant is industrial: **about 200,000 Tofu cables totaling ~894 km** (roughly 97,632 fiber AOCs spanning ~855 km between racks plus 119,232 electrical cables inside racks). Two lessons generalize. First, *topology follows packaging*: Tofu's dimensions exist because 3D space forces locality, and the network is designed so that physical neighbors are network neighbors. Second, *tori buy wire economy at the price of bisection*: a d-dimensional torus of N nodes has bisection bandwidth ∝ N^((d−1)/d) — sublinear. Per-node bisection bandwidth *shrinks* as the machine grows. Torus machines therefore lean on topology-aware mapping (Fujitsu even provides virtual-3D-torus rank mapping utilities) so that applications with local communication never need the missing global bandwidth.

**Third family, newly prominent: the reconfigurable topology.** Google's TPU pods put a fixed 3D torus *inside* each rack-sized "cube" of chips, but connect cubes through **optical circuit switches (OCS)** — MEMS mirror arrays that physically steer light, reconfiguring the cube-to-cube wiring pattern in milliseconds under software control. This gives a mostly-direct network a scheduling dimension: the effective topology can be reshaped per job, and failed cubes are bypassed by re-steering circuits around them. OCS is the bridge concept between fixed topology (Tofu, fat-tree) and full packet switching, and Section 2.1 shows it at exascale-AI scale.

**Comparison at a glance:**

| Topology | Bisection scaling | Diameter | Switch hardware | Natural fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fat-tree / Clos | ∝ N (non-blocking) | O(log N) | many switch tiers, long cables | uniform all-to-all; HPC clusters |
| d-D mesh/torus | ∝ N^((d−1)/d) | O(N^(1/d)) | none (on-die routers), short cables | stencil/local physics; huge N |
| Dragonfly | ∝ N (groups) | O(3) hops typically | on-die routers + long optical links | mid-scale uniformity with short cables |
| Torus + OCS | reconfigurable | small (per configuration) | on-die + optical circuit switches | sliced multi-tenant AI pods |

Research continues to attack the fat-tree's cost: Hoefler and colleagues' **HammingMesh** (SC22) shows that a job whose communication is the product of two all-to-all dimensions — exactly the data-parallel × model-parallel shape of large training — can get full bisection bandwidth in its own slice from a much cheaper mesh-of-switches fabric plus a commodity global network. Topology design is not a solved problem; it is an ongoing negotiation between workload structure and wire cost.

### 1.5 The supercomputer case-study method (SYS-2.7)

The method is a fixed checklist applied to any announced system, in order: **(1) node architecture** — CPU, accelerator, memory, local interconnect; **(2) scale-out networking** — NICs, switches, topology, bandwidth; **(3) storage** — filesystems, tiers, capacity, bandwidth; **(4) power and cooling**; **(5) software environment**; **(6) target workloads**. The point of the order is that each answer constrains the next: node fatness sets what the network must carry; balance sets what storage must supply; power sets how much of everything fits.

**Worked example: Alps (CSCS, Switzerland — No. 10, June 2026, 434.9 PF Rmax / 574.84 PF Rpeak / 7.12 MW).**

1. *Node.* The main partition's node is fat by design: **four GH200 Grace-Hopper superchips per node** — each a 72-core Grace Arm CPU with 128 GB LPDDR5X coherent over NVLink-C2C with an H100-class GPU and 96 GB HBM3 — plus **four Slingshot NICs, one per module, 200 Gb/s each** (CSCS documentation). 2,688 such nodes = 10,752 modules. Older phases add CPU-only Rome nodes, A100, MI250X, and MI300A partitions — Alps is also heterogeneous *within* one Slingshot fabric.
2. *Network.* HPE Slingshot-11 with 800 Gb/s injection per node (4×200), the same interconnect family as Frontier/El Capitan. Per-module NIC pairing is the balance decision: ~200 Gb/s per GPU, i.e., roughly 25 GB/s per ~4 TF FP64 device — a communication-light ratio suited to AI training's sharded parallelism and HPC's data-parallel phases.
3. *Storage.* ~100 PB HDD Lustre scratch at ~1 TB/s plus 5+1 PB of SSD flash, tiered by HPE DMF; two 130 PB tape libraries for archive. Data lifecycle (SYS-8.8 preview) is a first-class design element.
4. *Power/cooling.* 100% direct liquid cooling; the GH200 cabinets are deliberately underpopulated (7 blades per 8-slot chassis) to raise per-GPU power within a fixed cabinet envelope — balance against power, decided in packaging.
5. *Software.* Here is the distinctive one: Alps is run as **"vClusters" — software-defined clusters** carved out of the physical machine (Daint.Alps for the user lab, Clariden for the Swiss AI Initiative, Säntis for climate), each with its own Slurm environment and software stack on shared hardware. The machine is simultaneously one fabric and several logical supercomputers — "fusion of HPC and cloud" in CSCS's own words.
6. *Workloads.* Kilometer-scale climate simulation (ICON/EXCLAIM), large-language-model training for the Swiss AI Initiative, and general academic HPC — which is exactly why the node shape (coherent fat nodes), the network ratio (moderate per-GPU), and the multi-tenant software design look the way they do.

Six checklist entries, and every one traces back to a concept from Sections 1.1–1.4. That traceability is the entire skill. Next Sunday's SYS-2.8 completes Module 2 by asking when *none* of this implies superiority: why benchmark leadership (Alps is 10th, JUPITER 5th, LineShine 1st) is a statement about workload-fit, not a ranking of engineering quality — and Alps' tenant-sliced design is already a hint: the "machine" a user experiences is a slice, and its balance is what matters to them.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 Google's Ironwood pods: 9,216 chips, one two-tier scale-up domain — documented in IEEE Micro

**What happened.** Google's Cloud blog and a paper to appear in the July/August 2026 issue of *IEEE Micro* ("five generations of TPU supercomputers," Hoefler et al. and the TPU team, arXiv 2606.15870) give the fullest public account yet of **Ironwood, the seventh-generation TPU**, and the supercomputer built from it: pods of **9,216 chips delivering 42.5 exaflops of FP8** with **1.77 PB of directly addressable HBM** — described as a record for shared-memory multi-CPU systems.

**What technically changed / was demonstrated.** Ironwood is a pure two-tier SYS-2.3 design executed at unprecedented scale. Each chip: two compute dies, 4,614 TF FP8, 192 GiB of HBM3E at 7.4 TB/s, and **six on-package ICI (Inter-Chip Interconnect) links at 100 GB/s** — 600 GB/s of injection per chip, integrated into the die with no extra switching silicon. The *tier-one* fabric is a **3D torus**: 4 chips per host, 64 chips per rack forming a "cube," every chip directly linked to its cube neighbors in all three dimensions. The *tier-two* fabric is not packet switching but **optical circuit switches**: cubes are wired together through OCS units that the fabric manager reconfigures — bypassing failed cubes, splicing in spares, and carving "slices" of arbitrary size for different jobs. Above pods, superpods connect through a conventional data-center network. The *IEEE Micro* table quantifies the torus economics: pod bisection bandwidth is 76.8 TB/s — which our arithmetic reads as ~768 × 100 GB/s cut links, exactly the cut set of a 24×24×16-style 3D torus — i.e., **~8.3 GB/s of bisection per chip against 600 GB/s of injection**. That ~1.4% bisection-to-injection ratio is the torus bargain (SYS-2.6) taken with both hands: enormous local bandwidth, minimal global shuffle bandwidth, made workable because sharded inference and 2D-parallel training move most data within slices — and because the OCS can reshape which chips are "local" per job. The paper also documents the resilience role of OCS and identifies the *CPU host* (2,304 hosts per superpod) as the primary availability weak point, and notes ICI's "push-only" DMA restriction — a deliberate simplification of the load/store model.

**Why it matters and what to watch.** Ironwood is the largest coherent-domain experiment ever run: it pushes the scale-up tier from NVL72's 72 devices to 9,216 and answers SYS-2.3's "why not scale up everything?" with topology + circuit switching instead of a monolithic switch. For HPC readers, the transferable lesson is the *pattern*: torus inside, circuit-switched reconfigurability between, packet network only at the top. Watch whether OCS-style reconfigurability migrates into scientific supercomputing, where jobs are currently bound to whatever topology the installer wired.

### 2.2 NVLink Fusion: NVIDIA turns the scale-up domain into a product

**What happened.** NVIDIA (May 2025 announcement, expanded since) opened its rack-scale scale-up fabric to third-party silicon: **NVLink Fusion** licenses NVLink and the MGX rack architecture so that hyperscalers can attach their **custom XPUs and CPUs** into NVIDIA's scale-up/scale-out platform — with Fujitsu and Qualcomm named as early CPU partners, and semi-custom XPU programs from major cloud builders following.

**What technically changed.** Per NVIDIA's current materials, NVLink 6 connects **72 XPUs all-to-all at 3.6 TB/s per XPU (260 TB/s per domain)** with SHARP in-network reduction, and the roadmap extends **domain sizes up to 1,152 endpoints**; NVLink-C2C provides coherent chip-to-chip links for custom chiplets; the MGX rack standardizes power, cooling, and management so XPU and GPU racks can share a datacenter design and be re-provisioned against each other as demand shifts.

**Why it matters.** Read through SYS-2.3, this is the scale-up/scale-out boundary becoming an *industry ecosystem boundary* rather than an engineering detail. Whoever owns the scale-up fabric owns the unit of compute — which is why NVIDIA is willing to license its crown-jewel interconnect (keeping the switch/controller silicon proprietary, as coverage emphasizes) rather than lose semi-custom designs to open fabrics. The 1,152-endpoint roadmap says the domain keeps growing; each doubling shifts more of a training run's communication from the 100 GB/s tier to the 3.6 TB/s tier. **Connection to today's curriculum:** the wire-ratio arithmetic of §1.1, the fault-domain cost of §1.1, and the fat-rack allocation granularity of §1.2 all now apply to *other vendors' chips* inside an NVIDIA-defined domain. Watch whether adopters actually mix XPUs and GPUs in one domain (heterogeneous scale-up) or only co-locate them in shared racks.

### 2.3 UALink 1.0: an open standard for exactly the scale-up tier

**What happened.** The UALink Consortium (board: Alibaba, AMD, Apple, Astera Labs, AWS, Cisco, Google, HPE, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Synopsys; 85+ members) ratified and published the **UALink 200G 1.0 Specification** on April 8, 2025 — the open industry answer to NVLink for the scale-up tier.

**What technically changed.** UALink 1.0 defines a switched, **load/store/atomic memory-semantic interconnect** for accelerators: **200 Gb/s per lane, x1/x2/x4 links** (an 800 Gb/s "station" of four lanes), pods of **up to 1,024 accelerators** addressed by 10-bit accelerator IDs, connected through UALink switches that can be partitioned into isolated "virtual pods." Two design decisions stand out against the alternatives: it provides *raw memory semantics* — reads, writes, atomics with claimed PCIe-switch-class latency — rather than message semantics (contrast Ultra Ethernet's UET, covered October 6, which is packet transport for the scale-out tier); and the spec explicitly *excludes* host CPUs from remote memory access — it is accelerator-to-accelerator only, keeping the coherence problem tractable.

**Why it matters.** Combined with Ultra Ethernet, the industry now has two open specifications that map one-to-one onto the two tiers of §1.1: UALink below the boundary, UEC above it — with NVLink Fusion occupying the same boundary from the proprietary side. AMD's Helios rack (covered September 21) is the first flagship system built on this split: 72 MI455X GPUs scale-up-linked over UALink-class fabric at 260 TB/s, Ultra Ethernet beyond. For HPC, an open scale-up standard means rack-scale coherent domains could appear at non-hyperscaler price points — national labs and university centers, not just cloud giants. Watch first silicon interoperability (multi-vendor accelerators in one UALink pod is the claim that matters) and whether a 1,024-endpoint open pod can match NVLink-class effective bandwidth in practice.
