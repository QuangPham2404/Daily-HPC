## 0. Concise Summary

Theme 1 studied one GPU: how it executes, why it is built for throughput. Today Theme 2 opens with the question that creates the entire field of distributed HPC: **what happens when one processor — or one node — is no longer enough?** Three walls force the answer. *Memory capacity*: a dense 2,000,000 × 2,000,000 double-precision matrix needs 32 TB just to exist, ~50× more than an entire 8-GPU H100 node. *Time-to-solution*: factoring that matrix is 5.3 × 10¹⁸ floating-point operations — 28 years on one core. *Power and economics*: one fast processor dissipates power far less efficiently than many slow ones, the same argument that ended the frequency race in 2005, now applied at system scale.

The industry's answer is the distributed-memory cluster: many complete computers, each with private memory, connected by a network. The price is that **communication becomes explicit**. In shared memory, one thread "sends" data to another simply by writing it; in distributed memory, if you didn't send it, they don't have it. This issue builds the complete mental scaffold for that world: the execution entities (OS processes, MPI ranks, threads, and the GPUs attached to them), the way large arrays and meshes are partitioned across those entities (block, cyclic, block-cyclic, and geometric domain decomposition with halo regions), and the performance accounting that decides whether distribution pays off — compute time shrinks as you add ranks, but communication time does not, a tension captured by the surface-to-volume ratio and by Amdahl's and Gustafson's laws.

Two developments anchor the lesson in 2026 practice. First, **MPICH 5.0.0** (released February 4, 2026) is the first stable MPI delivering the full MPI-5 standard including its new **Application Binary Interface** — the strongest evidence that the process/rank/message-passing model introduced in 1994 remains *the* abstraction layer of distributed-memory HPC. Second, a 2026 *International Journal of High Performance Computing Applications* paper on **GPU-aware halo exchange** shows the day's most practical lesson in miniature: how you partition a grid, pack its boundaries, and place ranks on the hardware can swing communication performance by up to two orders of magnitude — before any faster network is involved.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Why one processor is not enough (DIST-1.1)

Yesterday's lesson ended inside a single GPU. To see why that viewpoint is insufficient, put numbers on the problems HPC is actually asked to solve.

**Wall 1 — memory capacity.** A dense n × n matrix of 64-bit floats occupies 8n² bytes:

| n | Matrix size (8n²) |
|---|---|
| 10,000 | 0.8 GB |
| 100,000 | 80 GB |
| 500,000 | 2 TB |
| 2,000,000 | 32 TB |

A single H100 GPU carries 80 GB of HBM; a full 8-GPU HGX node provides 640 GB. A dense matrix of n = 2,000,000 — the scale at which modern HPL runs operate on leading systems — needs 32 TB *before* factorization workspace, before a second copy for communication staging, before any other array the algorithm touches. The problem does not fit in the machine. No amount of cleverness in a single address space changes this: the data must be spread across many nodes' memories, each node holding a piece.

**Wall 2 — time-to-solution.** Factoring that matrix (LU, the core of HPL) costs (2/3)n³ ≈ 5.3 × 10¹⁸ floating-point operations. A single CPU core sustaining, say, 6 GFlop/s would need 8.9 × 10⁸ seconds — about **28 years**. One 8-GPU H100 node, even granting ~0.5 PFlop/s of FP64 tensor-core peak, needs ~3 hours *if the data fit* — and they don't (§ Wall 1). Meanwhile an exaflop/s machine performs the same work in seconds. To reach exascale rates from single-core rates you would need roughly 1.7 × 10⁸ cores *working concurrently* — which is exactly what real systems do: the June 2026 TOP500 leader, LineShine, runs its HPL benchmark across 13,789,440 cores, and El Capitan (No. 2, 1.809 exaflop/s) across about 11.34 million. Distribution is not a style of programming; at this scale it is the only way the arithmetic is physically reachable.

**Wall 3 — power and economics.** Why not just build one enormous processor? Because performance per watt is brutally against it. Dynamic power scales with frequency and with the square of voltage, and deep pipelines running at high frequency need high voltage — so doubling the clock of one core can cost far more than twice the energy, while two cores at the original clock deliver twice the work for twice the energy. This is why CPU frequency plateaued around 2005 and transistor budgets went into replication instead. The same logic repeats one level up: a cluster of 1,000 cost-efficient nodes beats a single heroic machine of equal throughput, because commodity replication amortizes design cost and each node runs in its efficient regime.

**The price.** Distribution solves capacity, speed, and cost — but it creates the two problems that occupy the rest of this issue and most of this theme:

1. The work and the data must be *divided* among the machines (§1.4).
2. Once divided, the pieces must *communicate*, and that communication is expensive enough to dominate design decisions at scale (§1.5).

One honest caveat: distribution is not free, and one processor is sometimes *enough*. A 4,000 × 4,000 GEMM (128 MB) runs beautifully on one GPU, and wrapping MPI around it would add complexity, debugging pain, and latency for no benefit. Amdahl's law (§1.5) formalizes the penalty for distributing the wrong workload. The engineering judgment — *when* to distribute — is part of the skill; the default answer of serious HPC is "when memory or time demands it," and for the workloads this curriculum follows (HPL/HPL-MxP, HPCG, CFD at scale), it always does.

### 1.2 Shared-memory versus distributed-memory systems (DIST-1.2)

Understanding what we lose when moving to a cluster requires understanding what a single shared-memory machine gives us.

**Shared memory: one address space, implicit communication.** In a shared-memory system, every core can load or store any address. Two threads communicate simply by one writing a variable and the other reading it — communication is *implicit*, disguised as ordinary memory access. The magic that makes this correct is **cache coherence**: each core's private caches hold copies of memory, and a hardware protocol (the MESI family and descendants) invalidates or updates copies whenever a line is written elsewhere. The programmer never issues a "send"; the hardware keeps the illusion of a single memory intact.

The cost of that illusion is why shared memory ends at node scale. Every potentially-shared write must be *observed* by the coherence mechanism across the whole machine, so coherence traffic and directory state grow with core count, and the latency of a remote core's cache line grows with topological distance. Practical shared-memory systems stop at a socket or two (tens of cores); cache-coherent NUMA machines extend to a few sockets; beyond that the physics of the interconnect defeats the protocol. Within the NUMA node the memory is shared but *non-uniform* — nearby memory is faster than far memory — which is already a preview of the locality thinking that dominates distributed systems.

**Distributed memory: many address spaces, explicit communication.** A cluster is a set of independent computers ("nodes"), each with its own memory, connected by a network. There is no load instruction that reaches another node's DRAM. The only way data crosses the network is an explicit **message**: a send on one node paired with a receive on another. Three consequences follow immediately:

- **Data ownership becomes real.** Every byte lives in exactly one node's memory (or in copies the program deliberately made). "If you didn't send it, they don't have it."
- **Communication becomes a programming construct**, not a side effect of memory access — with a cost you can measure, and a semantics (ordering, matching, completion) you must get right.
- **The system scales by composition.** Because no hardware has to keep the whole machine consistent, adding a node adds capacity and bandwidth without any protocol noticing. This is why the winning architecture at exascale is a distributed-memory cluster — and why every TOP500 system in the modern era is one.

A fair question: why not build one giant coherent machine? History tried. Cache-coherent NUMA systems (SGI Origin, SGI Altix, and relatives) delivered genuine shared memory at hundreds of processors, and were loved for programmability — but lost the market to clusters of commodity nodes on price, peak scale, and failure isolation. The hybrid that survived is the modern cluster itself: **distributed memory between nodes, shared memory within them** — multicore CPUs with OpenMP threads or Pthreads *inside* a node, and MPI messages *between* nodes. GPUs add one more layer: inside an HGX-class node, the eight GPUs can address one another's memory over NVLink (peer access), but each GPU still owns its HBM — coherence across GPUs is software-managed. The CPU↔GPU relationship you already know from Theme 1 (explicit `cudaMemcpy`, separate device memory) is itself a distributed-memory interface on a single board.

| Dimension | Shared memory | Distributed memory |
|---|---|---|
| Address space | One, global | One per node |
| Communication | Implicit (load/store) | Explicit (messages) |
| Consistency | Hardware cache coherence | Programmer's contract |
| Typical scale | 1 node / 1–8 sockets | 10³–10⁵+ nodes |
| Failure domain | Whole machine | Per node |
| Programming burden | Races, locks, locality | Decomposition, ownership, communication |

The rank-placement problem this table hides — *where* on the hardware each communicating entity actually sits — is deferred to Module 12, but keep it in mind: shared-versus-distributed is not a binary; it is a hierarchy of nested communication domains, each with different latency, bandwidth, and semantics.

### 1.3 Processes, ranks, threads, and devices (DIST-1.3)

Distributed memory needs execution entities that match its boundaries. The entity the operating system provides is the **process**: a program in flight with its own private virtual address space. That privacy is exactly what distributed memory wants — a process physically *cannot* touch another node's memory, so the programming model and the hardware agree. This is why message-passing systems are built from processes, not threads.

**Ranks: the logical identity of a process.** Launch a program on a cluster and the job launcher (`mpirun` / `mpiexec`, or `srun` under Slurm) starts P processes, typically all running the *same* binary — the SPMD (single program, multiple data) pattern you met in GPU-1.4. Each process calls into the MPI library, which assigns it a **rank**: a logical integer ID, 0 … P−1, inside a **communicator** — an ordered group of processes with its own message-matching context. The default group of all launched processes is `MPI_COMM_WORLD`:

```c
#include <mpi.h>
#include <stdio.h>

int main(int argc, char **argv) {
    MPI_Init(&argc, &argv);
    int rank, size;
    MPI_Comm_rank(MPI_COMM_WORLD, &rank);  /* my logical ID   */
    MPI_Comm_size(MPI_COMM_WORLD, &size);  /* total processes */
    printf("Hello from rank %d of %d\n", rank, size);
    MPI_Finalize();
    return 0;
}
```

Run with `mpirun -np 4 ./hello` and four processes print four greetings (in unspecified order — they are independent). Every rank executes the same code but takes different paths: `if (rank == 0) { ... }` is the distributed-memory version of a branch, and "rank 0 reads the input file and scatters it" is the canonical program structure.

The crucial property of a rank is that it is **logical, not physical**. Rank 7 does not know whether it runs on node 2, which CPU core, next to which GPU, or near which NIC. That mapping is decided by the launcher and scheduler, is invisible to correct code, and is decisive for performance — rank 3 on the same node as rank 4 shares a fast shared-memory path with it, while rank 3 and rank 40 cross the network. Exploiting this is the business of Module 12; today, internalize the separation: *the program speaks in ranks; the system speaks in hardware; someone must translate.*

**Threads: the shared-memory citizens.** Within one process, threads share the address space and serve node-level parallelism — OpenMP loops over CPU cores, or the CUDA host code orchestrating a GPU. Threads also appear *inside* communication libraries as progress threads that advance messages while the main thread computes (a story for Module 11). The two-level composition — **MPI between nodes + threads/GPUs within a node**, the "MPI+X" pattern — is the dominant structure of real scientific codes, and it mirrors the hardware hierarchy of §1.2 exactly.

**Devices: GPUs as rank-owned accelerators.** A GPU is not a peer of the CPU; it is *attached* to a process through a driver-level context, and its HBM is yet another distributed-memory region reached by explicit transfers (Theme 1). The near-universal convention on GPU clusters is **one MPI rank per GPU**: each rank owns one device, selects it from its rank number, and that GPU's memory is effectively that rank's private fast memory. On a cluster of 8 nodes × 4 GPUs, the job has 32 ranks; a typical mapping puts rank r on node ⌊r/4⌋, device r mod 4:

```text
node 0:  rank 0 (GPU 0)  rank 1 (GPU 1)  rank 2 (GPU 2)  rank 3 (GPU 3)
node 1:  rank 4 (GPU 0)  rank 5 (GPU 1)  rank 6 (GPU 2)  rank 7 (GPU 3)
   ...
node 7:  rank 28 (GPU 0) rank 29 (GPU 1) rank 30 (GPU 2) rank 31 (GPU 3)
```

This is precisely the process/GPU geometry of an HPL-MxP or NCCL-driven run, and it is why "how many ranks, and where?" is the first configuration question on any distributed GPU job. Two ranks sharing one GPU (or one rank driving four) is possible and occasionally useful, but it complicates memory, streams, and communication — the 1:1 convention exists because it matches the hardware's ownership structure.

**Why processes (not threads) across nodes:** isolation matches hardware reality; a crashed rank is one process, not a corrupted shared state; and nothing accidentally relies on a pointer that crosses the network. The cost is symmetrical: *nothing* is shared by accident either — every byte that must move is a byte the program must name.

### 1.4 Data partitioning (DIST-1.4)

Work is distributed by distributing the data it operates on, under the **owner-computes rule**: the rank that owns a piece of data is the rank that computes on it. Partitioning is therefore the single most consequential design decision in a distributed code — it determines the load balance, the communication volume, and the communication *pattern* all at once.

**A. Geometric domain decomposition (grids and meshes).** For PDE simulations the data is a physical domain discretized into cells. Split the domain across ranks so each owns a connected subdomain. The immediate problem: a stencil update for a boundary cell needs neighbor values that live on another rank. The standard solution is the **halo** (or ghost) region — each rank allocates extra cells around its subdomain holding *copies* of the neighboring ranks' boundary values:

```text
        rank A's subdomain          rank B's subdomain
   ┌─────────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────────┐
   │                     │    │                     │
   │   interior          │halo│          interior   │
   │   (computed here)   │◄──►│  (computed here)    │
   │                     │    │                     │
   └─────────────────────┘    └─────────────────────┘
        every timestep: exchange the boundary layer
```

Each simulation step, ranks pack their boundary values and exchange them — the **halo exchange**, the single most executed communication pattern in computational physics (and the subject of today's second development). How you *cut* the domain matters enormously. A cube of side L split P ways:

- **1D (slab) decomposition:** each rank owns a slab L² × (L/P) — only 2 cut planes, but at most P ≤ L ranks.
- **2D (pencil):** each rank owns L × (L/√P) × (L/√P) — more ranks, more cut surface; the natural layout for distributed FFTs, which need full lines along one axis at a time.
- **3D (block):** each rank owns a subcube of side s = L/P^(1/3) — maximum rank count, at the price of more boundary per rank.

The governing ratio is **surface to volume**. A rank owning a 3D block of side s has interior work ∝ s³ but halo communication ∝ 6s², so

```text
comm  ∝ s²        compute ∝ s³        comm/compute ∝ 6/s
```

Big subdomains are communication-cheap; small ones are communication-heavy. When you double the number of ranks (strong scaling), s shrinks by 2^(1/3), and *every rank's communication-to-compute ratio rises* — a geometric law, independent of hardware, that will be quantified in §1.5.

**B. Data decomposition for linear algebra.** Matrices have no geometry, so they are partitioned by index. A **block distribution** hands each rank a contiguous slab of rows (or columns) — simple, but in LU factorization the active region shrinks as factorization proceeds, so late stages leave most ranks idle. A **cyclic distribution** strides rows round-robin (row i → rank i mod P), interleaving activity so every rank always has some active rows, at the cost of scattered memory access. The **block-cyclic** distribution combines both: the matrix is tiled into NB × NB blocks, and blocks are dealt round-robin onto a **2D process grid** of Q × R ranks —

```text
        2D process grid (Q=2, R=2), block-cyclic layout

        rank(0,0)  rank(0,1)  rank(1,0) ...   ← columns of blocks
        rank(1,0)  rank(1,1)
             ↓ each rank owns a scattered, regularly repeating set of blocks
```

— which is exactly the layout of ScaLAPACK and HPL, balancing load *and* keeping large contiguous blocks for fast local GEMM. This is the partitioning you will live inside during Theme 3; today, note the principle: **the partitioning is chosen by the algorithm's access pattern and load evolution, not by convenience.**

**C. Irregular data.** Graphs, sparse matrices, and particle sets have no regular structure to slice. Graph partitioners (METIS and successors) split the data minimizing *edge cut* — the communication volume — subject to balanced piece sizes. The tension is now explicit as an optimization problem: perfect load balance with minimum communication and (ideally) compact, connected pieces are competing objectives; every partitioner trades among them.

| Scheme | Typical use | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Block (contiguous) | Arrays, sorted data | Locality, simple indexing | Load imbalance if work is uneven |
| Cyclic | LU-style shrinking active sets | Sustained balance | Poor locality |
| Block-cyclic | Dense LA (ScaLAPACK, HPL) | Balance + big local GEMMs | Index arithmetic; needs 2D process grid |
| 1D/2D/3D domain decomposition | Stencils, grids, FFTs | Matches physics; halo exchange | Surface/volume penalty at high P |
| Graph partitioning | Meshes, sparse matrices, particles | Minimizes edge cut | Partitioning itself costs time; NP-hard in general |

A concrete feel for the numbers: a rank owning a 128³ subdomain with a one-cell halo exchanges 6 × 128² ≈ 98,000 boundary cells against ~2.1 million interior cells — about 4.7% of its data per step. Shrink its subdomain to 64³ and the same ratio more than doubles. Nothing about the network changed; the *geometry of the partition* did.

### 1.5 Communication versus computation (DIST-1.5)

Partitioning splits the work P ways — but the communication it creates does not shrink the same way. Distributed performance is therefore an accounting problem, and the account has three entries beyond compute:

```text
T(P) ≈  compute(P)  +  exposed_communication(P)  +  idle(P)

compute:       total work ÷ (P × per-rank rate)   → falls as 1/P
communication: message start-ups (latency) + bytes ÷ bandwidth
idle:          waiting at synchronizations for the slowest rank
```

**A worked example — strong-scaling a stencil.** Take a 1024³ double-precision grid (8 GiB total) running a 7-point stencil, and model each rank at 100 GFlop/s sustained compute (a modest fraction of a modern GPU's stencil rate) and 25 GB/s effective network injection (one 200 Gb/s InfiniBand rail per rank). Assume ≈ 8 flops per cell, and communication fully serialized with compute — no overlap yet.

*P = 512 = 8³ ranks* → each owns a 128³ block (16 MiB):

```text
compute: 128³ cells × 8 flops = 16.8 Mflop ÷ 100 GFlop/s  = 168 µs
halo:    6 × 128² cells × 8 B = 0.79 MB ÷ 25 GB/s         =  31 µs
step time:                    168 + 31 = 199 µs   (comm/compute = 0.19)
```

*P = 4096 = 16³ ranks* → each owns a 64³ block:

```text
compute: 64³ × 8 = 2.1 Mflop ÷ 100 GFlop/s  = 21 µs
halo:    6 × 64² × 8 B = 0.20 MB ÷ 25 GB/s  = 7.9 µs
step time:                28.9 µs           (comm/compute = 0.38)
```

Read the comparison carefully, because it contains the whole subject:

- Adding 8× more ranks cut per-rank compute 8× (168 → 21 µs) but cut step time only **6.9×** (199 → 28.9 µs).
- The comm/compute ratio *doubled*, exactly as the surface-to-volume law predicts: (6/s) doubles when s halves... here s fell only 2× when P grew 8×, so the ratio doubles — while per-rank work fell 8×.
- Parallel efficiency fell from E = T₁/(P·T_P): compute is now 73% of the step instead of 84%, and the trend continues — at some P the ratio passes 1 and adding ranks makes the step *slower*.

This is the geometric core of **strong scaling** (fixed problem, more ranks): per-rank work vanishes as 1/P, communication shrinks slower (halo bytes as 1/P^(2/3) per rank in 3D), and latency-driven terms (synchronizations, global reductions — one per step, regardless of P) do not shrink at all.

**Amdahl's law — the serial ceiling.** If a fraction s of the program is inherently serial, speedup on P processors is bounded:

```text
S(P) = 1 / (s + (1−s)/P)   →   S_max = 1/s   as P → ∞
```

s = 5% caps every speedup at 20×, forever. Communication is *worse than Amdahl's serial fraction*: Amdahl's s is fixed, but the exposed-communication fraction **grows with P**, so real strong-scaling curves fall off Amdahl's curve — they plateau, then roll over. (Caveat for honesty: the occasional *superlinear* point below the plateau comes from cache effects — a smaller per-rank working set fits in cache.)

**Gustafson's law — grow the problem instead.** In production HPC the question is rarely "same problem faster" but "bigger problem in the same time." Scale the grid with P — keep each rank at 128³ — and the surface-to-volume ratio *per rank stays constant*: the 19% overhead above never degrades. With per-rank serial/communication overhead s′, the scaled speedup is

```text
S(P) ≈ P − s′·(P − 1)
```

nearly linear — this is **weak scaling**, and it is why the same code that strong-scales painfully past a few thousand ranks weak-scales comfortably to the full machine. (Not perfectly: per-step global operations — an all-reduce of a residual norm, a convergence check — have latency terms that stay constant while the step shrinks, and they bite small per-rank workloads first.)

**Hiding communication.** The accounting above assumed serialization, but the third lever is **overlap**: a rank can start its halo exchange and, while the bytes fly, compute the *interior* cells that need no neighbor data — only the boundary strip must wait. If interior work ≥ halo time, communication vanishes from the critical path: it becomes *hidden* rather than *exposed*. Whether overlap is possible depends on dependency structure (interior updates are genuinely independent — later modules make this precise with non-blocking operations, double buffering, and pipelining), and it is the single most valuable optimization in stencil codes. HPL plays the same game at dense-linear-algebra scale: the panel broadcast is overlapped with the enormous trailing-matrix GEMM updates (Theme 3 will trace this in detail).

**The three separate bottlenecks to keep distinct** (Module 16 turns this into a diagnostic method):

1. **Latency-bound** — many small messages (fine-grained halos, reductions): fix by aggregation, fewer/larger messages.
2. **Bandwidth-bound** — large transfers at the wire's limit: fix by reducing bytes (better partitioning, lower-precision halos) or more rails.
3. **Synchronization/load-balance-bound** — ranks idle at barriers waiting for the slowest: fix the imbalance, not the network.

The unifying habit of mind: **compute scales with volume, communication with surface — and every scaling decision is a bet on which term dominates.** HPL-MxP is the extreme case where the volume term (⅔n³ of dense GEMM work) so dominates the surface term (n²-scale panel movement) that dense linear algebra scales to the largest machines on Earth — provided the surface term's *latency* is hidden, which is exactly the art of HPL optimization on GPU systems.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 MPI 5.0's binary interface reaches production: MPICH 5.0.0

**What happened.** The MPI Forum ratified **MPI 5.0 on June 5, 2025**, its headline feature a standard **Application Binary Interface (ABI)** — new Chapter 20 of the standard. On **February 4, 2026**, Argonne released **MPICH 5.0.0**, the first stable implementation with full MPI-5 support *including* the ABI, plus more robust MPI Sessions support (finer-grained, dynamically manageable parallelism) and a native rendezvous protocol path in its CH4/OFI network layer (more on rendezvous in Module 2).

**What technically changed.** Since 1994 MPI has been an *API* standard: it fixes source code, but the compiled result — how handles, datatypes, and function symbols are represented in a binary — was implementation-defined. A program compiled against one MPI family had to run with that family: in practice the ecosystem had consolidated into two ABI camps, the MPICH lineage (MPICH, Intel MPI, MVAPICH, Cray MPICH) and the Open MPI lineage (Open MPI, NVIDIA HPC-X, IBM Spectrum MPI), which together — as the Forum's ISC 2025 BoF noted — cover over 90% of HPC platforms. MPI-5's ABI changes that: compile against the standard header (`abi/mpi.h`) and the `libmpi_abi` interface, and the same binary links against *any* conforming implementation. MPICH 5.0.0 is the first stable release shipping it; Open MPI's implementation was still in progress (a long-running pull request) as of the ISC 2025 BoF, and the community took stock of the first year of MPI 5.0 adoption at an ISC 2026 BoF.

**Why it matters.** This is a deployment and ecosystem story more than a performance story. Today, module systems, Spack, containers, and cluster admins fight a combinatorial explosion of "application × MPI implementation × compiler" builds; an ABI collapses it. Binary portability also simplifies vendor support ("ship one binary, run anywhere") and makes MPI usable inside software containers whose host MPI is unknown at build time.

**Connection to today's curriculum.** MPI is the standardized embodiment of exactly what this issue taught: processes as the distributed-memory execution entity, ranks and communicators as logical identity, and explicit messages as the only cross-node data path (§1.2–1.3). The deeper lesson is architectural: thirty-two years of hardware evolution — MPPs, clusters, fat nodes, GPU accelerators, exascale systems — has not displaced a model written for 1994's distributed-memory machines. The abstractions of §1.2 are *stable*; hardware changes underneath them, and libraries absorb the change (GPU-aware paths, RDMA, in-network collectives — Modules 6–9). Note also what MPI 5.0 did *not* change: ranks, communicators, and send/receive semantics are untouched — an ABI is invisible to them.

**What to watch next.** Open MPI's ABI landing in a release; MPI 6.0 work (the Forum's published directions include partitioned communication, GPU/accelerator bindings, Sessions-based dynamic resource management, and fault tolerance — all direct responses to the multi-GPU systems this curriculum is heading toward).

### 2.2 Halo exchange on GPUs: partitioning, packing, and topology (IJHPCA 2026)

**What happened.** A 2026 paper in the *International Journal of High Performance Computing Applications* by Johannes Pekkilä and Touko Puro, *"Optimizing GPU-aware halo exchange for computations on structured grids,"* systematically studies the complete halo-exchange pipeline on GPU systems: packing boundary data, communicating it with GPU-aware MPI, decomposing the domain, and assigning processes to the hardware — tested on one- to five-dimensional grids on AMD MI250X, NVIDIA V100, and NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

**What was demonstrated.** Three findings matter beyond the paper itself. First, replacing MPI's built-in **derived datatypes** (which describe strided memory regions to the library) with **custom packing kernels** — small GPU kernels that gather the halo into a contiguous buffer — sped up the exchange by up to **two orders of magnitude** compared with the datatype-based paths of MPICH and Open MPI on the tested systems. The convenience abstraction was leaving up to ~100× of performance on the table, because datatype-driven transfers serialize and copy far less efficiently than an explicitly written gather kernel on a GPU. Second, the authors show **topology-aware domain decomposition and process assignment** (matching the partition geometry to the hierarchy of NVLink/PCIe/network proximities) matters as much as the packing. Third, end to end, the techniques improved the strong scaling of a magnetohydrodynamics stencil code by **1.58× at high process counts** — squarely in the regime of §1.5 where surface-to-volume turns against you.

**Why it matters.** Halo exchange is the distilled essence of today's lesson: domain decomposition creates the surface (§1.4), the surface's bytes must be packed, moved, and unpacked (§1.2's explicit communication), and the comm/compute ratio decides whether scaling continues (§1.5). The paper is quantitative proof that on GPU clusters, the *software structure of the partition* — how you cut, pack, and place — can be worth more than any network upgrade. These are the authors' measured results on specific systems (MI250X, V100, A100, their MPI builds), not universal constants; the structural lesson — derived conveniences underperform explicit kernels on GPUs, and partitioning must respect topology — is what generalizes.

**What to watch next.** As GPU nodes get denser (more GPUs per node, faster scale-up fabrics), per-step time budgets shrink, and the halo exchange's share of runtime grows. The paper's open-source communication toolkit doubles as a benchmark suite — a good way to see these effects on your own hardware. The GPU-aware MPI machinery it relies on (how a NIC touches GPU memory at all) is the subject of Module 6 onward.
