## 0. Concise Summary

The first HPL volume (2026-09-09) built the mathematical objects: scalars, vectors, matrices, dot products, matrix-vector and matrix-matrix multiplication. Today we cross the boundary from *mathematics* to *machinery* in two deliberate steps.

First, we look at where matrices actually live. A matrix is a two-dimensional mathematical object, but memory is a one-dimensional address space — so every matrix library must choose a layout convention, and **transpose** (HPL-1.6) is the operation that exposes that choice. Column-major versus row-major storage, strides, and leading dimensions explain why identical-looking loops can differ by an order of magnitude in speed, and why BLAS routines accept a "use the transpose" flag instead of physically transposing data.

Second, we meet the cast of characters that factorization algorithms produce: **identity, diagonal, triangular, and permutation matrices** (HPL-1.7). These structures are not textbook decoration — they are the *output types* of LU factorization. A triangular matrix can be solved against in O(n²) instead of O(n³) work precisely because its zeros are guaranteed and can be skipped; a permutation is stored as an n-integer pivot vector instead of an n×n matrix. Dense linear algebra is largely the art of *manufacturing exploitable structure*.

That leads directly to **solving Ax = b** (HPL-1.8): why direct solvers factor A = P·L·U once (about ⅔n³ operations) and then solve each right-hand side with two cheap triangular solves (O(n²) each), rather than inverting the matrix — which costs several times more and is numerically worse. Finally, we build the accounting system: **operation counts** (HPL-2.1) — counting multiplications, additions, and fused multiply-adds kernel by kernel — and **FLOPs versus FLOP/s** (HPL-2.2), the crucial distinction between the algorithmic work a problem contains (a property of the algorithm) and the rate at which a machine executes it (a property of the hardware). Rpeak, Rmax, and efficiency are all constructed from these two ideas.

The developments section applies today's vocabulary to the real world: the **June 2026 TOP500 list**, where China's CPU-only LineShine debuted at No. 1 with 2.198 Exaflop/s on HPL — 80% of its theoretical peak — and where the HPL-to-HPL-MxP speedup ratio (9.2× for GPU-based El Capitan versus 3.6× for LineShine) is a direct lesson in precision-dependent FLOP/s; and the **BLIS 2.0 release**, which brings fully mixed-precision, mixed-domain Level-3 BLAS and user-registerable microkernels into an open-source BLAS framework.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Transpose: where mathematics meets memory (HPL-1.6)

**The problem.** So far we have treated matrices as abstract grids of numbers. A running program must store them in RAM, which is a linear sequence of bytes. The mapping from a two-dimensional index (i, j) to a one-dimensional offset is a *design decision*, not a law of nature — and the transpose operation is where that decision becomes visible, because transposing changes exactly the thing the layout convention controls.

**The transpose itself.** For an m×n matrix A, the transpose Aᵀ is the n×m matrix with (Aᵀ)ᵢⱼ = Aⱼᵢ: rows become columns. Two algebraic facts we will use constantly:

- (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ — order reverses;
- (Aᵀ)ᵀ = A.

For complex matrices, the *conjugate transpose* Aᴴ additionally conjugates each entry; it is the standard in Hermitian eigenproblems. BLAS routines encode this in their `transa`/`transb` arguments: 'N' (normal), 'T' (transpose), 'C' (conjugate transpose).

**The two layout conventions.** A column-major language (Fortran) stores the entries of a matrix column by column in memory; a row-major language (C, C++) stores them row by row. The BLAS and LAPACK — born in the Fortran world — are column-major to this day, and HPL inherits it. Concretely, for a 3×3 matrix

```text
        [ 10  11  12 ]        column-major offsets:      row-major offsets:
    A = [ 20  21  22 ]        10 20 30 11 21 31 12 22 32  10 11 12 20 21 22 30 31 32
        [ 30  31  32 ]
```

The element A(i, j) (1-based, LAPACK style) lives at:

- **column-major:** offset (i−1) + (j−1)·LDA — move down within a column first;
- **row-major:** offset (i−1)·LDA + (j−1) — move along a row first;

where LDA is the *leading dimension*: the allocated length of a column (column-major) or row (row-major). The leading dimension is what lets a routine operate on a submatrix *without copying it* — you point at element (1, k) of the big array and pass LDA of the big array, and the library strides through the surrounding memory correctly. That is why every BLAS/LAPACK signature carries LDA, LDB, LDC alongside the matrix pointers, and why the LAPACK documentation declares arrays as `A(LDA,*)`.

**Why the convention has performance consequences.** Walking down a column of a column-major matrix touches consecutive addresses — every load brings in a full cache line of useful data. Walking along a *row* of the same matrix jumps by n×8 bytes between elements (for FP64). With 64-byte cache lines holding 8 doubles, a stride-n row traversal touches a fresh cache line for every single element needed: 8× the memory traffic, no spatial locality for the prefetcher to exploit, and far more TLB pressure. The matrix is the same; the *access pattern relative to the layout* decides whether the memory system sees a stream or a scatter. This is the first concrete instance of a theme that will dominate this curriculum (HPL-2.5–2.8, HPL-9.4): on modern machines, *how you move data often matters more than how much arithmetic you do*.

**Transpose as pure data movement.** Transposing a square matrix in place swaps A(i, j) with A(j, i) for all pairs above the diagonal — n(n−1)/2 swaps, **zero floating-point operations**. It is the first operation in this curriculum whose entire cost is data movement, and it is not free: those swaps are scattered accesses in at least one direction. A non-square in-place transpose is harder still — it is a permutation of the underlying 1-D array with long cycles, which libraries implement either by cycle-following (slow, O(nm) moves with terrible locality) or by an out-of-place copy through a cache-sized *blocked* transpose: move the matrix tile by tile so that each tile fits in cache, and do the fine-grained transposition inside the tile. The blocked transpose is the canonical "hello world" of cache blocking — the same trick that blocked LU and tiled GEMM use (Modules 8 and 9).

**The BLAS answer: don't materialize the transpose at all.** Because (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ and because a transposed view is just a different stride pattern, `dgemm` can consume Aᵀ by walking the columns of A. You pass `transa='T'`; the library's kernel reads the same memory with swapped strides. No copy, no extra traffic. High-performance code goes further with *packing*: before the main GEMM loop, blocks are copied into a layout optimized for the microkernel (HPL-9.6) — a deliberate, controlled data-movement step bought to make everything after it fast.

**Where transpose shows up in this curriculum.** Symmetric matrices (A = Aᵀ) let you store one triangle and halve memory — LAPACK's symmetric and packed storage schemes do exactly that. In LU factorization, pivot row interchanges are in-place row swaps inside a column-major array; reading a row of U is a strided access; and applying the permutation P to a vector is a gather. Transpose is never the star of the show, but it is on the critical path everywhere.

**A practical gotcha worth knowing now.** cuBLAS, NVIDIA's BLAS library, is column-major — inherited from the Fortran BLAS interface — while most CUDA/C++ code thinks in row-major. The standard trick for computing a row-major C = A·B with cuBLAS is to ask it for the column-major result Cᵀ = Bᵀ·Aᵀ, which exploits exactly the reversal rule above. Anyone doing multi-GPU dense linear algebra (including HPL-MxP work) hits this in the first week.

### 1.2 Identity, diagonal, triangular, and permutation matrices (HPL-1.7)

**The problem.** Gaussian elimination and LU factorization (Modules 5–6) transform a general dense matrix into a product of *structured* matrices. To understand what those algorithms produce — and why that output is valuable — we need the structures themselves. Each one represents a guarantee about where the zeros are, and every guaranteed zero is work we never have to do.

**Identity.** I has 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere: Iᵢⱼ = δᵢⱼ. It is the multiplicative identity, IA = A. It costs nothing to apply and appears in factorizations as the "do nothing" factor — for example, the unit diagonal of L is an implicit identity block.

**Diagonal.** D = diag(d₁, …, dₙ) has entries only where i = j. Multiplying D·A scales row i of A by dᵢ; A·D scales columns. The structural payoff: multiplying a *vector* by D is n multiplications, not n² — the O(n²) "dense" cost collapses to O(n) because the zeros are known in advance. Diagonal scaling is used constantly to normalize matrices (equilibration) and, later in this curriculum, to make low-precision formats safe (HPL-14.7). A matrix that is *dominated* by its diagonal — diagonally dominant — is also the classic easy case for iterative solvers and for LU stability.

**Triangular.** A lower-triangular matrix L has zeros above the diagonal; an upper-triangular U has zeros below. The defining property is a *dependency direction*: in a lower-triangular system Lx = b, x₁ depends only on b₁; x₂ on b₁, b₂; xᵢ only on already-known quantities. That is why *forward substitution* solves it directly, top to bottom:

```text
x₁ = b₁ / l₁₁
xᵢ = (bᵢ − lᵢ₁x₁ − lᵢ₂x₂ − … − lᵢ,ᵢ₋₁xᵢ₋₁) / lᵢ,ᵢ     for i = 2 … n
```

Each xᵢ costs one pass over the already-solved entries: about 2(i−1) multiply–adds plus one division. Summing over i gives ≈ n² FLOPs total — compared with the 2n² FLOPs of even a single general matrix-vector product, and the ⅔n³ of factoring a general matrix in the first place. Upper-triangular systems are solved identically but bottom-to-top (*back substitution*). This asymmetry — general systems are cubic, triangular ones quadratic — is the entire economic engine of direct solvers.

A *unit* triangular matrix has 1s on its diagonal. LAPACK's LU convention uses unit lower-triangular L, and the LAPACK documentation for the driver `dgesv` states plainly that "the unit diagonal elements of L are not stored": n numbers saved, and n divisions skipped during the solve, because dividing by 1 is free.

**Permutation.** A permutation matrix P has exactly one 1 in each row and each column, 0 elsewhere. Multiplying P·A reorders the rows of A; A with its columns reordered comes from right-multiplication. Three properties make permutations special:

1. They are *orthogonal*: PᵀP = I, so P⁻¹ = Pᵀ — undoing a permutation is itself a permutation, with no arithmetic.
2. Any permutation is a product of pairwise row swaps (transpositions).
3. In LU with partial pivoting, P is the product of the n−1 row swaps chosen during factorization — so it is never stored as an n×n matrix. LAPACK stores the pivot indices in an integer vector `ipiv` of length n, with the documented meaning "row i of the matrix was interchanged with row ipiv(i)". An O(n²) mathematical object is represented in O(n) storage, and applying it to a vector is a sequence of swaps rather than a matvec.

**Structure summary.**

| Structure | Guarantee | What it buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | zeros everywhere but diagonal 1s | free pass-through |
| Diagonal | zeros off-diagonal | O(n) scaling instead of O(n²) |
| Triangular | zeros on one side of the diagonal | O(n²) substitution instead of O(n³) solve |
| Permutation | one 1 per row/column | row swaps, O(n) storage as pivot vector |

The deep point to carry forward: **dense LU factorization is the manufacture of triangular structure.** We will spend Modules 5–11 on how to build L, U, and the pivot sequence as cheaply and as scalably as possible — and the entire motivation is this section: once the structure exists, exploiting it is easy.

### 1.3 Systems of linear equations: why factor first (HPL-1.8)

**The problem stated precisely.** Given an n×n matrix A and a vector b, find x with Ax = b. Two complementary readings:

- *Row view:* each row of A defines a hyperplane aᵢᵀx = bᵢ in n-dimensional space; the solution is the point where all n hyperplanes meet.
- *Column view:* x is the set of coefficients that expresses b as a linear combination of the columns of A; a solution exists for every b exactly when the columns span the whole space.

A unique solution exists **if and only if A is nonsingular** (equivalently: no zero pivot will be fatal, no column combination collapses). Nonsingularity guarantees existence in exact arithmetic — it says nothing about how *sensitively* x depends on A and b. That sensitivity is the condition number, and it becomes the central object of Modules 15 and 21. Keep the two questions separate from the start: "is the problem solvable?" is a property of A; "is the answer trustworthy in finite precision?" is a property of the *problem* (conditioning) interacting with the *algorithm* (stability).

**Why not just invert A?** Mathematically, x = A⁻¹b. Computationally, this is the classic trap. Forming A⁻¹ explicitly costs on the order of 2n³ FLOPs — roughly three times the ⅔n³ of LU factorization — before you even multiply it by b. It is also numerically inferior: an LU-based solve comes with a small, well-understood *backward error* guarantee (the computed x is the exact solution of a slightly perturbed system), which explicit inversion does not improve and can worsen. The rule taught in every numerical linear algebra course — and enforced by every production library — is: *write A⁻¹b on paper; never compute A⁻¹ on a machine.* LAPACK does provide an explicit inverse routine (`getri`), because some applications genuinely need all n² entries of A⁻¹ — but solving Ax = b is not one of them.

**The factor-then-solve pattern.** The direct-solver strategy is a one-time expensive step followed by many cheap steps:

```text
              factor once:  PA = LU              cost ≈ ⅔n³  + O(n²)
                           ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
   A, b ──►  dgetrf  ──►  │ A's storage now holds L and U       │
                           │ (L unit-diagonal unstored)          │
                           │ ipiv(1..n) records the row swaps    │
                           └─────────────────────────────────────┘
              solve, per right-hand side:
                           1. apply swaps:      Pb        O(n)
                           2. forward solve:    Ly = Pb    ≈ n² FLOPs
                           3. back solve:       Ux = y     ≈ n² FLOPs
```

This is exactly the LAPACK driver `dgesv`: it factors A as P·L·U using `dgetrf`, then uses the factors to solve A·X = B. Note the interface detail: B is n×NRHS — a *matrix* of right-hand sides. Factor once, solve for many right-hand sides; each additional column of B costs O(n²), not another ⅔n³. The break-even is immediate: the moment you have (or expect) a second right-hand side — or a refinement step, which is precisely a sequence of correction solves (Module 15) — factoring first has already paid for itself.

The solve step is where Section 1.2's structures do their work: L and U are triangular, so steps 2–3 are forward and back substitution; P is a permutation, so step 1 is a sequence of row swaps recorded in `ipiv`. A = P·L·U is not just a formula — it is a *data structure*: the factored matrix, packed in place into the original n² storage (HPL-6.4 will detail exactly how L and U share the array).

**Direct versus iterative.** *Direct* methods (LU here; QR, Cholesky in the same family) perform a finite, predetermined sequence of steps and, in exact arithmetic, deliver the solution. *Iterative* methods (conjugate gradients, GMRES) start from a guess and improve it until a tolerance is met, with each step dominated by a matrix-vector product. The trade: direct methods are robust and exact-ish but pay ⅔n³ and require the dense matrix in memory; iterative methods cost O(n·nnz) per iteration and can solve systems with billions of unknowns — but only converge quickly when the matrix has helpful spectral properties. Dense direct solvers are the natural home of the TOP500 benchmark precisely because their dominant inner kernels (GEMM) can drive a machine to its arithmetic peak — a statement we will make precise with arithmetic intensity in HPL-2.5–2.6. The HPCG benchmark, in contrast, exercises the sparse-iterative world, and the gap between the two on any given machine (about 100×, as we will see in Section 2) is one of the most quoted numbers in HPC.

**HPL, in one paragraph.** The High Performance Linpack benchmark *is* this section, industrialized: generate a random dense A of order N and a right-hand side b; solve Ax = b by LU with partial pivoting on a distributed-memory machine; verify that the scaled residual ‖Ax − b‖ / (‖A‖·‖x‖) is small; report the execution rate. The verification step is not decorative — a wrong answer at record speed is worth nothing (a principle the curriculum formalizes as HPL-21.8). Everything HPL-specific — the block-cyclic distribution, the process grid, the panel broadcasts, the look-ahead — exists to make the factorization of Section 1.3's diagram run fast on thousands of nodes. We reach those mechanisms in Modules 10–13.

### 1.4 Operation counts: reading the bill line by line (HPL-2.1)

**The discipline.** An operation count tallies the floating-point additions, subtractions, multiplications, and divisions an algorithm performs, as a function of problem size. It is a property of the *algorithm* — independent of the machine, the language, the precision, and the compiler. The habit to build: when you meet a kernel, count its multiplications and additions *separately* first, then sum. The separate tallies matter for old hardware (multiplies and divides once cost very different amounts), for pedagogy — and because the multiply–add pair is about to become the FMA.

**Counting the kernels from Modules 1 and today.**

| Kernel | Definition | Multiplications | Additions | Total FLOPs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dot product | s = x·y | n | n−1 | ≈ 2n |
| AXPY | y ← αx + y | n | n | 2n |
| GEMV | y = Ax (m×n A) | mn | m(n−1) | ≈ 2mn |
| Triangular solve | Lx = b | n(n−1)/2 | n(n−1)/2 + n div | ≈ n² |
| GEMM | C = AB (m×n×k) | mnk | mnk | 2mnk |

Reading the GEMM row the way we will formalize next volume: C has mn entries; entry cᵢⱼ is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B — k multiply–adds, i.e., 2k FLOPs — so the total is 2mnk, which is 2n³ for square matrices. The inner statement `c[i][j] += a[i][k] * b[k][j]` executes mnk times and carries one multiplication plus one addition each time. (The full derivation, and why O(n³) growth dominates everything else in dense linear algebra, is HPL-2.3, next volume.)

**Fused operations.** On every modern CPU and GPU, the dominant floating-point instruction is the *fused multiply-add*: fma(a, b, c) computes a·b + c in one instruction, with a single rounding. Two points are easy to confuse and essential to separate:

1. **An FMA is one instruction but two FLOPs.** The FLOP is a unit of *algorithmic work*; the instruction count is a property of the implementation. Operation counts do not change when code is fused, vectorized, or unrolled — 2mnk FLOPs of GEMM work remains 2mnk FLOPs whether the hardware issues 2mnk scalar instructions or mnk/32 vector FMAs.
2. **FMA changes the cost *ratio*, not the total.** Because a fused multiply-add retires a multiply and an addition together, modern pipelines make the two operations essentially equally fast. This is why the convention of counting each as one FLOP is fair on today's machines — a point Dongarra makes explicitly in the LINPACK benchmark history paper when explaining why the benchmark's operation accounting treats additions and multiplications symmetrically.

Divisions are the exception: a floating-point divide is multi-cycle and often not fully pipelined. LU factorization performs only n of them (the pivot-row normalizations), so for large n they are statistically invisible — but in small-kernel and panel tuning (Module 8) they reappear, because there the "negligible" terms are no longer negligible.

**Counts as benchmark conventions.** Here is a subtlety that separates people who read benchmark rules from people who skim them: the operation count used to compute a reported FLOP/s rate is a *fixed convention*, not a measurement of the instructions actually executed. For the Linpack family, the rule is that the count is ⅔n³ + 2n² FLOPs — "independent of the actual method used", as the LINPACK benchmark documentation puts it — so that rates are comparable across machines and algorithms. The TOP500's Linpack description is explicit that this fixed count is also what excludes Strassen-style fast multiplication (a different operation count entirely) and mixed-precision-then-refine methods (which moved to their own benchmark, HPL-MxP). The historical paper behind the benchmark shows the spirit of the convention: for the original n = 100 LINPACK benchmark, an exact instruction-level tally came to 697,509 floating-point operations, while the scoring formula ⅔n³ + 2n² gives 686,667 — the fixed formula deliberately ignores the small stuff.

**Why we count.** Three reasons that will recur every week of this curriculum:

- **Runtime prediction:** time ≈ work / rate. Knowing a problem contains 2×10¹² FLOPs and a machine sustains 10¹² FLOP/s on that kernel predicts ~2 s — and the error in that prediction is diagnostic (Section 1.5).
- **Finding the dominant kernel:** summing the counts phase by phase tells you where the time must go. In LU factorization, the trailing-matrix GEMM updates account for essentially all of the ⅔n³; panels and solves are lower-order terms. That single fact explains why dense linear algebra performance *is* GEMM performance — the design center of Modules 8, 9, and 12.
- **Sanity-checking claims:** a speedup that implies more FLOP/s than the hardware can issue is a red flag, whatever the benchmark graph looks like (HPL-24.3 develops this into a reading skill).

### 1.5 FLOPs and FLOP/s: work versus rate (HPL-2.2)

**Two different quantities.** FLOPs (lowercase s) is a *count of work*: the number of floating-point operations a computation contains. FLOP/s (the slash matters) is a *rate*: operations per unit time, work divided by elapsed time. The distinction sounds pedantic and is anything but: the first belongs to the algorithm and the problem; the second belongs to the machine *and the workload being run on it*. Every performance number you will ever quote is a fraction whose numerator is a FLOP count and whose denominator is a measured second.

A small worked example fixes the difference. Multiply two 10⁴×10⁴ matrices: work = 2·(10⁴)³ = 2×10¹² FLOPs, memory for each matrix = 8×10⁸ bytes = 800 MB (the O(n²) storage story is HPL-2.4, next volume). The *same work* takes:

- ~10 s at 200 GF/s (one strong CPU core),
- ~2 ms at 1 PF/s (one large node or small cluster),
- ~2 µs at 1 EF/s (an exascale machine — if you could keep such a small problem from being drowned in overheads, which you cannot; why not is Section 1.5's last lesson).

**Rpeak: the theoretical rate.** A processor's peak FLOP/s is a definition, computed from three numbers: cores × clock frequency × FLOPs per core per cycle, where the last factor is (SIMD lanes per FMA unit) × (FMA units) × 2. Summed over a whole machine, this is Rpeak. It is a ceiling that assumes every cycle issues maximum-width FMAs with no interruptions — no cache misses, no waits on data, no communication, no branches. Nothing real sustains Rpeak; the interesting question is what fraction of it real workloads achieve.

A useful exercise you can now do yourself: the June 2026 TOP500 list reports LineShine (Section 2.1) with Rpeak = 2.736 EF/s, 13,789,440 cores, and a 1.55 GHz clock. Dividing, 2.736×10¹⁸ ÷ (13,789,440 × 1.55×10⁹) = **128 FP64 FLOPs per core-cycle** — that is, 64 double-precision FMAs per cycle per counted core. Whatever a "core" means in that submission (TOP500 counts what the submitter counts), the published peak *defines* the per-cycle capability this way. Rpeak is arithmetic on a spec sheet; it is not measured.

**Rmax: the measured rate.** Rmax is the HPL result: total conventional FLOPs (⅔N³ + 2N² for problem size N) divided by measured wall-clock time on the largest problem run (Nmax). Efficiency = Rmax / Rpeak is the single most information-dense number in HPC procurement: it says how much of the machine's arithmetic a real, communication-heavy, load-balanced application can actually extract. Sustained efficiencies above ~70% on a full-scale machine are excellent; small-problem effects, panel critical paths, and interconnect contention all push down. TOP500 also tracks N½ — the problem size at which a machine reaches half its Rmax — a measure of how quickly a machine "warms up" to peak; a machine with large N½ wastes most of its capability on everything smaller.

**Rate is kernel-dependent and size-dependent.** The same processor sustains very different FLOP/s on different operations:

- GEMM, with its enormous data reuse (HPL-2.7), approaches a large fraction of Rpeak for large enough matrices.
- A dot product performs 2 FLOPs per 16 bytes of data moved — the memory system, not the arithmetic units, sets the ceiling: rate ≤ bandwidth × (FLOPs per byte). A dot product runs at a small fraction of Rpeak no matter how it is scheduled.
- HPCG, built from sparse matvecs and vector triads in that same memory-bound regime, yields a few percent of a machine's HPL rate — on the June 2026 list, No. 1 LineShine scored 22.00 PF/s on HPCG against 2,198 PF/s on HPL: almost exactly 1%. One percent is not a scandal; it is the arithmetic-intensity difference between the two benchmark worlds, made concrete (HPL-2.6 formalizes this).

Rates also grow with problem size toward an *asymptotic* ceiling — for small matrices, launch overheads, pipeline fill, and short inner loops dominate, and the measured FLOP/s is far below the large-N value. (The LINPACK benchmark documentation describes exactly this behavior when explaining N½.)

**Rate is precision-dependent.** A "FLOP" is one operation at some precision — and the precision must be stated or the number is meaningless. On modern GPUs, the low-precision matrix engines run FP16/BF16 (and FP8-class) GEMM at many times the FP64 rate; a tensor-core FP64 path may itself run at 2× the CUDA-core FP64 path. The same physical machine therefore has several legitimate "peak FLOP/s" numbers. This is precisely the distinction the HPL-MxP benchmark institutionalizes: it measures a mixed-precision solve (factor fast in low precision, recover accuracy by refinement — Module 14) and reports a different rate on the same hardware. Section 2.1's headline ratios — 9.2× for a GPU machine, 3.6× for a CPU-only one — are this paragraph wearing a news headline.

**The reading skill to retain.** When you see a FLOP/s claim, ask: (1) what operation, at what problem size? (2) what precision, and are multiply-accumulates counted as 2? (3) measured, or projected from Rpeak? (4) numerator from the fixed benchmark formula or from an instruction tally? Four questions, and most marketing comparisons fail at least one. This is the vocabulary the next two volumes extend: from "how fast" to "how much data must move, and what does moving it cost" (HPL-2.5–2.8).

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 The June 2026 TOP500: LineShine debuts at No. 1 — a table you can now read

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 in Hamburg (June 23, 2026), the 67th TOP500 list put a previously unlisted Chinese system, **LineShine**, at No. 1 with an HPL result of **2.198 Exaflop/s** — the first submitted result above two exaflops of sustained FP64, and about 80% of the machine's 2.736 EF/s theoretical peak. LineShine is installed at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen and built on the custom "LingKun" platform: 13,789,440 cores organized as 304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz, a proprietary LingQi interconnect, and Kylin OS. It is the fifth exascale-class system overall and the first Chinese system to lead the list since Sunway TaihuLight in 2017. El Capitan (1.809 EF/s) and Frontier (1.353 EF/s) complete the top three; El Capitan also holds No. 1 on HPL-MxP at 16.7 EF/s, while LineShine simultaneously took No. 1 on HPCG at 22.00 PF/s.

**What technically changed or was demonstrated.** Two things make this a textbook event for today's topics rather than just a leaderboard shuffle. First, the efficiency figure: 80.3% of Rpeak on HPL, on a *CPU-only* machine with 13.8 million cores. Section 1.5 defined efficiency as the ratio of measured work rate to spec-sheet arithmetic; holding 80% of that ratio across a machine of this scale means the LU implementation kept its GEMM engines nearly saturated — the trailing-update GEMM (which Section 1.4 showed is nearly all of the ⅔N³ work) consistently won its race against panel factorization and interconnect waits. Second, the precision story in the HPL-MxP column: LineShine reached 7.92 EF/s there — only 3.6× its FP64 HPL rate — while GPU-based El Capitan shows 9.2×. That ratio is a direct read-out of Section 1.5's "rate is precision-dependent": El Capitan's accelerators contain dedicated low-precision matrix engines that multiply the effective rate for the mixed-precision algorithm, whereas LineShine's general CPU pipelines have far less headroom between FP64 and lower precision. The ratio, not either absolute number, is the architectural fingerprint. And the HPCG number — 22 PF/s against 2,198 PF/s of HPL, the ~1% gap predicted by arithmetic intensity — shows the same machine measured in the memory-bound world.

**Why it matters and how it connects.** The TOP500's Rmax, Rpeak, efficiency, and the HPL/HPL-MxP/HPCG spread are exactly the quantities Sections 1.4–1.5 built from first principles. This list is also a live experiment in architectural diversity at the exascale frontier: a CPU-only design at No. 1, GPU-apu designs behind it, and materially different mixed-precision profiles — evidence that there is still no single dominant path to leadership-class dense-linear-algebra performance.

**What to watch next.** The November 2026 list (whether any system answers with dedicated matrix engines of its own), and — closer to this curriculum — Modules 14–16, where the HPL-MxP algorithm that produced that 9.2× ratio gets its full treatment: which parts of LU actually run in low precision, and why the correction steps are cheap enough to preserve the speedup.

### 2.2 BLIS 2.0: mixed precision and user kernels come to open-source Level-3 BLAS

**What happened.** The BLIS project — the open-source "BLAS-like Library Instantiation Software Framework" from the FLAME group — released version 2.0 (June 2025), a major framework update rather than a routine patch. Headline changes: a **plugin system** that lets users register their own optimized microkernels and block sizes *outside* the BLIS source tree, with run-time selection based on the actual hardware; a public API for modifying the **control tree**, the data structure that defines the algorithmic steps (packing, blocking, kernel calls) used to implement each Level-3 operation; **full mixed-precision, mixed-domain Level-3 computation** — the A, B, and C matrices and the α/β scalars may now be in any supported type, with a separately specified *computational* precision governing how the arithmetic is actually performed internally; a new "level-0" macro back-end implementing the typed operations underneath; and a ScaLAPACK compatibility mode.

**What technically changed or was demonstrated.** The mixed-precision design is the most conceptually interesting part for this curriculum: it factorizes what today's lesson treated as one thing. An operation like C = A·B now has *storage* precisions (what format each operand lives in), a *computational* precision (what the internal arithmetic uses — e.g., inputs in double, products accumulated in single), and *output* precision — three independent knobs where classic BLAS had one. That is precisely the precision hierarchy that mixed-precision factorization (HPL-14.2, HPL-14.4) and HPL-MxP (Module 16) rely on, now expressed in a general-purpose library API. The plugin system, meanwhile, is the framework philosophy made literal: BLIS isolates the performance-critical microkernels — small, architecture-specific inner loops for the multiply-accumulate core — so that optimizing one kernel instantly benefits the whole Level-3 family. This is the same "microkernel" architecture that HPL-9.7 will examine inside GEMM, and the reason a BLAS library's GEMM rate is the number from which all dense linear algebra performance follows (Section 1.4's dominant-kernel argument).

**Why it matters and how it connects.** HPL does not implement its own arithmetic: it calls a BLAS library (vendor BLAS on TOP500 systems — cuBLAS, oneMKL, and their AMD counterparts). The rate measured in Section 1.5's Rmax is, to first order, the GEMM rate of whatever BLAS the site chose, orchestrated by HPL's distributed algorithm. BLIS matters in that ecosystem as the readable open implementation: when Modules 9 and 17 dissect packing, microkernels, and mixed-precision accumulation, BLIS is a codebase you can actually open. The 2.0 release also lands at a moment when mixed-precision APIs are proliferating across vendors (cuBLASLt, oneMKL's mixed-precision GEMM), all serving the same demand HPL-MxP created: exploit the low-precision matrix engines, control the accuracy.

**What to watch next.** BLIS's plugin documentation and control-tree guide (`docs/PluginHowTo.md` in the repository) are excellent primary reading on how a modern Level-3 operation is actually assembled; and when Module 14 arrives, revisit this release with the precision hierarchy in hand — you will recognize every API decision.
