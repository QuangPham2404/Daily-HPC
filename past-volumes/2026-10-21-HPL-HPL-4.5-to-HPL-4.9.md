## 0. Concise Summary

Last week's HPL volume (2026-10-14) established the BLAS *contract* — the three-level taxonomy and why arithmetic intensity separates the levels. Today we open the toolbox and take apart the four kernels that dense linear algebra is actually built from: **AXPY**, **GEMV**, **GEMM**, and **TRSM**, then close with the general theory of **why Level-3 operations are the only ones that run near peak**.

The through-line is *data reuse per operand*. AXPY touches each vector element exactly twice and can never beat memory bandwidth. GEMV gives each matrix element exactly one scalar partner — reuse factor 1 — so it is still bandwidth-bound. GEMM gives each element of \(A\) a full block of columns of \(B\) to multiply against — reuse factor grows with the block size — which is what finally lets arithmetic, not data movement, set the speed. TRSM is the subtle one: it is triangular *substitution*, a Level-2-shaped recurrence, reorganized by blocking so that its bulk becomes GEMM work and it inherits Level-3 speed.

Quantitatively, we will derive that in blocked LU factorization the fraction of all FLOPs performed inside GEMM is \(1 - \frac{3}{2r} + O(r^{-2})\), where \(r = N/NB\) — for HPL-scale problems this exceeds 99.9%. HPL is, in FLOP terms, a GEMM benchmark with a small Level-1/2 tax collected on the critical path.

The developments today all illuminate these kernels directly: **cuBLAS 13.4** (September 2026) adds dynamic SM-scheduling for Grouped GEMM on Blackwell and extends emulated FP64 GEMM to the Rubin architecture at up to 212 TFLOP/s; **OpenBLAS 0.3.34** (July 2026) ships new TRSM kernels for RISC-V vector hardware and makes GEMM block sizes L2-cache-aware at runtime; and the historical development is the paper that codified how every modern GEMM is built — Goto and van de Geijn's *"Anatomy of High-Performance Matrix Multiplication"* (ACM TOMS, 2008).

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 HPL-4.5 — AXPY: the canonical streaming operation

**Definition.** AXPY is "\(\alpha\) times \(x\) plus \(y\)":

\[
y \leftarrow \alpha x + y, \qquad y_i \leftarrow \alpha x_i + y_i \quad \text{for } i = 1, \dots, n .
\]

The BLAS names are `SAXPY` (single precision), `DAXPY` (double), and `CAXPY`/`ZAXPY` (complex): one prefix letter, one operation. It is the smallest operation that has all three ingredients of a real BLAS kernel — a scalar parameter, a read-modify-write data dependence, and vector length as the only shape parameter.

**Cost model.** For length \(n\) in FP64: \(2n\) FLOPs (one multiply, one add per element), and \(3n\) memory words moved — read \(x\) (n), read \(y\) (n), write \(y\) (n) — which is \(24n\) bytes. The arithmetic intensity is therefore

\[
\frac{2n \text{ FLOPs}}{24n \text{ bytes}} = \frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083 \ \text{FLOP/byte},
\]

independent of \(n\). No problem size ever makes AXPY compute-bound. On a CPU with 2 TFLOP/s FP64 peak and 200 GB/s of bandwidth, the bandwidth ceiling is \(200 \times 0.083 \approx 17\) GFLOP/s — under 1% of peak. The kernel runs at the *speed of memory*, full stop. This is not an implementation failure; it is the operation's definition.

**Why it is worth studying anyway.** First, AXPY is the operation in which *machines are measured*: the STREAM Triad benchmark — \(a_i \leftarrow b_i + s \cdot c_i\) — is precisely an AXPY whose output aliases one input. Published STREAM Triad numbers are, to within naming, published AXPY bandwidths. When you estimate whether some phase of HPL is memory-bound, the Triad number is the yardstick.

Second, AXPY is the atom of Gaussian elimination. One unblocked elimination step reads

\[
\text{row}_i \leftarrow \text{row}_i - \ell_{ip} \cdot \text{row}_p ,
\]

which is \(y \leftarrow \alpha x + y\) with \(\alpha = -\ell_{ip}\), \(x\) the pivot row's trailing segment, and \(y\) the victim row's trailing segment. The reference unblocked LU kernel inside LAPACK (`DGETF2`, which bottoms out the recursive panel factorization) is assembled almost entirely from Level-1 calls — `IDAMAX` (pivot search), `DSWAP` (row exchange), `DSCAL` (multiplier computation), and the rank-1 update `DGER`, which is nothing but \(m\) parallel AXPYs sharing the same \(x\). The *slowest part of the fastest benchmark on Earth* is, at its innermost level, today's first kernel. That inversion — trivial arithmetic, dominant consequence — recurs in module 20 (HPL-20.2, the panel as the scalability limiter).

**Mechanism notes.** On a CPU, the loop body compiles to one fused multiply-add per element (\(y_i = \mathrm{fma}(\alpha, x_i, y_i)\)); a 512-bit vector unit retires 8 FP64 elements per instruction, so the loop is trivially vectorized — and still bandwidth-bound, because 8 elements per cycle of demand is exactly what the cores can generate and more than what a single core's cache path sustains. On a GPU, AXPY is the canonical "one thread per element" kernel; its performance is a direct measurement of achieved DRAM bandwidth, which is why it is everyone's first CUDA program. The interesting engineering questions are all about *streams*: keeping three concurrent read/write streams flowing (non-temporal stores can help when \(y\) will not be re-read), avoiding allocation delays, and — in the `DGER` variant — deciding how many rows to update per thread block so that \(x\) stays resident in registers or shared memory.

**A small worked example.** \(y \leftarrow 2x + y\) with \(x = (1, 2, 3, 4)\), \(y = (10, 20, 30, 40)\):

```
lane 0:  y0 = 2*1 + 10 = 12        lane 1:  y1 = 2*2 + 20 = 24
lane 2:  y2 = 2*3 + 30 = 36        lane 3:  y3 = 2*4 + 40 = 48
```

Four independent FMAs, zero cross-lane communication, one pass over memory. "Embarrassingly parallel" — and that is exactly the problem: with no reuse, parallelism cannot buy speed, only concurrency. Two threads halve neither time nor bandwidth cost per element; they merely share the same finite pipe. Keep this example in mind through 1.3, where the same four-element idea grows a third index and everything changes.

### 1.2 HPL-4.6 — GEMV: one equation, two algorithms

**Definition.** GEMV is general matrix-vector multiplication:

\[
y \leftarrow \alpha \, \mathrm{op}(A) x + \beta y, \qquad \mathrm{op}(A) \in \{A,\ A^T,\ A^H\},
\]

with \(A\) an \(m \times n\) matrix, \(x\) length \(n\) (or \(m\)), \(y\) length \(m\) (or \(n\)). Cost: \(2mn\) FLOPs; traffic ≈ \(8mn\) bytes (read \(A\) once, plus vectors), intensity ≈ 0.25 FLOP/byte — a factor 3 better than AXPY, and still a *constant*. GEMV is bandwidth-bound for life; on the reference CPU its ceiling is ~50 GFLOP/s, ~2.5% of peak.

What makes GEMV worth a full topic is that **one mathematical equation has two genuinely different loop structures**, and the choice between them is a data-layout decision, not a math decision. For \(y \leftarrow Ax\) with column-major \(A\) (the BLAS convention):

**Column-oriented ("AXPY") form.** Factor \(Ax = \sum_j A_{:,j}\, x_j\):

```
for j = 1 .. n:                # sweep columns of A left to right
    y += x[j] * A[:, j]        # an AXPY: y <- alpha*x_vec + y
```

Each iteration walks one column of \(A\) contiguously (column-major storage: stride 1), broadcasts the scalar \(x_j\), and accumulates into all of \(y\). Memory access is perfectly regular; \(x\) is read once; \(y\) is read and written \(n\) times but stays in cache because it is short.

**Row-oriented ("dot") form.** Compute each \(y_i\) independently:

```
for i = 1 .. m:                # sweep rows of A top to bottom
    y[i] = dot( A[i, :], x )  # a strided read: A[i,:] has stride m
```

Each output element is one dot product. But in column-major storage, a *row* of \(A\) has stride \(m\) — every element is a separate cache line — so on a CPU this form is a strided-access disaster unless the matrix is transposed or the rows are short. The two forms have identical FLOP counts and identical bandwidth floors; they differ in *which operand streams and which is random-accessed*, and therefore in achieved bandwidth.

**Why BLAS exposes `transa`.** The reference implementation (see netlib's `dgemv.f`) branches on `transa` and, for each branch, on whether the matrix or the vector is longer, choosing the loop structure that keeps the unit-stride operand innermost. The interface lets the *library* make the layout-aware choice once, correctly, for every caller. This is the BLAS contract of HPL-4.1 at work one level down: same math, multiple loop bodies, dispatch hidden behind a flag.

The transposed case \(y \leftarrow A^T x\) is not symmetric with the non-transposed one. In column-major storage, \(A^T x\) means dot products over *columns* of \(A\) — unit stride! — so the transposed GEMV is naturally dot-form, and the non-transposed one is naturally AXPY-form. One input flag silently selects between two different memory-behavior regimes; a programmer who hand-writes "the same" operation twice may see 2–5× differences. On GPUs the same split reappears as two different thread-mapping strategies (per-row warps doing strided loads vs. per-column blocks doing coalesced loads and reductions), and cuBLAS ships separate kernels for them. GEMV is the first place a dense-linear-algebra programmer meets the lesson that **loop structure is a performance property**, ahead of module 9 (HPL-9.5, loop ordering for GEMM).

**Where GEMV appears in the HPL/HPL-MxP world.** The residual computation of iterative refinement, \(r = b - A\hat{x}\) (HPL-15.2), is a GEMV when there is one right-hand side. Panel factorization on a tall-skinny matrix is "GEMV-shaped" in the sense of module 19 (HPL-19.2): one long dimension, one short one, limited blocking freedom in the short dimension. And the sparse cousin, SpMV, is the dominant kernel of iterative solvers — same intensity ceiling (0.25-ish), plus indirect addressing, which is why HPCG scores sit so far below HPL scores on every machine: HPCG's inner life is GEMV-class, HPL's is GEMM-class, and today's two topics are exactly that divide.

### 1.3 HPL-4.7 — GEMM: the engine

**Definition.** GEMM is general matrix-matrix multiplication:

\[
C \leftarrow \alpha \, \mathrm{op}(A)\,\mathrm{op}(B) + \beta C ,
\]

with \(\mathrm{op} \in \{\text{identity}, T, H\}\), \(A\) \(m \times k\), \(B\) \(k \times n\), \(C\) \(m \times n\). FLOP count: each of the \(mn\) elements of \(C\) is a length-\(k\) dot product — \(2k\) FLOPs — so

\[
\text{GEMM FLOPs} = 2mnk + (\text{mn for the }\beta C \text{ term, usually negligible}).
\]

For square \(n \times n \times n\): \(2n^3\). A single trailing-update GEMM of an HPL run at \(N = 10^6\), \(NB = 384\) (updating the \((10^6 - 384)^2\) trailing matrix by a rank-384 product) performs \(2 \times (999{,}616)^2 \times 384 \approx 7.7 \times 10^{14}\) FLOPs — one call, nearly an exaflop-second of work at Frontier-class speed.

**The interface is part of the concept.** The BLAS GEMM signature (Fortran order) is:

```
CALL DGEMM(TRANSA, TRANSB, M, N, K, ALPHA, A, LDA, B, LDB, BETA, C, LDC)
```

The two parameters that repay careful thought are the **leading dimensions** `LDA/LDB/LDC`. They say: "the matrix I am passing you lives inside a bigger array, with row-to-row (i.e., column-to-column) stride `LDA`." A submatrix view of a \(1000 \times 1000\) array starting at element \((601, 601)\) with size \(400 \times 400\) is passed as a pointer to `A(601,601)` with `LDA = 1000`. No copy is made. This is *the* mechanism that makes blocked algorithms expressible: when blocked LU (module 8) finishes a panel and updates the trailing submatrix, it calls GEMM on a view into the original allocation, in place, at zero packaging cost. Without leading dimensions, every block operation would require physical extraction of a contiguous block — an extra \(O(n^2)\) copy per step, and blocked LAPACK as we know it would not exist. (The price of views is that the *inner* dimensions of a view may be contiguous while rows are strided — one reason packed copies appear inside optimized GEMM, HPL-9.6.)

**Loop structure, briefly.** The naive triple loop

```
for i = 1 .. m
  for j = 1 .. n
    for k = 1 .. k:  C[i,j] += A[i,k] * B[k,j]
```

has six permutations (\(ijk, ikj, jik, jki, kij, kji\)), all with identical FLOP counts and wildly different memory behavior — which operand is streamed, which is re-read, which accumulates. In column-major storage the innermost loop should touch the first index (stride-1). Choosing well gets you a *correct* GEMM that is nevertheless several times slower than the library's; the full machinery — packing, register tiles, microkernels — is module 9's subject (HPL-9.2–9.8). Today's point is just that the gap between the naive loop and peak is not FLOPs; it is *traffic*, and the fix is blocking, which is section 1.5's subject.

**GEMM's share of LU: the number that explains HPL.** Blocked LU with block size \(NB\) on an \(N \times N\) matrix proceeds in \(r = N/NB\) steps. At step \(i\), with trailing size \(m_i = N - i \cdot NB\), the three phases cost approximately:

- panel factorization (unblocked LU on \(m_i \times NB\)): \(\;m_i NB^2 - NB^3/3\)
- triangular solve (TRSM on \((m_i - NB) \times NB\)): \(\;(m_i - NB)\,NB^2\)
- trailing update (GEMM of rank \(NB\)): \(\;2 (m_i - NB)^2 NB\)

Summing the GEMM term over all steps (a sum of squares, \(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1} j^2 = r(r-1)(2r-1)/6\)) gives the GEMM share of the total \(\frac{2}{3}N^3\) LU FLOPs:

\[
\boxed{\ \text{GEMM fraction} \;=\; 1 - \frac{3}{2r} + \frac{1}{2r^2}, \qquad r = \frac{N}{NB}.\ }
\]

Sanity checks: \(r = 2\) gives 37.5% (verify by hand on a \(2 \times 2\)-block matrix — it checks); \(r = 16\) gives 90.8%; and for HPL-typical values — \(N = 10^6\), \(NB = 384\), \(r \approx 2600\) — the GEMM fraction is **99.94%**. Panel and TRSM FLOPs are \(\approx N^2 NB\), a factor \(\sim 2N/(3NB)\) smaller than the GEMM's. Two consequences that organize everything ahead of us:

1. **HPL performance is GEMM performance.** A machine's HPL ceiling is its sustained-GEMM ceiling times a small correction. This is why every optimization chapter of this curriculum (modules 9, 17, 22, 23) keeps returning to GEMM.
2. **The non-GEMM work is not negligible in *time*.** It is small in FLOPs but low-intensity and dependency-chained, so it occupies the critical path disproportionately — the panel bottleneck and GPU starvation stories of module 20. FLOP share and time share are different currencies; confusing them is the classic HPL-tuning error.

**Precision and GEMM.** The FLOP count \(2mnk\) is precision-agnostic, but the hardware rate is not: on tensor-core GPUs the FP16/BF16 GEMM rate is 8–30× the FP64 rate (the staircase of HPL-3.10/3.3), with FP32 accumulation as the standard accuracy floor for mixed-precision use. Whether the *accumulation* happens at 32 bits while inputs are 16 — and what that does to the result — is the subject of modules 14 and 17. Today's developments section shows this axis moving in real products, including FP64 GEMM *synthesized from* narrow-precision GEMMs. One algorithmic footnote: asymptotically faster schemes (Strassen-class, \(O(n^{2.8})\)) exist, but production dense LU — including HPL — uses the classical algorithm, because blocking and tensor cores win more in practice than asymptotics do at feasible sizes, and because error-growth behavior is better understood.

### 1.4 HPL-4.8 — TRSM: substitution disguised as GEMM

**Definition.** TRSM solves triangular systems with multiple right-hand sides:

\[
\mathrm{op}(A) \cdot X = \alpha B \quad \text{or} \quad X \cdot \mathrm{op}(A) = \alpha B,
\]

where \(A\) is triangular (lower or upper, `UPLO`), possibly transposed/conjugated (`TRANSA`), with unit or non-unit diagonal (`DIAG`), and \(X\), \(B\) are \(n \times m\). It is the matrix-valued generalization of forward/backward substitution — or, read the other way, TRSV (Level 2) with the vectors replaced by matrices, which is literally how the Level-3 BLAS paper derives it from Level 2.

Cost: each element of \(X\) requires one FMA per earlier element in its row/column — \(n^2\) FLOPs per right-hand-side column, \(m \cdot n^2\) total for an \(n \times n\) triangle with \(m\) right-hand sides. Compare forming \(A^{-1}\) explicitly (\(\approx 2n^3\) FLOPs via LU plus \(n\) triangular solve pairs) and then multiplying (\(2mnk\) per use): for the square case the explicit-inverse route costs roughly 4× the FLOPs *per solve* — and it is numerically worse, which is the more important point.

**Never invert.** The classical analysis (Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, 2nd ed., SIAM 2002, ch. 14) shows that computing \(X = A^{-1}B\) as explicit-inverse-times-matrix can square the condition number in the forward-error bound — error growing like \(\kappa(A)^2 u\) — while substitution-based solves keep the dependence linear in \(\kappa(A)\). "Invert the matrix" is almost always both slower and less accurate than "solve the system"; TRSM exists precisely so that the fast, accurate route is also a Level-3 operation. This is a rule you will apply in every factorization code you ever write, and interviewers still trip people up with it.

**A worked example (unblocked, by hand).** Solve \(Lx = b\) with

\[
L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 15 \end{pmatrix}:
\quad x_1 = 6/2 = 3, \quad x_2 = (15 - 4 \cdot 3)/3 = 1.
\]

Forward substitution: each unknown uses all previously computed unknowns of its row. That per-element dependence on *earlier results in the same column* is why the naive algorithm cannot be a GEMM — the right-hand side of one FMA is produced by earlier FMAs.

**Blocking breaks the dependence chain into GEMM-sized pieces.** Partition \(L\) and the unknowns into \(NB \times NB\) blocks:

\[
\begin{pmatrix} L_{11} & 0 \\ L_{21} & L_{22} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{pmatrix}
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
\begin{aligned}
X_1 &= L_{11}^{-1} B_1 && \text{(recursive TRSM)}\\
X_2 &= L_{22}^{-1}\,\big(B_2 - L_{21} X_1\big) && \text{(GEMM update, then recursive TRSM)}
\end{aligned}
\]

Concretely with \(NB = 2\) on a \(4 \times 4\) system: solve the top-left \(2\times 2\) triangle by substitution (base case), compute the update \(B_2 \leftarrow B_2 - L_{21}X_1\) — a plain GEMM, \(2 \cdot 4 \cdot 4\) FLOPs here, dominating as sizes grow — then recurse on the bottom-right triangle. Recursion bottoms out at a small unblocked substitution (say \(NB \times NB\) with \(NB \sim 64\)–256), where the divisions live. Note what happened to the divisions: \(n^2\) of them in the unblocked algorithm become \(NB^2\) per base-case panel, i.e., \(O(n \cdot NB)\) total — divisions are slow, low-throughput operations on every architecture, and blocking amortizes them exactly as it amortizes data movement.

The FLOP accounting makes the payoff explicit: the base-case substitutions contribute \(O(n^2 NB)\) work while the GEMM updates contribute \(O(n^3)\) (for the square, many-RHS case). So **blocked TRSM is asymptotically a GEMM with a substitution tax** — the same shape as blocked LU itself, and the same lesson at a smaller scale: a recurrence (inherently serial, Level-2-flavored) is reorganized so that the serial part is \(O(1/r)\) of the total.

**Where TRSM sits in LU and HPL.** At each step of blocked LU, after the panel \(PB\)-columns are factored into \(L_{11}, U_{11}, L_{21}, U_{12}\), two TRSMs produce the off-diagonal blocks: \(L_{21} \leftarrow A_{21} U_{11}^{-1}\) and \(U_{12} \leftarrow L_{11}^{-1} A_{12}\) (in place, overwriting \(A\)'s storage — note `LDB` here is the *global* leading dimension, the 1.3 view trick again). In distributed HPL, TRSM is computed rank-locally on the row/column of the process grid that owns the relevant blocks (HPL-11.4), and its output feeds the trailing GEMM — so TRSM latency sits directly on the critical path between panel broadcast and the big update. TRSM is also where CPU BLAS libraries spend disproportionate engineering effort — it has GEMM's FLOPs in its updates but none of GEMM's regularity: recursive structure, triangular corner cases (`UPLO`/`DIAG`/side variants quadruple the code paths), and division-heavy base cases. Today's second development is direct evidence of that effort.

### 1.5 HPL-4.9 — Why Level-3 BLAS is fast: blocking as the universal mechanism

HPL-4.4 (2026-10-14) established *that* Level-3 intensity grows with problem size. Today's job is the *mechanism*: what a real implementation does to realize the \(n/12\) ideal, and what that costs. Four mechanisms, in decreasing order of importance.

**Mechanism 1: blocking multiplies reuse.** Start from the naive column-major GEMM in an order that re-reads \(B\) once per row of \(A\): traffic \(\sim 8n^3\) bytes, intensity \(\sim 0.25\) FLOP/byte — GEMM run naively is *as slow as GEMV*, a factor of \(n/3\) off the ideal. Now tile all three matrices into \(b \times b\) blocks and loop over block pairs, keeping the current block-column of \(A\) and block of \(B\) resident while a \(b \times b\) patch of \(C\) accumulates. Each fetched block of \(B\) is then reused for \(b\) columns of the \(C\)-patch, and each block of \(A\) for \(b\) rows: DRAM traffic collapses from \(O(n^3)\) to \(O(n^3/b)\), so achieved intensity becomes

\[
\text{intensity} \;\approx\; \frac{b}{4} \ \text{FLOP/byte} \quad (\text{one cache level, block } b),
\]

independent of \(n\) but *set by your block size versus your cache size* — this is the operational meaning of "surface-to-volume" (HPL-2.8). One level of blocking is not the end: register blocking (a \(m_R \times n_R\) tile of \(C\) held in registers, each \(A\)-fragment reused \(n_R\) times, each \(B\)-fragment \(m_R\) times) multiplies reuse again below L1; multi-level blocking (register tile × L1 tile × L2 panel) stacks reuse factors multiplicatively. Modern compute-to-bandwidth ratios are ~5 FLOP/byte (GPU FP64) to ~10–40 (CPU vector units, tensor cores at low precision reach 150+), and stacked reuse factors of 100–1000 are what lift GEMM from the bandwidth ceiling to 80–95% of arithmetic peak. **Blocking is not an optimization; it is what makes the \(n/12\) formula true at all.**

**Mechanism 2: two levels of parallelism, both inside the kernel.** A Level-3 operation offers parallelism *across* independent output blocks (different \(C\)-tiles are independent until a reduction) and *within* each block (SIMD lanes, threads, warps over the \(m\)- and \(n\)-dimensions of the microkernel). A Level-1/2 operation offers only the second. This is why a tuned Level-3 library scales across cores with no caller effort — the concurrency is structural, not scheduled — and why HPL can run one enormous GEMM per step across thousands of ranks with near-perfect balance (module 11).

**Mechanism 3: regularity.** A GEMM inner loop is branch-free, stride-predictable, and shape-uniform: perfect conditions for hardware prefetchers, software pipelining, DMA engines, and — decisively on modern hardware — fixed-shape matrix engines. Tensor cores and their CPU cousins (AMX, SME) execute *specific tile shapes* (e.g., \(16\times 8\) fragments); a GEMM structured so that its tiles match the hardware's shapes converts the entire trailing update into peak-rate matrix-engine work (HPL-17.3, 17.8). Irregularity anywhere — remainder blocks when \(N \bmod NB \neq 0\), tall-skinny panel shapes — breaks the tile mapping and pays scalar-path prices (HPL-19.4).

**Mechanism 4: the contract concentrates the engineering.** Because BLAS Level 3 is a stable interface over a huge, regular operation, a vendor can invest decades of kernel engineering *underneath it* — packing formats, microkernels per ISA, autotuned block sizes, split-K reductions, emulated-precision paths — and every LAPACK/HPL program inherits the payoff without recompiling. The 2026-10-14 volume called this "implementation freedom"; today's developments section shows it operating at full speed: the same `DGEMM` call site now dispatches to INT8-tensor-core emulation when that is faster at equal accuracy.

**What blocking costs.** None of this is free, and the costs shape the rest of the curriculum. (i) *Complexity migrates*: blocking makes the inner kernels fast by making the *outer* algorithm harder — panel factorization, critical paths, look-ahead (modules 8, 20). (ii) *Memory overhead*: packed copies of \(A\) and \(B\) panels require workspace beyond the \(n^2\) matrix itself — nontrivial at \(N = 10^6\), where the matrix alone is 8 TB and every extra buffer competes with the problem size you can fit (HPL-13.2). (iii) *Tuning burden*: block size becomes a first-class parameter trading panel frequency against GEMM efficiency — HPL's `NB` (HPL-12.4, 13.3), tuned per machine, and today's second development shows even OpenBLAS now adapts it to measured L2 size at runtime. (iv) *Shape sensitivity*: "Level 3" is a classification by operation, not a performance guarantee — a \(4096 \times 64 \times 4096\) product is formally GEMM but behaves partly like GEMV because the thin dimension starves blocking of reuse (HPL-9.9, 19.2).

### 1.6 The kernel census of one blocked-LU step

Assemble the day. One step of blocked LU on \(N = 4096\), \(NB = 256\) (\(r = 16\)), in kernel terms:

| Phase | Kernels at bottom | FLOPs (first step) | Share of total | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Panel factorization | `IDAMAX`, `DSWAP`, `DSCAL`, `DGER` (Lv 1–2) | \(\approx m NB^2 \approx 2.6\text{M}\) | ~2.9% (all steps) | latency, dependencies, pivoting |
| Triangular solves | TRSM → blocked substitution → GEMM updates | \(\approx m NB^2 \approx 2.6\text{M}\) | ~2.9% | divisions, recursion |
| Trailing update | GEMM → blocked → packed microkernels | \(\approx 2m^2 NB \approx 7.9\text{G}\) | 90.8% (all steps) | streaming, parallel, matrix engines |

(Shares from the boxed formula with \(r = 16\); panel and TRSM shares fall as \(\sim N^2 NB / N^3 = 1/r\), so at HPL scale they are thousandths of a percent of FLOPs — yet they anchor the runtime critical path, module 20's story.)

The five topics of today are the four bottom rows of that table plus the theory that explains the last column. AXPY is the atom of the panel; GEMV is the shape class of residuals and tall-skinny work; GEMM is 90–99.9% of the arithmetic; TRSM is the bridge that converts substitution into GEMM; and blocking is the mechanism that makes all of it run where the hardware is fastest. Next Wednesday (module 5) we climb one level: Gaussian elimination proper — how the panel's eliminations are derived, ordered, and counted.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 cuBLAS 13.4: Grouped GEMM becomes a scheduling problem, and emulated FP64 GEMM reaches 212 TFLOP/s on Rubin

**What happened.** CUDA Toolkit 13.4 (September 2026; NVIDIA blog September 9, release notes September 11) rolls out a cluster of GEMM-centric changes in cuBLAS/cuBLASLt. First, on Blackwell data-center GPUs, cuBLASLt now **dynamically schedules Grouped GEMM computations across streaming multiprocessors** to reduce load imbalance, improving performance by up to 20% for calls with many groups (e.g., 32), with NVIDIA explicitly targeting mixture-of-experts (MoE) workloads — building on CUDA 13.3's similar 20% gain for large problem sizes that exceed L2 cache. Second, **emulated FP64 matrix multiplication** (the Ozaki fixed-point scheme covered in our 2026-10-14 issue) now uses the improved Ozaki-II variant when beneficial (supported on Ampere and newer), reaching up to 175 TFLOP/s of emulated DGEMM on B200 and 295 TFLOP/s of ZGEMM; and it now supports the new **Rubin architecture (compute capability 10.7)**, where a new TI16 input type on the `tcgen05.mma` instruction lifts emulated DGEMM to **up to 212 TFLOP/s** and ZGEMM to 301 TFLOP/s. Third, the 13.0-line improvements included better non-GEMM Level-3 kernels — SYRK, HERK, **TRMM**, SYMM, HEMM in FP32/CF32 on Blackwell — the TRSM-adjacent operations of section 1.4.

**What technically changed.** Two different lessons. The Grouped GEMM scheduler is an admission that *families* of GEMMs of unequal shape do not self-balance the way one giant regular GEMM does: with many differently-sized matrix multiplications in flight, static partitioning leaves SMs idle, so the library now does runtime work-distribution — the same general principle as an HPL trailing update being trivially balanced while its panel is not (HPL-4.7's FLOP-share versus time-share distinction, and module 20's load-imbalance topics). The emulated-FP64 numbers are today's 1.3 precision footnote made concrete: one FP64 GEMM is decomposed into many narrow-precision GEMMs on tensor cores, with exponent alignment and mantissa slicing handled inside the kernel — HPL-3.10's trade implemented below the interface, invisible to the caller.

**Why it matters and what to watch.** For this curriculum: cuBLAS is progressively turning GEMM from a kernel into a *system* — heuristics, schedulers, precision-routing, all under one stable call — which is HPL-4.1's "contract" thesis proven at industrial scale. Watch whether emulated FP64 becomes the default path (NVIDIA has signaled moving it from opt-in), and whether TOP500's HPL rules engage the "what counts as FP64" question our 10-14 issue raised — 212 TFLOP/s is already in the range where a Rubin-class FP64 HPL submission's arithmetic could be substantially synthesized.

### 2.2 OpenBLAS 0.3.34: TRSM kernels for RISC-V, and block sizes that read the L2 cache

**What happened.** OpenBLAS 0.3.34 (July 16, 2026) is a dense catalog of exactly today's kernels. On riscv64: new optimized **STRSM** kernels and **D/C/ZTRSM** kernels (right-non-transposed and right-transposed cases) for RVV vector lengths 128/256B, GEMV speedups on two cores, and — the item to notice — **L2 cache size is now queried at runtime to scale GEMM block sizes**. On Arm: a significantly faster SGEMM kernel. Plus a fix for race conditions between concurrent Level-3 BLAS calls, and multithreading thresholds added or improved across a dozen Level-2/3 operations.

**What technically changed and why it connects.** Every line maps to a section of today's lesson. The TRSM work confirms 1.4's claim that TRSM is where BLAS engineering effort concentrates — the update-heavy kernel with awkward corner cases, now being brought to RVV hardware one triangular case at a time. The runtime-L2-aware block sizing is section 1.5's core mechanism made literal: block size versus cache size is the variable that sets GEMM's realized intensity (\(\approx b/4\) FLOP/byte per cache level), and OpenBLAS is moving from compile-time guesses to measured values. The Level-3 concurrency fix matters to HPL-style codes, where BLAS calls from inside OpenMP regions are routine: "GEMM is embarrassingly parallel *inside*" does not automatically mean "many concurrent GEMMs are safe" — thread-private buffers and lock discipline are part of the contract's fine print. And the multithreading thresholds are the AXPY/GEMV lesson in library form: below some size, threading overhead exceeds the work, because the operation is bandwidth-bound and bandwidth does not parallelize across cores on a single socket — so the library declines to try.

**What to watch.** Whether RISC-V server parts (and their RVV implementations) reach the GEMM efficiency where HPL-class runs become plausible on them; the memory-hierarchy-aware block-sizing trend spreading to TRSM and other Level-3 operations.

### 2.3 Historical development: Goto and van de Geijn codify how GEMM is actually built (2008)

**What happened.** Kazushige Goto and Robert van de Geijn's *"Anatomy of High-Performance Matrix Multiplication"* (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 34(3), Article 12, May 2008) documents the architecture of the DGEMM at the heart of GotoBLAS — the design that OpenBLAS inherited directly and that BLIS generalized into a framework. It remains the single most-cited practical reference for the machine that section 1.5 describes.

**What it established.** The paper's model, refined through successive approximations of the memory hierarchy, lands on the structure every high-performance GEMM still uses: pack a panel of \(A\) to be L2-resident (chosen so the panel's TLB footprint, not just its byte count, fits), stream \(B\) through L2 into registers, and perform the inner computation as a hand-written register-blocked microkernel with \(k\) as the streaming dimension. Two of its observations deserve permanence in your mental model. First, the ratio between FLOP rate and the L2-to-register streaming rate is small enough that streaming one operand from L2 is sustainable — i.e., *the hierarchy, not just the top level, is a design parameter*. Second, the **TLB, not cache capacity, often limits panel size** — virtual-memory translation is a real dense-linear-algebra constraint, which surprises everyone once. The paper also enumerates six possible inner-kernel shapes and argues one is inherently superior — the kind of exhaustive-but-principled enumeration that makes it a methods lesson as much as a results lesson.

**Why it belongs in today's issue.** Modules 9 and 17 of this curriculum (tiled GEMM, packing, microkernels, matrix-core tiles) are, in effect, a guided walkthrough of this paper's ideas one decade-plus of hardware later — tensor-core fragments now play the role the register file played in 2008, but the packing/streaming structure survives intact. Read it when you reach module 9; today, it is the proof that section 1.5's "mechanism" is not a textbook simplification but the actual shape of the code inside every BLAS you will ever link.
