## 0. Concise Summary

The two previous HPL volumes built the vocabulary of work and cost: operation counts, FLOPs versus FLOP/s, Rpeak/Rmax, and efficiency. Today we ask a sharper question — one that explains almost everything about why dense linear algebra is organized the way it is: **why does the amount of *arithmetic* in a matrix problem grow faster than the amount of *data*?**

The answer starts with two scaling laws. Matrix multiplication needs about 2n³ FLOPs (HPL-2.3) while the matrix itself occupies only 8n² bytes in FP64 (HPL-2.4). The consequence: as matrices grow, arithmetic grows like n, per byte stored, relative to data. A 10,000×10,000 matrix stores ~800 MB and performs ~2×10¹² FLOPs; multiply both by ten... the stored bytes grow ×100 but the FLOPs grow ×1000. Work outruns data — and, crucially, work outruns *memory traffic* only if we are clever about reusing data while it sits in fast memory. That is the whole game of high-performance dense linear algebra.

We formalize it as **compute work versus data movement** (HPL-2.5): two algorithms with identical FLOP counts — say, the classic three-loop matrix multiply and a cache-blocked one — can differ by an order of magnitude in runtime, because memory hierarchies charge per byte moved between levels, and the naive version moves far more bytes. **Arithmetic intensity** (HPL-2.6) is the ratio FLOPs/byte, the single number that predicts whether a kernel is compute-bound or memory-bound on a given machine via the roofline model — we treat it as a preview of PERF Theme 4, where the full model arrives. **Data reuse** (HPL-2.7) is the algorithmic lever: by computing on blocks small enough to sit in cache (or registers), the same matrix element feeds many FLOPs before it is evicted, so the expensive DRAM traffic is amortized across tens or hundreds of operations. Level-3 BLAS (matrix-matrix) is precisely the class of operations that makes this possible; Level-1/2 (vector and matrix-vector) cannot, which is why blocked algorithms exist at all.

In the developments section we go back to TOP500, this time armed with arithmetic intensity: the June 2026 list shows GPU systems whose HPL-MxP/HPL ratio is 8–11×, and we show that a B200-class GPU running FP16 tensor-core GEMM needs an arithmetic intensity of roughly 600 FLOP/byte to saturate — a number you physically cannot reach unless the kernel reuses data at block scale. Second: the Demmel–Grigori–Hoemmen–Langou "communication-optimal QR/LU" line of work, which turned today's intuition into theory, proving that conventional ScaLAPACK factorizations move asymptotically more data than necessary and giving algorithms that provably attain the data-movement lower bound. The lesson is general: on modern machines, data movement is the first-class cost; arithmetic is nearly free by comparison.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 The cubic law: why matrix multiplication is O(n³) (HPL-2.3)

Matrix multiplication is not expensive by accident — the cost is baked into the definition. C = A·B means every entry of C is the dot product of a full row of A with a full column of B:

$$c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} \, b_{kj}$$

There are n² entries in C, and each requires n multiply-add pairs — n multiplications and n−1 additions, conventionally counted together as ~2n FLOPs. Multiply the two factors:

$$T(n) \approx 2n^3 \text{ FLOPs}$$

A small concrete example makes the growth visceral. For n = 4, C has 16 entries, each a 4-term dot product: 64 multiplies, 48 adds ≈ 112 FLOPs ≈ 2·4³. Now step n up by just one, from n to n+1: you gain (n+1)³ − n³ = 3n² + 3n + 1 more "cubic units" of work. The *increment* is quadratic. Doubling n octuples the work: 2³ = 8.

**Where the cubic law comes from structurally.** There is a cleaner way to see it: three independent indices (i, j, k) each range over n values, and the triple loop nest executes once per (i, j, k) combination — n³ iterations. Dense linear algebra is full of such triple-nest structures: the trailing-matrix update in LU (a GEMM), Cholesky, QR updates. This is why the dominant cost of dense LU is not the elimination itself but the rank-nb updates of the remaining matrix — they are matrix multiplications, and they inherit the cubic count.

**Deriving the connection to LU.** In the last theme volume we quoted LU as (2/3)n³ FLOPs. That number is the integral of shrinking GEMMs: at elimination step k, the trailing submatrix is (n−k)×(n−k), and updating it costs ~2(n−k)² FLOPs (one rank-1 update per eliminated column). Summing over k:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} 2(n-k)^2 = 2\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} m^2 \approx 2 \cdot \frac{n^3}{3} = \frac{2}{3}n^3$$

So the (2/3)n³ of LU is not a separate fact — it is the cubic law of matrix multiplication, applied to a sequence of progressively smaller rank-1 updates. The n³/3 comes from the shrinking trailing matrix; the factor 2 from multiply+add per term. Keep this picture: **dense factorization = a sequence of GEMM-like updates**, which is why everything we say about GEMM's data behavior below applies to HPL as a whole.

**Can we beat n³?** Yes — asymptotically. Strassen's algorithm (1969) multiplies via a divide-and-conquer scheme using 7 recursive multiplications of half-size matrices instead of 8, giving T(n) = O(n^2.807). The exponent has since crept down (currently below 2.373 in theory), but practical fast-multiplication methods see little use in HPC beyond special cases: extra memory, worse numerical error behavior, and communication patterns that fight the network. Crucially, the TOP500 rules explicitly exclude such algorithms — the benchmark's operation count must be (2/3)n³ + O(n²) in conventional arithmetic, which keeps results comparable across systems. For an HPC engineer, O(n³) is the working law; sub-cubic methods are a research sidebar you should know exists.

### 1.2 The quadratic law: why matrix storage is O(n²) (HPL-2.4)

A matrix is a two-dimensional object with n rows and n columns: n² entries. In FP64 (8 bytes per entry), a dense n×n matrix occupies

$$S(n) = 8n^2 \text{ bytes}$$

This is the *whole* memory story for HPL: the benchmark allocates essentially one matrix of size N (plus modest workspace), so the memory capacity of a machine directly caps the largest N it can run — and since runtime ~ (2/3)N³/R, memory size indirectly sets the runtime. Practical numbers from the FP64 world:

| n | Storage (8n² bytes) | LU work (2/3·n³ FLOPs) |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 0.8 GB | 6.7×10¹¹ |
| 100,000 | 80 GB | 6.7×10¹⁴ |
| 1,000,000 | 8 TB | 6.7×10¹⁷ |
| 2,000,000 | 32 TB | 2.7×10¹⁸ |

Read that table as a system-design constraint: a single node with 256 GB of RAM tops out around n ≈ 165,000 (using ~80% of memory, the usual HPL sizing guideline). A cluster needs the matrix *distributed* (Theme 2's lesson: aggregate many nodes' memory), and an exascale HPL run at N ≈ 2,000,000 pushes 32 TB — which is why TOP500-class HPL runs are exercises in distributed memory management as much as in compute.

**The ratio is the story.** Divide work by storage:

$$\frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{bytes stored}} \approx \frac{(2/3)n^3}{8n^2} = \frac{n}{12} \text{ FLOP/byte}$$

For n = 100,000, that's ~8,300 FLOP per stored byte. If every stored byte had to travel from DRAM for every single operation that uses it, matrix multiplication would be hopeless — no machine has 8,000× more compute throughput than memory bandwidth. The *only* way dense algorithms work on real hardware is to ensure each byte, once fetched, serves many FLOPs. That single sentence is the thesis of the next three topics and, in a deep sense, of the entire blocked/distributed design of LAPACK, ScaLAPACK, and HPL. (Historical footnote: this imbalance is ancient — the CRAY-1's vector registers existed precisely to let data be fetched once and used many times; caches on every CPU since serve the same role.)

### 1.3 Compute work versus data movement (HPL-2.5)

The runtime of a program is not determined by its FLOP count alone. On a modern processor, each level of the memory hierarchy has its own cost per byte moved:

```
registers  →  L1  →  L2  →  L3/LLC  →  DRAM/HBM  →  network
 faster, smaller, cheaper per access          slower, larger, costlier per access
```

A useful first-order model of runtime — the one used throughout the communication-avoiding literature (Demmel et al.) — is:

$$T \approx \gamma \cdot \text{FLOPs} \;+\; \beta \cdot \text{words} \;+\; \alpha \cdot \text{messages}$$

- γ (time per FLOP) shrinks with every generation — on a B200, an FP64 tensor-core FLOP takes on the order of 10⁻¹⁴ s;
- β (time per word moved from DRAM) falls much more slowly;
- α (per-message latency) barely falls at all.

The gaps are enormous and widening: time-per-FLOP has historically improved ~26%/year, memory bandwidth ~15%/year, and network latency ~5–15%/year (the ITRS/FOSC numbers cited by Demmel). When γ ≪ β, the *arithmetic is nearly free*; what you pay for is moving data. Two algorithms with the same FLOP count can therefore have wildly different runtimes — the one that moves fewer bytes wins.

**Worked example: the naive three-loop multiply.** Consider the classic i-j-k loop order computing C = A·B with all matrices stored row-major. The inner loop over k reads A[i][k] (sequential in memory — good) and B[k][j] (striding down a column — every access a different cache line in row-major storage). For each of the n³ inner iterations you touch 2 operands; if B's column accesses miss cache, you re-fetch B's data n times over the whole run. Total traffic ~ O(n³) words, for 2n³ FLOPs: **~1 FLOP per word** — catastrophic on any machine whose compute-to-bandwidth ratio exceeds 1. By contrast, a cache-blocked version (next topic) moves only O(n³/√M) words, where M is the fast-memory capacity — the same FLOPs, asymptotically less traffic. This is not a small constant-factor effect; it is a change in the *exponent* of data movement, and it is the difference between a GEMM that runs at 5% of peak and one that runs at 80–90%.

**Sequential versus parallel data movement.** The same cost structure appears twice in HPC: *within* a node (between DRAM and cache/registers — the sequential case) and *between* nodes (over the network — the parallel case). The β·words + α·messages model covers both: on the network, per-word cost is bandwidth-limited, per-message cost is latency. An HPC engineer optimizes both with the same mental tool: **increase the number of FLOPs performed per byte/message sent**. Within a node, that means blocking for cache; between nodes, it means sending larger, fewer messages — the deep reason why distributed dense LA distributes matrices in large contiguous blocks (block-cyclic distribution, coming in Module 10) rather than element by element.

### 1.4 Arithmetic intensity (HPL-2.6)

If runtime = max(arithmetic cost, data cost) in the coarse sense, we want a single number that tells us which side dominates. That number is the **arithmetic intensity** (also "operational intensity"):

$$I = \frac{\text{total FLOPs}}{\text{total bytes moved}} \quad [\text{FLOP/byte}]$$

Strictly: bytes moved *from the memory level we're worried about* — usually DRAM/HBM for GPU kernels, since registers and cache are the fast layers above it.

Compute the intensity of the canonical BLAS operations on n×n matrices in FP64:

| Operation | FLOPs | Bytes (DRAM, ideal) | Intensity |
|---|---|---|---|
| AXPY (y ← αx + y), Level 1 | 2n | 3·8n (read x, y; write y) | 1/12 ≈ 0.083 |
| GEMV (y ← A·x), Level 2 | 2n² | 8n² + 16n | → 1/4 |
| GEMM (C ← A·B), Level 3 | 2n³ | 3·8n² (read A, B; write C) | n/12 |

The three BLAS levels are not an API convention — they are three *regimes* of arithmetic intensity. Level 1 does ~0.08 FLOP per byte: no amount of hardware makes it fast; it is forever bandwidth-limited. Level 2 asymptotes to 0.25 FLOP/byte (every matrix element, read once, feeds exactly one multiply-add into the output). Level 3 grows *without bound* as n grows — each A element feeds n dot products, each B element feeds n dot products, each C element accumulates n terms.

**The roofline in one paragraph (preview of Theme 4).** A machine has a peak compute rate π (FLOP/s) and a peak bandwidth b (byte/s from the relevant memory). A kernel with intensity I can achieve at most min(π, b·I) FLOP/s: if I < π/b (the *ridge point*), the kernel is memory-bound and performance = b·I; if I > π/b, it is compute-bound and performance saturates at π. On an NVIDIA B200: FP64 tensor core π ≈ 40 TFLOP/s, HBM bandwidth b ≈ 8 TB/s, so the FP64 ridge point is 40/8 = **5 FLOP/byte**; for FP16/BF16 tensor cores (π ≈ 5,000 TFLOP/s dense), the ridge is 5000/8 ≈ **625 FLOP/byte** (these are per-GPU figures from NVIDIA's Blackwell datasheet). Two consequences follow immediately. First: GEMV (I ≈ 0.25) is memory-bound on *every* machine ever built — its speed is bandwidth/4, period. Second: to use FP16 tensor cores you must sustain ~625 FLOP/byte — but a full-matrix GEMM read-once-from-DRAM achieves only n/12, which reaches 625 only at n ≈ 7,500... *if* the ideal DRAM traffic were achievable, and only if the whole matrices can't fit in fast memory. For smaller n — or for any kernel that streams data — you must manufacture intensity by reuse, which is the next topic. (The full roofline model — axes, ceilings, hierarchical variants, and its limitations — is Module 5 of the PERF theme; today we only need the ridge-point arithmetic.)

**A subtlety worth internalizing.** Arithmetic intensity is a property of the *algorithm as implemented*, not of the mathematical operation. "GEMM is O(n) intensity" assumes each matrix element crosses the DRAM boundary once. A poorly implemented GEMM — bad loop order, no blocking — moves each element many times and has effective intensity far below n/12. Intensity is thus a *diagnosis knob*: measure a kernel's achieved FLOP/s and bytes moved (e.g., via counters), compute I, compare with the machine's ridge point, and you know which roof is pinning you and how much headroom remains.

### 1.5 Data reuse (HPL-2.7)

Reuse is the mechanism by which algorithms manufacture arithmetic intensity. The principle:

> **Bring a block of data into fast memory once; perform as much work with it as possible before evicting it.**

**The 3×3 block example.** Partition C = A·B into 3×3 grids of b×b blocks (b = n/3). The block formula mirrors the scalar one — C₁₂ = Σₖ A₁ₖ·Bₖ₂ — but now the "scalars" are b×b submatrices, and the inner product of blocks is itself a small GEMM. Consider computing the *entire* C block-row C₁,: you load A's block-row once (3b² elements), then for each of the 3 block-columns you sweep all of B's block-rows. Alternatively — and this is the key — fix the A block A₁ₖ *once* in cache and use it against *all* B block-columns k-th rows and all C entries in row 1: each cached A-block element now participates in b multiply-adds against every element of a full B block-row. The general result: with a three-level blocking where the working set b×b blocks fit in fast memory of size M words, traffic drops to

$$W(n) = \Theta\!\left(\frac{n^3}{\sqrt{M}}\right) \text{ words}$$

versus Θ(n³) for the naive nest — the classical Hong–Kung/Irony–Toledo–Tiskin result. The ratio of FLOPs to *actual* traffic is then Θ(√M) FLOP/word: with M = 32 MB of L3 cache (4M words FP64), that's ~2,000 FLOP/word ≈ 16,000 FLOP/byte of potential intensity — comfortably above any DRAM-based ridge point, which is why *large blocked GEMMs run near peak* on CPUs and GPUs. The famous practical corollary: to make GEMM fast, choose block sizes so that a panel of A and a row of B-blocks fit in L2/L1 (CPUs) or shared memory (GPUs) while the C tile lives in registers. In GPU terms: a 128×128 C-tile held in registers, fed by A and B tiles staged in shared memory — every A element loaded from DRAM is reused 128 times before eviction.

**Reuse in factorizations — the deeper reason for blocking.** Blocked LU (Module 8) factors a narrow panel, then updates the trailing matrix as a GEMM. The panel operations themselves (rank-1 updates, small GEMVs) are low-intensity — the *trailing update* is where the FLOPs and the reuse live. This is why LAPACK's blocked algorithms exist: not as an implementation trick, but to convert what would be O(n²)-intensity work into Level-3, high-reuse work. The curriculum's Module 8 (blocked LU) and Module 9 (GEMM as the performance engine) are, structurally, the systematic exploitation of today's topic.

**Where reuse comes from structurally** — three sources, worth naming:
1. **Temporal reuse of operands**: A and B elements used many times (GEMM's k-dimension).
2. **Spatial reuse via contiguity**: reading consecutive addresses amortizes each cache line / DRAM burst across multiple elements. Row-major C = A·B^T keeps all three streams sequential — one reason HPL's tuning guide recommends the transposed-U storage so the multiply is called as C := C − A·Bᵀ.
3. **Accumulator reuse**: the C tile is read once, accumulated n times in registers, written once.

**The cost side: why not block infinitely?** Larger blocks → more reuse → less traffic, but: (a) fast memory is finite (b must fit), (b) panels get harder to factor as NB grows (more low-intensity panel work per column — the pivot-search/swap costs grow), (c) parallelism and load balance suffer if blocks get too coarse relative to the number of workers. HPL's tuning notes put it plainly: good NB is "almost always in the [32..256] interval," balancing data-reuse gains against panel overhead and message counts. The optimal block size is a *machine-dependent equilibrium* — exactly the kind of coupled trade-off Module 13 will treat systematically.

**Connection across themes.** GPU shared memory and register files exist to create reuse layers (Theme 1); the network-versus-arithmetic gap makes the same argument at cluster scale, where block-cyclic distribution creates "reuse" across ranks by keeping communications large and few (Theme 2); the roofline model (Theme 4, Module 5) quantifies it; and HPL-MxP's FP16 path pushes the compute roof 125× higher, making the reuse requirement 125× more stringent — conversion, scaling, and panel costs that were negligible at FP64 speeds become first-order at FP16 tensor-core speeds. One idea, every scale.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 Arithmetic intensity at exascale: reading the June 2026 TOP500/HPL-MxP gap with today's tools

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 (June), the 67th TOP500 list debuted China's LineShine at No. 1 with 2.198 Exaflop/s on HPL (FP64), while El Capitan retained the HPL-MxP (mixed-precision) crown at 16.7 Exaflop/s — a 9.2× ratio over its own HPL score; Aurora's ratio is 11.5×, Frontier's 8.4×. LineShine, a CPU-only system, shows a much smaller 3.6× ratio (7.92 Exaflop/s MxP). The full list's combined HPL performance reached 18.73 Eflop/s across 500 systems.

**What technically changed / was demonstrated.** The HPL→MxP gap is a direct, publicly visible measurement of the ridge-point arithmetic from today's lesson. HPL-MxP factors in FP16-class precision on tensor/matrix cores and recovers FP64-accurate solutions via iterative refinement; on GPU systems whose low-precision peak is ~125× the FP64 peak, the *compute roof* rises by two orders of magnitude. Today's analysis explains why the gap lands at 8–11× rather than 125×: once compute is that fast, intensity requirements explode (on B200-class silicon, ~625 FLOP/byte for FP16 — vs 5 FLOP/byte for FP64), and the pipeline saturates on data movement instead: FP16 conversion kernels, scaling operations, panel factorizations (which stay low-intensity), and inter-rank communication. The 3.6× CPU-only figure is the control experiment: without a tall low-precision compute roof, the intensity bar stays low and less is lost to the bandwidth side.

**Why it matters / what to watch.** This is the benchmark-level expression of a decade-long architectural trend: compute scaling (via wider and lower-precision matrix engines) has far outpaced bandwidth scaling, so *realizable* performance increasingly depends on how much reuse an algorithm can manufacture. Watch the November 2026 list for whether GPU systems' MxP ratios grow (more matrix-engine headroom exploited) or stall (bandwidth-bound), and note that HPL-MxP's iterative-refinement validation — the criterion that keeps the mixed-precision result honest — is itself on the curriculum path (Modules 14–15).

### 2.2 The communication-avoiding revolution: when "data movement is the cost" became a theorem

**What happened.** In work culminating around 2008–2012, James Demmel, Laura Grigori, Mark Hoemmen, and Julien Langou published "Communication-optimal parallel and sequential QR and LU factorizations" (SIAM J. Sci. Comput., 2012), formalizing the program Demmel had been advocating: algorithms should be designed to minimize *communication*, not just FLOPs.

**What technically changed.** Two things. First, **lower bounds**: extending Hong–Kung-style arguments (and Irony–Toledo–Tiskin for the parallel case), they proved that any conventional Θ(n³) matrix-multiplication-based algorithm — including LU and QR — must move at least Ω(n³/√M) words sequentially (M = fast memory) and Ω(n²/√p) per processor in parallel, plus corresponding message-count lower bounds. Second, **matching algorithms**: TSQR (tall-skinny QR via a reduction over row blocks) and CAQR attain these bounds up to polylogarithmic factors — same numerical stability as Householder QR, asymptotically less communication. Standard LAPACK/ScaLAPACK routines, they showed, communicate asymptotically more than the bound — the gap is not a constant; it grows with problem size.

**Why it matters.** Today's lesson derived the Θ(n³/√M) blocked-GEMM traffic bound by reuse arguments; this line of work established that it is *optimal* — you cannot do better within conventional algorithms — and then built factorizations that hit it. The consequences are now everywhere in HPC software: communication-avoiding LU/QR in ScaLAPACK descendants, the 2.5D/3D algorithm family (HPL-18.6), and even the structure of modern HPL-MxP implementations, where look-ahead and panel reorganization exist to keep the critical path off the communication side. It also reframed performance thinking: since ~2010, "optimize the FLOPs" stopped being the frontier; "minimize and overlap data movement" became the dominant lens — precisely the β·words + α·messages worldview from Section 1.3. (The U.S. DOE's FY2012 budget request to Congress even cited communication-avoiding algorithms as a priority — a rare policy-level cameo for numerical linear algebra.)

**What to watch/understand next.** When we reach HPL-18 (communication-avoiding LU) and HPL-11–12 (distributed factorization, look-ahead), the "panel is the bottleneck" and "broadcast on the critical path" phenomena will be the practical face of these bounds. The Demmel lecture-sphere also connects to the "Flash" FFT and stencil projects under the same communication-minimizing umbrella.
