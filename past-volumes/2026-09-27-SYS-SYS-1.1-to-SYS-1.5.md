## 0. Concise Summary

Today this curriculum opens its fifth and final theme: **Broader HPC Systems**. Themes 1–4 built the pieces — processor and GPU architecture, multi-node communication, dense numerical algorithms, and optimization methodology. SYS assembles those pieces into the machine you actually log into: a complete system with nodes, networks, schedulers, storage, and people operating it around the clock.

Five concepts are covered. First, what actually makes a computer an *HPC system* — not a marketing label but a definable design point: extreme concurrency at every level, memory and interconnect sized to feed that concurrency, and balance between subsystems. Second, the scale ladder from workstation to server to cluster to supercomputer, and what qualitatively changes at each rung. Third, the anatomy of a compute node: CPUs, accelerators, memory, NICs, local storage, and the baseboard management controller that makes thousands of nodes remotely operable. Fourth, why production clusters strictly separate login, service, and compute node roles — why your compile job runs on one machine and your 10,000-rank MPI job on another. Fifth, the cluster fabric: not one network but several deliberately separated ones — compute, storage, management, and site access.

The unifying idea: **an HPC system is a system of systems.** The node is the atomic unit of computation; the fabric is what turns thousands of nodes into one machine; role separation and the management plane are what keep that machine usable by hundreds of simultaneous users without mutual interference. Every kernel you optimized in earlier modules executes inside this structure, and the structure explains constraints you have felt but perhaps never named — queue waits, sluggish login nodes, filesystem hiccups under load.

Three developments connect directly to today's topics. The June 2026 TOP500 (announced at ISC 2026 in Hamburg) crowned a new No. 1 — China's CPU-only **LineShine** at 2.198 exaflop/s — a striking demonstration that a supercomputer is a *system-integration* achievement, not a chip. Europe signed the **€387.8 million LUMI-AI** procurement with Bull (AMD Instinct MI430X nodes, deployment H2 2027). And Huawei's **Atlas 960E SuperPoD** pushed cluster-fabric engineering to near-package optics, betting that at 10-trillion-parameter scale the fabric — not the chip — is the computer.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### SYS-1.1 — What makes a computer an HPC system

Start with what does *not* define an HPC system: raw clock speed, a vendor badge, or the word "supercomputer" on a spec sheet. A gaming PC with a high-end GPU can out-FLOP a decade-old terascale cluster in raw single-GPU throughput. What defines an HPC system is a **design point**: the machine is engineered to sustain high throughput on *parallel technical workloads* — simulation, linear algebra, data analysis at scale — and every subsystem is sized relative to every other subsystem to serve that goal.

Concretely, an HPC system is characterized along these dimensions:

- **Peak arithmetic throughput (FLOP/s), with precision stated.** A FLOP/s figure is meaningless without its precision (FP64, FP32, FP16, FP8, or tensor-core conditions) — a distinction you already know from the HPL vs. HPL-MxP discussion in the HPL theme. A modern accelerator can be an order of magnitude faster at FP8 than at FP64; which number a vendor quotes is a marketing decision.
- **Concurrency at every level.** Instruction-level parallelism and SIMD within a core; multiple cores per socket; multiple sockets per node; GPUs with tens of thousands of threads; thousands to millions of processes across nodes. The entire software stack — compilers, MPI, schedulers — exists to express and manage this concurrency.
- **Memory capacity *and* bandwidth.** Capacity determines problem size; bandwidth determines how fast you can actually stream data through the machine. You met this distinction in the PERF theme's roofline reasoning: a kernel with high arithmetic intensity is compute-bound, one with low intensity is memory-bound, and most real codes live somewhere in between.
- **Interconnect.** On a single node, PCIe/CXL and NVLink-class links; across nodes, the system-area network whose latency and bisection bandwidth determine whether a 100,000-process job behaves like one machine or like 10,000 quarreling ones. The DIST theme covered what runs *on* this network (MPI, collectives, NCCL); today we look at the network itself.
- **Storage.** Checkpoint/restart of huge jobs, input datasets, and output analysis require aggregate bandwidth that a single fileserver can never deliver — hence parallel filesystems (a full SYS module later).
- **Workload characteristics.** Dense linear algebra, sparse solvers, stencils, FFTs, particle methods, and AI training each stress different proportions of compute, memory, and network. A machine is "an HPC system for X" to the extent its balance matches X.

The concept that ties these together is **balance**. Compute, memory bandwidth, network bandwidth, storage bandwidth, and power must scale together; over-provision one and the others become bottlenecks. A vivid, current illustration comes from the June 2026 TOP500 and HPCG lists. El Capitan sustains 1,809 PFlop/s on HPL but only 17.41 PFlop/s on HPCG — about **0.96%**. The new No. 1, LineShine, scores 2,198 PFlop/s HPL and 22.00 PFlop/s HPCG — about 1.0%. HPCG (sparse, latency-sensitive, communication-heavy) and HPL (dense, compute-bound) bracket the workload space, and *no* HPL-optimized machine — from any vendor, on any continent — escapes the sparse-workload wall. That is not a failure of engineering; it is what it means for a system to be balanced for one class of workload. Keep this ratio in mind whenever you see a peak-FLOP/s headline: it characterizes exactly one point in workload space.

### SYS-1.2 — Workstation, server, cluster, and supercomputer

The four rungs of the ladder differ less in "speed" than in *organization* — who owns the machine, how many users it serves, how it fails, and how it is operated.

| | Workstation | Server | Cluster | Supercomputer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Users | 1, interactive | Services for many | 10s–1000s of users | 100s–1000s of users, via projects |
| Composition | 1–2 sockets, maybe 1 GPU | 1–2 sockets, high reliability | 10s–10,000s of commodity nodes + network + scheduler + shared storage | 1000s–10,000s of custom-integrated nodes, custom interconnect, liquid cooling |
| Network | PCIe, Ethernet | Ethernet | Ethernet or IB/commodity fabric | Engineered fabric (Slingshot, BXI, InfiniBand DragonFly+, proprietary) |
| Failure model | Reboot it yourself | Redundant PSUs, ECC, hot-swap | Nodes fail routinely; scheduler routes around them | Failure is a statistical certainty; designed for it |
| Procurement | Retail | Vendor quote | Nodes + switches + software stack | Multi-year contract, acceptance benchmarks |

**Workstation.** One user, interactive latency as the optimization target, and a single OS instance you administer yourself. All the parallelism available is what fits in one box: a few cores, one or two GPUs. Everything is shared with your text editor and browser — which is fine, because there is only one of you.

**Server.** A server is not a fast computer; it is a *dependable* one. Registered ECC memory, redundant power supplies, out-of-band management (more on the BMC below), and packaging for racks rather than desks. A server is essentially a node *waiting for a cluster* — and the moment you put many of them behind a scheduler and a shared filesystem, that is exactly what it becomes.

**Cluster.** A cluster is the composition pattern: many commodity nodes, a network that connects them, a scheduler that allocates them, a shared filesystem, and a management plane that provisions and monitors them. The insight that made clusters dominant is historical: NASA's 1994 **Beowulf** project demonstrated that commodity PCs linked by commodity Ethernet could deliver serious FLOP/s per dollar, displacing the idea that HPC required exotic vector mainframes. Nothing about the individual nodes is special; the *system built from them* is. A three-node lab cluster with one head node and Ethernet — like a home testbed — has exactly the same logical anatomy as a top-10 system, just scaled down. That is why clusters are the right mental model to learn first: the concepts transfer upward unchanged.

**Supercomputer.** A supercomputer is what happens when the cluster pattern is pushed until *every* subsystem must be engineered together. The differences are qualitative:

- **Custom interconnect.** El Capitan uses HPE Slingshot 11; JUPITER uses NVIDIA InfiniBand NDR in a DragonFly+ topology; LUMI-AI will use Bull's BXI. At millions of cores, the network — its topology, its routing, its congestion behavior — is as much a part of the machine's identity as its processors. (LineShine's LingQi interconnect and Kylin OS show the same pattern from China.)
- **Custom packaging and cooling.** El Capitan's MI300A nodes live in liquid-cooled HPE EX4000 cabinets; JUPITER and LUMI-AI are direct-liquid-cooled. Air cooling physically cannot remove heat at modern rack densities — a constraint that feeds back into processor design itself.
- **Near-node storage tiers.** El Capitan's "Rabbit" NVMe nodes and JUPITER's ExaFLASH tier exist because pulling checkpoint I/O through the general-purpose storage fabric at exascale rates does not work.
- **System-level procurement.** Supercomputers are bought as complete machines under multi-year contracts with acceptance benchmarks — El Capitan via the DOE CORAL-2 procurement; JUPITER (€500M total budget) from the ParTec-Eviden consortium via EuroHPC JU. The buyer purchases a *system*, including the building modifications, cooling plant, and often the software stack.

The takeaway: "cluster" is an architecture pattern; "supercomputer" is the extreme of that pattern where integration, not componentry, is the product.

### SYS-1.3 — Compute nodes

The compute node is the atomic unit of an HPC system — the smallest machine the scheduler hands out, and the level at which most architecture decisions you studied in the GPU theme play out. A modern node contains:

**CPUs and memory.** One or two sockets, each a NUMA domain with its own memory controllers. Do the bandwidth arithmetic once, because it explains almost everything about node design. A current server socket with twelve DDR5-4800 channels provides 12 × 38.4 GB/s ≈ **461 GB/s** of theoretical memory bandwidth, shared by every core on the socket. Share that across, say, 96 cores and each core can stream under 5 GB/s. Now compare a GPU: HBM stacks deliver *multi-terabyte-per-second* bandwidth to thousands of lanes of data-parallel work. That bandwidth gap — not FLOP/s — is the core reason HPC went heterogeneous. Floating-point units are cheap; feeding them is not.

**Accelerators.** Three integration styles, in increasing coupling:

1. *Discrete GPU over PCIe* — the classic model; CPU and GPU have separate memories, with explicit copies (or unified memory) across PCIe.
2. *Coherent superchips* — NVIDIA Grace-Hopper (JUPITER's 5,884 Booster nodes) couples an Arm CPU and an H100-class GPU with a high-bandwidth coherent link.
3. *True APUs* — AMD MI300A puts 24 Zen 4 CPU cores, a CDNA 3 GPU, and 128 GB of HBM3 on one package with one coherent memory. El Capitan's node is *four* MI300A packages: 96 CPU cores, 4 GPUs, and 512 GiB of HBM per node, with no "host vs. device" copy at all — the CPU cores and GPU compute units address the same physical memory.

**Network interface cards.** An HPC NIC is not a consumer Ethernet card. It is a DMA engine with protocol offload: it moves data between remote memory and local memory with no CPU involvement (RDMA — covered in the DIST theme), exposes queue pairs, and sustains hundreds of gigabits per second of *injection bandwidth* (the rate at which a single node can push data into the fabric). The ratio of injection bandwidth per node to compute per node is one of the defining balance parameters of the whole machine.

**Local storage.** Node-local or near-node NVMe serves as scratch space and staging: El Capitan pairs compute cabinets with "Rabbit" NVMe arrays; JUPITER layers ExaFLASH in front of its bulk tiers. The pattern — a fast, small tier absorbing I/O bursts before they hit the shared filesystem — recurs at every scale and will get a full module of its own.

**The baseboard management controller (BMC).** Inside every server is a second, small computer that runs even when the main OS is dead. It exposes remote power control, serial console, sensor readings, and firmware management over the management network, via protocols like IPMI and Redfish. The BMC is why an operator can reinstall the OS on 11,424 nodes without touching a keyboard, and why a node with a kernel panic can still be power-cycled and rebooted — the control path is *hardware*, independent of the software it manages. When SYS-1.5 describes the out-of-band network, this is the device at the end of it.

The node is also the scheduler's atomic unit. When you request resources, you are ultimately requesting *nodes* (or slices of nodes), and properties of the node — cores per socket, memory per node, GPUs per node, NIC count — are the vocabulary of every job script you have ever written.

### SYS-1.4 — Login, service, and compute nodes

If you SSH into a production cluster, you land on a **login node**. Your first HPC habit — "never run computations on the login node" — is enforced by every center's documentation, sometimes with bluntness: TACC's user guide states that login nodes are strictly for submitting jobs and that resource-hogging users "will be immediately suspended"; NC State's policy puts it as "Basic Rule 1: Do not run on a login node," with non-trivial processes "terminated without notice." The reason is structural, not etiquette.

A login node is **shared, interactive infrastructure**. Hundreds of users may hold SSH sessions on the same few nodes simultaneously — editing files, compiling, submitting jobs, checking queues. Interactive work is latency-sensitive by nature: an editor that stutters or an `ls` that takes seconds degrades everyone's experience at once. A single user running `make -j64` or a "quick test" of a solver can consume an entire login node's cores and memory bandwidth, degrading the control plane for every other user. So centers separate the planes:

- **Login nodes** — the interactive plane: SSH access, file editing, compilation (moderate), job submission, environment setup. Modest hardware, no accelerators, many simultaneous users.
- **Bastion/gateway hosts** — a security layer in front of the login nodes whose only job is credential verification; the University of Arizona's documentation describes its `gatekeeper` host as usable for "nothing else."
- **Data transfer nodes (DTNs)** — dedicated, network-heavy machines for large `scp`/`rsync`/Globus` transfers, so that a multi-terabyte upload doesn't compete with interactive sessions.
- **Service nodes** — the machinery users never log into: the scheduler's controller, accounting databases, authentication (LDAP/Kerberos), monitoring collectors, provisioning services, and the metadata and object servers of the parallel filesystem. These run the *control plane* of the cluster.
- **Compute nodes** — the data plane: no direct user SSH (access is brokered by the scheduler, which launches your processes inside its allocation), hardware allocated exclusively to jobs, and utilization accounted per job.

The scheduler is the component that enforces this separation. In Slurm — the dominant open-source batch system, which SYS modules 3 and 4 will cover in depth — a central `slurmctld` daemon on a management node plus a `slurmd` daemon on every compute node provide three functions, per the official quick-start documentation: (1) allocate exclusive or non-exclusive access to compute nodes for a period of time, (2) provide a framework for starting, executing, and monitoring work on the allocated nodes, and (3) arbitrate contention for resources by managing a queue of pending work. Every user process on a compute node exists inside such an allocation. (El Capitan notably runs **Flux**, LLNL's next-generation resource manager, rather than Slurm — the concepts are the same, and the fact that a top-2 system uses a different scheduler is itself a lesson: the *role* is universal; the implementation is a choice.)

The role split also explains things you have directly experienced. Compiling on the login node is allowed (in moderation) because compilation is short and interactive; running the resulting binary is not. And the queue wait before your job starts exists because compute nodes are allocated exclusively — the mechanism that guarantees your benchmark numbers aren't polluted by a stranger's workload is the same mechanism that makes you wait.

El Capitan's published node counts make the planes concrete: of 11,520 total nodes, 32 are user-facing login nodes, 64 are debug nodes, and 11,424 are batch (compute) nodes — a roughly 350:1 ratio of compute to interactive capacity, which tells you exactly what the machine is *for*.

### SYS-1.5 — Cluster fabric

The final concept is the one that turns a warehouse of servers into a single machine: the **fabric**. The key insight is that a cluster is not one network but **several deliberately separated networks**, each engineered for a different traffic class. Vendor reference architectures make this explicit. NVIDIA's HGX AI Factory reference architecture defines three physical fabrics: a **compute (East/West) network** — RDMA-based, rail-optimized leaf-spine for application traffic; a **CPU converged (North/South) network** for storage and in-band management (e.g., dual 400 GbE per node); and an **out-of-band management network** connecting every BMC. AMD's Instinct datacenter design guide describes the same partitioning: an isolated management/OOB network for BMCs and switch management, a lower-bandwidth public access network for user services, a high-bandwidth storage fabric, and a high-speed low-latency backend fabric (fat-tree or rail-optimized) for accelerator traffic.

```
                Internet / site VPN
                       │
                ┌──────┴──────┐
                │  bastion /  │      security boundary
                │   gateway   │
                └──────┬──────┘
                       │
        ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
        │         LOGIN NODES         │      interactive plane
        │  (edit, compile, submit)    │      shared, no compute
        └──────────────┬──────────────┘
                       │ job submission
        ┌──────────────┴───────────────────────┐
        │            SERVICE PLANE             │
        │  scheduler controller · auth (LDAP)  │
        │  monitoring · provisioning · DB      │
        └────┬────────────────────────┬────────┘
             │                        │
 ┌───────────┴──────────┐  ┌──────────┴───────────┐
 │    COMPUTE FABRIC    │  │    STORAGE FABRIC    │
 │  RDMA, low latency,  │  │  high aggregate BW,  │
 │  high bisection BW   │  │  parallel filesystem │
 │  [node][node][node]  │  │  (MDS/OSS, NVMe)     │
 └───────────┬──────────┘  └──────────────────────┘
             │
 ┌───────────┴──────────┐
 │   OOB MGMT NETWORK   │      isolated: BMC per node,
 │  power · console ·   │      switch mgmt, sensors,
 │  sensors · OS deploy │      no user traffic
 └──────────────────────┘
```

Why separate them? Three reasons, all systemic:

1. **Performance isolation.** MPI collectives, storage I/O, and management polling have utterly different traffic shapes. If a checkpoint storm saturates a converged fabric, an all-reduce elsewhere stalls — and a stalled tightly-coupled job wastes *every* node it holds. Separate fabrics turn one congestion event into one congestion event, not three.
2. **Failure isolation.** The out-of-band network must work precisely when things are broken. A node with a dead OS is power-cyclable because its BMC lives on a different network with a different failure domain. This is the difference between "degraded" and "unrecoverable."
3. **Security.** BMCs control hardware; exposing them on user-reachable networks would make every node one credential compromise away from physical control. Reference architectures therefore keep management ports on physically or logically isolated segments.

The **compute fabric's topology** is a first-class design decision. The DIST theme treated the network as a given; at supercomputer scale it is engineered: **fat-tree/Clos** designs give uniform any-to-any bandwidth at the cost of many switch tiers and much cabling; **dragonfly** topologies reduce cost and diameter by grouping nodes locally and linking groups with long-range cables. El Capitan's Slingshot-11 fabric is a dragonfly — publicly reported specifications describe 64-port switches, at most three hops between any two nodes, and roughly 145 km of cable — while JUPITER runs InfiniBand NDR in a DragonFly+ arrangement that connects *all* compute, storage, and service systems into one fabric. Which topology a site chooses encodes its bet on its workload mix: uniform bisection (favoring irregular communication) versus cheaper locality (favoring nearest-neighbor and map-onto-topology codes).

This is also the bridge back to everything in the DIST theme: topology-aware MPI rank placement, rail optimization, and collective algorithms are *consumers* of fabric properties. The fabric is not plumbing beneath your parallel code; it is half the machine your parallel code runs on.

**The thread through all five topics.** SYS-1.1 said a system is defined by balance. SYS-1.2 said scale changes organization. SYS-1.3 gave you the atom (the node). SYS-1.4 gave you the discipline (role separation) that lets thousands of users share it. SYS-1.5 gave you the connective tissue (the fabrics) that makes it one machine. Next Sunday's SYS volume continues the foundation: head nodes and management infrastructure, system topology, and capability versus capacity computing.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 LineShine debuts at No. 1 on the June 2026 TOP500 — exascale goes global, and CPU-only

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 in Hamburg (June 23, 2026), the 67th TOP500 list installed China's **LineShine** as the new No. 1: 2.198 exaflop/s Rmax on HPL, about 80% of its 2.736 exaflop/s Rpeak, at approximately 42.2 MW (52.07 GFlop/s/W). It displaced El Capitan (1.809 EF/s), which had led the list since November 2024. LineShine is the first Chinese system at No. 1 since Sunway TaihuLight in 2017, and the fifth exascale system overall — for the first time, exascale machines simultaneously exist in Asia, North America, and Europe.

**What technically changed.** The most interesting fact is *how* LineShine got there: 13.79 million cores of a custom Chinese processor — 304-core LX2 chips at 1.55 GHz — linked by the proprietary **LingQi** interconnect and running the Kylin OS. It is a **CPU-only exascale system**, with no GPU/accelerator hardware. Three system-level readings follow directly from today's curriculum. First, hitting 80% of peak on HPL across 13.79 million cores is a *fabric and system-software* achievement — HPL at that scale is limited by the network's bisection behavior and the MPI stack far more than by arithmetic (SYS-1.1, SYS-1.5). Second, the CPU-only design shows up immediately in the mixed-precision HPL-MxP benchmark: LineShine reached 7.92 EF/s, a speedup of only 3.6× over its HPL score, versus 9.2× for El Capitan's MI300A system — the signature of dense FP64 silicon without low-precision tensor engines. Third, LineShine also took No. 1 on HPCG at 22.00 PFlop/s — yet that is still only ~1% of its HPL figure, the same balance ratio as every other exascale machine (SYS-1.1): leadership on one benchmark says nothing universal about workload coverage.

**Why it matters / what to watch.** The list is a snapshot of one benchmark under one measurement process; the durable lesson is architectural — two completely different system designs (304-core CPUs + proprietary interconnect vs. 4-GPU APU nodes + Slingshot dragonfly) both crossed the exascale threshold in the same era, by making the *whole system* (nodes, fabric, software) cohere. Watch the November 2026 list for whether Chinese systems continue regular submissions and whether the HPL-MxP spread between CPU-only and accelerator systems keeps widening.

### 2.2 LUMI-AI: Europe signs its €387.8M next-generation system (August 31, 2026)

**What happened.** The EuroHPC Joint Undertaking signed a procurement contract with **Bull** to deliver **LUMI-AI**, the next-generation supercomputer for CSC's new data center in Kajaani, Finland, with deployment scheduled for the second half of 2027. The contract totals €387.8 million covering acquisition, delivery, installation, and maintenance, funded equally by EuroHPC JU and the six-country LUMI AI Factory consortium (Finland, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Norway, Poland). It is EuroHPC JU's sixth AI Factory procurement.

**What technically changed.** The announced architecture is a textbook example of a supercomputer as an integrated system (SYS-1.2): BullSequana XH3500 cabinets with AMD Instinct MI430X GPUs and 6th-generation AMD EPYC 256-core CPUs; IBM Storage Scale for the storage solution; Nokia providing datacenter networking alongside Bull's **BXI** interconnect — which Bull describes as Europe's only high-bandwidth, low-latency interconnect designed for HPC and AI. The machine is directly liquid-cooled with warm water, and the waste heat feeds Kajaani's district-heating network — power and cooling co-designed with the city's infrastructure, not just the machine room. The performance targets are asymmetric by design: roughly **10× the AI capacity but only ~2× the HPC performance** of today's LUMI. That asymmetry is silicon, not marketing: per AMD's product page (as reported), the MI430X pairs up to 288 TFlop/s of FP64 with up to 432 GB of HBM4 — enormous low-precision throughput relative to FP64. The same GPU is expected to appear in ORNL's Discovery and EuroHPC's Alice Recoque and Herder systems, making it a node architecture (SYS-1.3) worth learning now.

**Why it matters / what to watch.** For a systems student, LUMI-AI is a preview of the procurement-to-production pipeline: contract signed 2026, users 2027 — and every element (node silicon, interconnect, storage, networking, cooling, facility) arriving as one engineered whole, exactly the "supercomputer as system-level product" pattern from SYS-1.2. Watch whether the H2 2027 deployment date holds, and how CSC handles the software-environment transition for the existing LUMI user base — the modules/containers theme later in SYS is where that story lands.

### 2.3 Huawei's Atlas 960E SuperPoD: near-package optics and the "fabric is the computer" bet (September 17, 2026)

**What happened.** At HUAWEI CONNECT 2026 in Shanghai, Huawei launched the **Atlas 960E SuperPoD**, which it calls the industry's first SuperPoD built on **near-package optics (NPO)**. Per Huawei's announcements, a single SuperPoD scales to 4,096 NPUs with unified memory addressing over Huawei's UnifiedBus interconnect, delivering a claimed 8 EFLOPS at FP8 and 16 EFLOPS at FP4. The "supernode" is networked into larger "SuperClusters" of up to 512,000 NPUs via a two-tier, four-plane Clos architecture, or up to a million NPUs with a multi-rail topology. All figures are vendor claims from a product launch keynote.

**What technically changed.** The engineering story is entirely about SYS-1.5 — the fabric. Huawei's Hi-ONE optical engine (7.2 Tbit/s per engine, claimed as the industry's first mass-produced NPO with a built-in light source) moves optical conversion from pluggable modules at the switch faceplate to *next to the package*, shortening electrical traces. The claimed system consequences: about 5,500 Hi-ONE units replace the 48,000 800G optical modules that a conventional design would need for a 4,096-NPU pod, cutting power by over 550 kW, doubling fault-free operating time, and reaching a claimed 99.8% system availability. Huawei's parallel UnifiedBus claims are the same thesis in protocol form: more than ten interconnect protocols unified into one, round-trip latency reduced from ~7 μs to ~2 μs, and memory semantics extended across physical servers. The reliability argument is as important as the bandwidth argument: at 100,000-card scale, optical-module count is a dominant failure population, and reducing component count is a resilience strategy (a theme SYS module 18 develops).

**Why it matters / what to watch.** This development blurs the SYS-1.2 ladder: a 4,096-NPU SuperPoD with unified memory addressing is effectively an aggressive **scale-up domain** wearing cluster clothes — closer to "one very large computer" than to a distributed system, and a cousin of the CXL/memory-centric designs in SYS-26. It also matters geopolitically and economically: NPO is moving toward standardization (an OIF "12.8 TB/s NPO module" proposal was initiated in May 2026 with 40+ supporting vendors), so the technique will not stay confined to one vendor. Read the numbers critically (SYS-30 habits): FP8/FP4 peaks are not FP64, availability figures are vendor-claimed, and no independent evaluation exists yet. But the direction — spending innovation budget on the interconnect rather than the compute die — is the same conclusion the TOP500's own data keeps pointing to.
