## 0. Concise Summary

Two weeks ago (PERF-4.9) we met the **serial fraction** — the piece of work that does not shrink when you add parallel resources — as one item in a list of performance limits. Today's five topics are the quantitative machinery built around that one idea. **Amdahl's Law** (PERF-6.2) turns the serial fraction into a hard ceiling: with fraction \(f\) of work stuck at one worker, no machine, however large, runs the *same* problem more than \(1/f\) times faster. **Gustafson's Law** (PERF-6.3) is the 1988 counterargument from Sandia's 1024-processor hypercube: if you let the problem *grow* with the machine, the measured speedup is roughly linear in the processor count, because the serial part stays fixed in absolute terms while the parallel part grows. The two laws are not rivals — they are the same algebra applied under two different experimental contracts, and those contracts have names: **strong scaling** (PERF-6.4, fixed total problem size, increase resources) and **weak scaling** (PERF-6.5, problem size grows proportionally with resources). **Parallel efficiency** (PERF-6.6), \(E(p) = S(p)/p\), is the number you actually report from either experiment: the fraction of the machine's added capacity that you converted into useful speedup.

The key technical connection: Amdahl's ceiling is the reason strong scaling always dies eventually (halo-exchange surface-to-volume, latency-bound small messages, and the serial fraction all grow *relative* to shrinking per-rank work), and Gustafson's linearity is the reason production HPC — including every HPL run on the TOP500 list — is run in weak-scaling style, with the matrix chosen to fill the machine. The Karp–Flatt serial-fraction metric ties the four together as a diagnostic: it tells you whether lost efficiency comes from fixed limited parallelism or from overhead that grows with scale.

In developments: the June 2026 TOP500 list — still the standing list until SC26 in November — puts today's arithmetic on display, with LineShine sustaining 2.198 exaflop/s at ~80% of its theoretical peak across 13.79 million cores, a machine-level efficiency that is the end product of weak-scaled dense linear algebra. A 2025 eScience paper reports near-100% parallel efficiency for the iPIC3D plasma code on El Capitan out to 32,000 MI300A APUs. And an August 2026 Euro-Par paper from ORNL shows why kernel-level wins (a dominant kernel 20% faster under power capping) can fail to become application-level wins — exposed communication and load imbalance act exactly like a growing serial fraction.

Why this matters: every time you decide how many nodes to request for an HPL or HPCG run, whether to grow \(N\) or add ranks, or when to stop tuning and accept a number, you are applying these five topics. They are the difference between "the machine has 100 nodes" and "the machine *gives* me 100 nodes' worth".

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Where we stand: speedup, recalled

From PERF-6.1, **speedup** on \(p\) workers is

\[
S(p) = \frac{T(1)}{T(p)},
\]

the ratio of the baseline runtime to the parallel runtime. Everything today is a consequence of *what you hold fixed* between the numerator and the denominator of that ratio. Hold the problem fixed and you get strong scaling; hold the per-worker problem fixed and you get weak scaling. Hold nothing fixed and your speedup number is meaningless — a point worth internalizing now, because most inflated speedup claims in the wild (PERF-27.7) are inflated precisely because the numerator and denominator quietly measured different amounts of work.

### 1.2 Amdahl's Law (PERF-6.2)

**The historical argument.** Gene Amdahl's 1967 AFIPS paper, "Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large Scale Computing Capabilities," was a panel contribution arguing against the then-fashionable claim that large arrays of processors would deliver enormous speedups on real problems. Two observations carried the argument. First, measurements of production runs showed that "data management housekeeping" — the sequential scaffolding around real computation — had accounted for roughly 40% of executed instructions for about a decade, and he judged it "highly improbable" that it could be reduced by even a factor of three. Second, real physical problems have irregular boundaries, inhomogeneous interiors, and state-dependent computations that wreck geometrically regular parallel decompositions. Amdahl placed the "probable region" of real workloads around 25% sequential overhead plus ~10% coordination overhead, and concluded that effort on parallel processing rates was wasted unless sequential rates improved comparably.

Notably, the 1967 paper contains no speedup equation at all. The formula everyone calls "Amdahl's Law" is the later formalization of his argument, and it is worth being precise about this because the formalization is sharper than the paper.

**The derivation.** Split the total work into a serial part and a parallelizable part. Run the *same* work on \(p\) processors: the serial part still takes its serial time, the parallel part is divided across \(p\) workers:

\[
T(p) = W_{\text{ser}} + \frac{W_{\text{par}}}{p}, \qquad
T(1) = W_{\text{ser}} + W_{\text{par}}.
\]

Normalize by \(T(1)\) and define the serial fraction \(f = W_{\text{ser}}/T(1)\):

\[
S(p) = \frac{T(1)}{T(p)} = \frac{1}{f + \dfrac{1-f}{p}}, \qquad
\lim_{p\to\infty} S(p) = \frac{1}{f}.
\]

The ceiling is the whole message: a serial fraction of 1% caps speedup at 100×, forever, regardless of how many processors you buy.

**Numbers on the table.**

| Serial fraction \(f\) | \(S(10)\) | \(S(100)\) | \(S(1000)\) | \(S(10^4)\) | Ceiling \(1/f\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 1.82 | 1.98 | 2.00 | 2.00 | 2× |
| 10% | 5.26 | 9.17 | 9.91 | 9.99 | 10× |
| 1% | 9.17 | 50.3 | 91.7 | 99.0 | 100× |
| 0.1% | 9.91 | 90.8 | 500 | 909 | 1000× |

Read the last row the way an HPL tuner must: even a heroic 0.1% serial fraction converts 10,000 cores into at most ~909× — you have already lost 91% of the machine's nominal capacity *to the ceiling alone*, before any communication cost, imbalance, or kernel inefficiency. This is why "just add nodes" stops working on a fixed problem size.

**The effective serial fraction.** In real codes, \(f\) is not just literally serial code. It is *everything that does not shrink when you add workers*: sequential dependencies (the pivot chain in LU panel factorization, time stepping), but also exposed communication, synchronization waits, rank imbalance at the final barrier, and I/O. Karp and Flatt made this precise in a 1990 CACM paper with the **experimentally determined serial fraction**: given a measured speedup \(S(p)\), invert Amdahl's formula to recover the \(f\) that would explain it,

\[
e = \frac{\dfrac{1}{S(p)} - \dfrac{1}{p}}{1 - \dfrac{1}{p}}.
\]

Its diagnostic power is in how \(e\) *moves* as \(p\) grows:

- **\(e\) roughly constant** → your loss is a fixed amount of inherently serial or non-scaling work. Adding hardware cannot fix it; restructuring or shrinking the serial part can.
- **\(e\) smoothly increasing** → overhead that grows with \(p\) (synchronization, communication, contention). Karp and Flatt call this a warning that task granularity is too fine.
- **\(e\) irregularly jumping** → load-balance artifacts: 12 equal chunks balance perfectly on 2, 3, 4, 6, or 12 workers, and badly in between.

A worked pair of runs (all numbers consistent with the formulas above): run A measures \(S(64)=44.4\), \(S(256)=91.9\); the recovered \(e\) is 0.7% at both counts — fixed serial bottleneck. Run B measures \(S(64)=45.0\), \(S(256)=80.0\); the recovered \(e\) rises from 0.67% to 0.86% — something (communication, sync, imbalance) is growing with scale, and no amount of serial-code hunting will find it. Same speedups, different diseases, different cures.

**Where you meet it.** Any time the problem size is fixed by the physics or the assignment — a fixed mesh resolution required for validation, a fixed matrix from a benchmark spec, a strong-scaling study to find the smallest machine that meets a wall-time deadline — Amdahl governs, and the correct response to "we're only getting 40% of peak" is to compute \(e\) before touching a single line of code.

### 1.3 Gustafson's Law (PERF-6.3)

**The historical result.** In 1987–88, John Gustafson, Robert Benner, and Gary Montry ran three production applications on Sandia's 1024-processor nCUBE hypercube: beam stress analysis by conjugate gradients, baffled surface-wave simulation by explicit finite differences, and unstable fluid flow by flux-corrected transport. They measured speedups of **1021, 1020, and 1016 on 1024 processors** — on applications with measured serial fractions of only 0.4–0.8%. Amdahl's formula, fed those serial fractions at \(p=1024\), predicts speedups of roughly 170–250×, not ~1020×. The work was recognized with the first-place 1988 Gordon Bell Award, and the SIAM Journal on Scientific and Statistical Computing paper describing it became one of the most cited results in parallel computing.

**The reframe.** Gustafson's 1988 CACM note, "Reevaluating Amdahl's Law," pinpoints the discrepancy: Amdahl's argument assumes the problem size is fixed and the runtime shrinks. But that is not why people buy 1024-processor machines. Users scale the problem *up* to use the machine, and then the arithmetic runs differently. Measure on the parallel machine: serial time \(s'\), parallel time \(p'\), with \(s' + p' = 1\). Now ask the Amdahl question *backwards*: how long would a serial processor take on this scaled problem? It would have to do the parallel part sequentially: \(s' + N p'\). So the **scaled speedup** (the formulation Gustafson credits to Ed Barsis at Sandia) is

\[
S_{\text{scaled}} = \frac{s' + N p'}{s' + p'} = s' + N p' = N - (N-1)\,s' \quad (\text{since } s'+p'=1).
\]

With \(s' = 0.5\%\) and \(N = 1024\): \(S_{\text{scaled}} = 0.005 + 0.995 \times 1024 \approx 1019\). That is the Sandia regime — and note it is simply a *line* in \(s'\) with slope \(1-N\), whereas Amdahl's fixed-size speedup has curvature \(-N^2\) near \(s'=0\). Gustafson's point in the companion "Scaled-Sized Model" paper: for the same serial fraction, scaled problems are \(N\) times "easier" to parallelize than fixed-size ones.

**Why both laws are true.** The two formulas do not contradict each other; they describe different experiments:

| | Amdahl (1967) | Gustafson (1988) |
|---|---|---|
| Held fixed | Problem size | Runtime (approximately) |
| Grows with \(p\) | Nothing — runtime shrinks | Problem size, with \(p\) |
| Serial fraction | Fixed \(f\), dominates as \(p\) grows | \(s'\) measured on scaled problem, small because \(p'\) grew |
| Prediction | Speedup saturates at \(1/f\) | Speedup ≈ linear in \(p\) |
| Question answered | "How fast can I run *this* problem?" | "How much *more* problem fits in the same time?" |

The deep reason the serial fraction *appears* to shrink: the absolute serial work (setup, I/O, some analysis, the pivoting chain per step) stays roughly constant as the problem grows, while the parallel work grows with problem size. A 1% serial fraction on a workstation-sized problem becomes 0.001% on a machine-sized problem — if the serial part truly does not grow.

**Caveats a performance engineer must carry.**

1. *The baseline is hypothetical.* The "serial processor" that would take \(s' + Np'\) time also needs \(N\times\) the memory; no such machine exists for the scaled problem. Scaled speedup is a fixed-time comparison, not a race between two real machines. Vendor claims of "1.8× larger simulations" (see §2.2) should be read with this in mind.
2. *Serial work does not always stay fixed.* Some serial costs grow with total problem size (global I/O, \(O(N)\) post-processing, output dumps). Weak scaling then decays too — Amdahl re-enters through the back door.
3. *Memory, not time, is often the binding constraint.* Xian-He Sun and Lionel Ni's 1990 memory-bounded speedup model (the Sun–Ni law) generalizes both: let the problem grow according to a memory-bounded function \(G(M)\); \(G(M)=1\) recovers Amdahl, \(G(M)=N\) recovers Gustafson. In practice, "scale the problem to the machine" almost always means "scale until the memory is full," which is Sun–Ni territory.

### 1.4 Strong scaling (PERF-6.4)

**Definition.** Strong scaling holds the *total* problem size fixed and increases the number of workers \(p\), measuring \(S(p) = T(1)/T(p)\). It answers the time-to-solution question: "what is the fewest resources that run this exact problem within my deadline?" It is Amdahl's experiment.

**What ideal looks like, and where it breaks.** Perfect strong scaling doubles speedup with each doubling of \(p\) until per-worker work runs out entirely. The mechanisms of deviation are the core of this topic:

*Surface-to-volume.* Decompose an \(n^3\) cell grid across \(p = q^3\) ranks, \(m = n/q\) cells per edge per rank. Work per rank scales as \(m^3\); halo traffic per step scales with the six faces, \(6m^2\). The communication-to-compute ratio per rank is therefore \(\propto 1/m \propto p^{1/3}/n\). Concretely, on a fixed \(n=512\) grid: at \(p=1\) the ratio is \(\propto 1/512\); at \(p=64\) (\(m=128\)) it is \(4\times\) worse; at \(p=512\) (\(m=64\)) it is \(8\times\) worse. Every strong-scaling run eventually crosses into a regime where moving the halo costs more than computing on the interior — the same surface-to-volume logic that drives blocking and tiling (PERF-5.6).

*Latency-bound messages.* As \(m\) shrinks, each of the six messages per step shrinks too, but their *latency* floor does not. Time per message = latency + size/bandwidth; once size/bandwidth < latency, halving the work per rank halves the compute but barely dents the communication. The per-step time stops falling.

*Load imbalance and remainders.* \(n^3\) cells do not divide evenly across arbitrary \(p\); remainder ranks finish early and wait. Barrier amplification (coming in PERF-18.4) turns small skew into large idle time.

*The ceiling.* All of the above is on top of Amdahl's \(1/f\). In practice, communication and imbalance show up *as* an effective serial fraction in the Karp–Flatt metric — which is exactly why that metric exists.

**How to run a strong-scaling study.** Use a geometric sequence (1, 2, 4, 8, …) of ranks on identical hardware, fixed problem size and code, same node type and clock configuration (PERF-3.6), report \(S(p)\) *and* \(E(p)\), and compute the Karp–Flatt \(e\) at each point. A knee in \(S(p)\) plus a *constant* \(e\) says "serial fraction rules — restructure or accept it"; a knee plus a *rising* \(e\) says "overhead grows with scale — coarsen the decomposition, overlap communication, or fix balance." The knee location, not the peak, is the actionable output: it is the largest machine worth paying for this problem.

**The HPL connection.** Strong-scaling HPL at fixed \(N\) is the classic student-cluster mistake: adding nodes to a fixed matrix shrinks per-rank GEMM work while the panel factorization (serial-ish, dependency-heavy) and broadcasts stay, so efficiency falls. The benchmark is designed to be *weak-scaled* — which is the next topic.

### 1.5 Weak scaling (PERF-6.5)

**Definition.** Weak scaling grows the problem size proportionally with the number of workers — per-rank problem size (roughly) constant — and measures how the runtime behaves. Two equivalent reporting conventions:

- **Weak efficiency:** \(E_{\text{weak}}(p) = T(1, w)\,/\,T(p, p\cdot w)\) — the ratio of one-worker time on one worker's worth of work, to \(p\)-worker time on \(p\) workers' worth. Ideal: exactly 1 (constant time as the machine and job grow together).
- **Scaled speedup** (Gustafson's form, §1.3): time a hypothetical serial machine would need for the scaled job divided by the actual parallel time. Ideal: \(p\).

Weak scaling is Gustafson's experiment. It answers the capacity question: "if I get 4× the machine, do I get 4× the science per unit time?"

**What breaks weak scaling.** The failure modes are different from strong scaling's, and recognizing the difference is diagnostic gold:

- **Global reductions and collective operations.** Even with perfect per-rank work scaling, an allreduce spanning the machine costs at least \(\Theta(\log p)\) at best, and is bounded by the machine's bisection bandwidth. One global reduction per time step puts a \(p\)-dependent term inside every step — a serial fraction that *grows* with the machine.
- **Globally coupled phases.** FFT-based methods (3D transforms), transposes, and particle-grid deposition mix data across the whole job; their cost per rank does not stay constant as the machine grows.
- **Per-node bandwidth saturation.** If the per-rank working set grows to fill a node's memory, the arithmetic intensity seen by that node can fall (bigger footprints, worse cache behavior) — the hierarchical-Roofline effects from PERF-5.7 re-enter.
- **Serial parts that grow with total size.** As noted in §1.3, if output or analysis is \(O(N_{\text{global}})\), weak scaling decays like strong scaling does, just more slowly.

**HPL as the canonical weak-scaled workload.** Every serious HPL run — including every TOP500 submission — picks \(N\) so the matrix fills some large fraction of the machine's memory. Work grows as \(N^3\); adding nodes at fixed per-node memory means \(N \propto p^{1/3}\), which is exactly the per-rank-constant contract. HPL's internal structure then fights the weak-scaling enemies: the panel broadcast spans the whole machine (a global operation per panel), which is why look-ahead and overlapped broadcast exist (HPL-5.x, and PERF-24.6 ahead). When you read that the number-one system sustains ~80% of theoretical peak (§2.1), you are reading the *end product* of weak-scaled dense linear algebra: a machine-level efficiency earned by keeping every one of millions of cores fed with rank-local GEMM work while hiding the global operations.

**Choosing the experiment.** A question about deadlines or minimum hardware → strong scaling. A question about throughput, capacity, or "is this code ready for the big machine" → weak scaling. Reporting only one and generalizing to the other is the most common scaling-claim error in student reports and vendor slides alike.

### 1.6 Parallel efficiency (PERF-6.6)

**Definition.** Parallel efficiency is speedup divided by the resource multiplier:

\[
E(p) = \frac{S(p)}{p}.
\]

It is the fraction of the machine's added capacity that became useful performance: \(E = 0.7\) on 8 nodes means 30% of those nodes' time is being spent on something other than forward progress — exposed communication, synchronization waits, imbalance, or the serial fraction itself. The weak-scaling analogue \(E_{\text{weak}}\) was defined in §1.5; both get reported as "efficiency," so always ask which contract produced the number.

**Efficiency under Amdahl.** Substituting Amdahl's speedup gives a clean closed form worth memorizing:

\[
E(p) = \frac{1}{1 + f\,(p-1)}.
\]

| Serial fraction \(f\) | \(E(8)\) | \(E(64)\) | \(E(256)\) | \(E(1000)\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1% | 99.9% | 94% | 80% | 50% |
| 1% | 93.5% | 61% | 28% | 9.2% |

Two punchlines: a 1% serial fraction *halves* efficiency by \(p = 100\) — long before speedup visibly saturates — and by \(p = 1000\) you are running a 9% efficient job. Efficiency is the early-warning metric; speedup is the flattering one.

**Machine efficiency is not parallel efficiency.** In the wild, "efficiency" also means \(R_{\max}/R_{\text{peak}}\) — the fraction of theoretical peak flops sustained by a benchmark. That is a property of a (benchmark, machine) pair, dominated by kernel quality, memory bandwidth, and network; \(E(p)\) is a property of a (application, machine) pair at a specific scale, dominated by exposed overheads. LineShine's 80% \(R_{\max}/R_{\text{peak}}\) (§2.1) and a code's 60% parallel efficiency at \(p=256\) are different claims about different things. Keep the denominators straight and half of all benchmark-table arguments (PERF-27.6) dissolve.

**The iso-efficiency view.** Since \(E(p)\) decays at fixed work, holding efficiency constant requires growing the work with \(p\) — the **iso-efficiency function** asks how fast. Codes with cheap, local communication (dense GEMM per rank) have gentle iso-efficiency curves and weak-scale superbly; codes with global synchronizations per step have steep ones and exhaust large machines quickly. This is the formal bridge between today's laws and the algorithm-structure topics of Theme 2: the same collective that is an implementation detail to the algorithm is an iso-efficiency cliff to the performance engineer. (The standard treatment is Kumar et al., *Introduction to Parallel Computing* — see Further Reading.)

**Using efficiency to make decisions.** Three practical rules:

1. **Budget rule.** If a job must finish in \(T_{\max}\) and your strong-scaling curve says \(T(p)\), take the smallest \(p\) meeting the deadline — not the largest available. Wasted allocation is someone else's lost science, and on a competition cluster it is lost budget.
2. **Marginal rule.** Add resources only while the *marginal* speedup \(\Delta S / \Delta p\) justifies the allocation cost. The knee from §1.4 is where this derivative collapses.
3. **Diagnosis rule.** When \(E\) disappoints, compute Karp–Flatt \(e\) before changing anything: constant \(e\) → attack the serial structure; rising \(e\) → attack communication, synchronization, or balance.

**Synthesis.** Amdahl tells you the price of scale at fixed work; Gustafson tells you the payoff of scale at fixed time; strong and weak scaling are the two experiments that operationalize them; and parallel efficiency is the exchange rate each experiment reports. One framework, four viewpoints — and every serious performance question ("how many nodes?", "grow \(N\) or grow ranks?", "is 70% good?") is one of these four in disguise.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 The June 2026 TOP500: weak scaling and efficiency at 13.79 million cores

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 in Hamburg (June 23, 2026), the 67th TOP500 list debuted **LineShine**, installed at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, at No. 1 with **2.198 exaflop/s on HPL — about 80% of its 2.736 exaflop/s theoretical peak**. It is the first CPU-only system above two sustained exaflop/s, built from 13.79 million cores (304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz) on the proprietary LingQi interconnect, drawing ~42.2 MW (52.07 gigaflops/watt), and it is the first Chinese system to lead the list since 2017. El Capitan holds No. 2 at 1.809 exaflop/s (11.34M cores, 60.94 GF/W — that is \(1.809/2.821 \approx 64\%\) of peak), with Frontier (1.353), Aurora (1.012), and JUPITER Booster (1.000) rounding out five exascale systems spanning Asia, North America, and Europe for the first time. LineShine also took No. 1 on HPCG (22.00 PF/s).

**The technical read through today's lens.** An HPL run on 13.79 million cores is the largest weak-scaling experiment routinely performed on Earth: \(N\) is chosen per machine so per-rank work stays GEMM-heavy, which is precisely the Gustafson/Sun–Ni contract (§1.3, §1.5). The 80% figure is a *machine efficiency* (\(R_{\max}/R_{\text{peak}}\)), but it is earned or lost through the mechanisms of §1.5: every global operation — panel broadcasts, reductions — that fails to hide behind rank-local trailing updates becomes an effective serial fraction across 13.79M cores, where even a 0.01% exposed fraction would forfeit \(\sim\)58% of the machine (from \(E = 1/(1+f(p-1))\)). A CPU-only design reaching 80% at this scale says something concrete about LingQi's bisection structure and the software stack's ability to overlap — and LineShine's more modest 3.6× HPL-MxP/HPL ratio (versus El Capitan's 9.2×) reflects the absence of low-precision matrix engines rather than any scaling deficiency.

**What to watch.** The November list at SC26, and whether HPCG leaders (LineShine 22.0 PF/s over El Capitan's 17.41) continue to compress the HPL-to-HPCG gap — HPCG's global dependencies make it a much harsher weak-scaling test than HPL, which is the entire point of its existence.

### 2.2 iPIC3D on El Capitan: near-100% parallel efficiency to 32,000 APUs

**What happened.** At IEEE eScience 2025, Lumsden, Markidis, Taufer, and colleagues reported performance optimization of **iPIC3D**, an implicit-moment particle-in-cell code for planetary-scale magnetosphere modeling, on El Capitan: strong and weak scaling studies show **near-linear scaling up to 8,000 nodes (32,000 MI300A APUs) with parallel efficiency of nearly 100%**, after optimizations that cut memory use by 97% and runtime by 74%, enabling simulations 1.8× larger than before.

**Why it matters technically.** This is Gustafson's paradigm executed on 2024-vintage exascale hardware: the "1.8× larger simulations" claim is the capacity question of §1.5, and near-100% efficiency at 32,000 devices means the exposed serial fraction — including the global solves inherent to implicit PIC — was kept astonishingly small. Implicit methods trade per-step cost for fewer, larger steps; that trade only pays if the global coupling does not serialize, which makes the efficiency result a statement about their domain decomposition and solver overlap, not just fast kernels.

**How to read it.** This is a single-team result measured against their own prior configuration (the 74%/97%/1.8× are relative to the authors' baseline, per the paper's abstract); "nearly 100%" should be read at the reported scale and workload. The paper is two pages (a conference short/extended-abstract track) — the full methodology is in the group's longer papers. Still, as an existence proof that weak scaling past 30k APUs is achievable for a globally coupled code, it is a directly relevant data point for today's curriculum.

### 2.3 Euro-Par 2026: faster kernels, slower applications

**What happened.** In August 2026 at Euro-Par (Pisa), Costa, Georgiadou, White, and colleagues at ORNL published "When Faster Kernels Do Not Mean Faster Applications in Exascale GPU Systems," studying power and frequency capping on Frontier with three applications (Cholla, Pennant, HACC). In Pennant across 32 nodes, the dominant kernel ran **up to 20% faster under power capping**, yet the gains were partially offset by higher communication costs and load imbalance across MPI calls. In Cholla, synchronization imbalance rose from 30.6% to 39.8% and communication imbalance from 5.8% to 16.6%, lengthening MPI wait times despite faster kernel execution.

**Why it matters.** This is §1.6's lesson demonstrated on a production exascale system: parallel efficiency is a property of the *whole* (kernels + network + balance + power state), not of any kernel in isolation. Faster kernels change the relative timing of ranks; if that perturbs overlap and balance, the freed compute time converts into MPI wait time — which is, in Karp–Flatt terms, an *increase in the effective serial fraction*. Note also the environmental-control echo of PERF-3.6: a power cap is an experimental variable, and the same cap can be a kernel-level win and an application-level loss.

**What to watch.** Power-aware scheduling on upcoming systems — as machines harden into fixed power budgets, "the fastest kernel configuration" and "the fastest application configuration" will increasingly diverge, and the efficiency-vs-energy frontier (the SCREAM power-profiling work from IPDPS 2026 is a sibling result) becomes its own tuning axis.

### Further Reading

- **Gustafson, "The Scaled-Sized Model: A Revision of Amdahl's Law"** (1988 companion piece) — [author's copy](http://www.johngustafson.net/pubs/pub1988.2/ScaledSized.pdf). The \(N^2\)-versus-\(N\) slope analysis in full. *Deep dive — 30 min.*
- **Karp & Flatt, CACM 1990** (citation 4) — the worked machine examples (Cray Y-MP, Alliant FX/40) are the best illustrations of serial-fraction diagnosis ever published. *Technical paper — 20 min.*
- **A. Grama, A. Gupta, G. Karypis, V. Kumar, *Introduction to Parallel Computing*** — the iso-efficiency function and scalability analysis chapters. *Textbook reference — deep dive.*
- **Sun–Ni law (memory-bounded speedup)** — [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun%E2%80%93Ni_law); original: X.-H. Sun and L. Ni, Proc. IEEE Supercomputing Conference, 1990. The memory-bound generalization of both laws. *Concept — 10 min.*
- **LLNL tutorial** (citation 8), "Potential Benefits, Limits and Costs" and "Scalability" sections — a compact refresher of today's whole lesson. *Foundation — 15 min.*
