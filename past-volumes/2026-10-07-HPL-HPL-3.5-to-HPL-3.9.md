## 0. Concise Summary

Last week's volume ended at machine epsilon: the price list of the precision ladder. Today we spend that epsilon, over and over, and watch what it buys — and what it costs. Five topics form one continuous story about the difference between mathematical arithmetic and the arithmetic your hardware actually executes.

**Rounding** (HPL-3.5) is the atomic event: every floating-point operation ends by mapping an exact real result onto the nonuniform grid from last week, and IEEE 754 guarantees this mapping is *correctly rounded* — as if computed infinitely precisely, then rounded once. That single guarantee is what makes error analysis possible at all. **Overflow and underflow** (HPL-3.6) are the grid's edges: finite exponent range means some exact results cannot be rounded because they have no representable neighborhood, and the standard's answers (±∞, gradual underflow through subnormals) create both numerical and *performance* consequences. **Non-associativity** (HPL-3.7) follows immediately: because each rounding is relative to the current partial result's magnitude, the *grouping* of operations changes the answer — which is why parallel reduction order is a numerical variable, why bitwise reproducibility is hard, and why HPL itself warns that all its parameter combinations are numerically equivalent but may differ bit-wise. **Error accumulation** (HPL-3.8) is the composition of many roundings — bounded worst case by \(n \cdot u\), in practice behaving like \(\sqrt{n}\cdot u\) by random-walk statistics, and made dangerous chiefly through cancellation, which does not create error but reveals it. **The fused multiply-add** (HPL-3.9) is the hardware answer: one rounding instead of two, and — far more importantly — the primitive that makes *error-free transformations* possible, recovering the exact rounding error of a product in a second operation.

The developments show this material deciding billion-dollar architecture bets. NVIDIA now ships **FP64 emulation via the Ozaki scheme in cuBLAS** — matrix multiplication rebuilt from exact low-precision tensor-core products plus a careful high-precision reconstruction, which is literally today's two-product/FMA idea scaled to whole matrices. AMD is betting the other way: the **Instinct MI430X** (announced July 2026, 288 native FP64 TFLOP/s, early 2027) argues that IEEE-compliant rounding guarantees cannot be outsourced to software. The precision-vs-speed trade you will formalize tomorrow (HPL-3.10) is, right now, the central fault line in accelerator design.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Rounding: the atomic error event (HPL-3.5)

**The problem.** Last week you learned that a binary64 number is a point on a nonuniform grid: within each binade \([2^e, 2^{e+1})\) the representable values are spaced \(2^{e-52}\) apart. Almost every exact mathematical result — a sum, a product, a square root — falls *between* grid points. Something must choose a grid point. That choice is rounding, and it is the only place where error enters a floating-point computation. Everything in sections 1.2–1.4 is a consequence of how this one step behaves.

**The mechanism.** IEEE 754 requires that the basic operations — add, subtract, multiply, divide, square root, and the fused multiply-add — are **correctly rounded**: the returned value is the one you would get by computing the exact result to infinite precision and then applying the rounding rule once. The default rule is **round-to-nearest, ties-to-even**: pick the nearest representable value; if the exact result is exactly halfway between two neighbors, pick the one whose last significand bit is 0.

Why ties-to-even and not ties-away-from-zero? Because the error of a rounding step, \(\mathrm{fl}(x) - x\), should be *unbiased*. If ties always rounded up, a long computation over randomly signed data would accumulate a systematic drift; breaking ties by parity makes the expected rounding error zero. The rule looks pedantic until you notice that the entire statistical model of section 1.4 — the reason a billion-FLOP computation still works — rests on roundings being mean-zero noise.

**Concrete, verified examples.** In binary64:

- \(0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004\), not \(0.3\). Neither 0.1 nor 0.2 is representable; each stored value is the nearest grid point, their *exact* sum then rounds to `0x3FD3333333333334` while 0.3 is `0x3FD3333333333333` — one ulp apart (the difference is \(2^{-54} \approx 5.55\times10^{-17}\)). Note the error here comes from *representation* (input rounding), not from the addition: the addition itself was correctly rounded.
- \(1 + 2^{-53} = 1\) exactly. The exact sum sits exactly halfway between 1 and \(1+2^{-52}\) (the grid spacing at \([1,2)\) is \(2^{-52}\), so half an ulp is \(2^{-53}\)); the tie goes to 1, whose significand is even.

The tie example is worth internalizing because it is the *smallest* perturbation that changes nothing — the resolution limit of the format near 1.0, which is exactly the machine-epsilon discussion from last week, now seen as a rounding decision rather than a static property.

**The model that makes analysis possible.** Correct rounding gives the standard model of floating-point error: for operands \(a, b\),

$$
\mathrm{fl}(a \,\mathrm{op}\, b) = (a \,\mathrm{op}\, b)(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u = 2^{-53} \text{ (binary64)},
$$

where \(u\) is the unit roundoff. One inequality, true for every basic operation, is the entire foundation of numerical linear algebra's error bounds: a bound on a computation is obtained by counting its operations, charging each at most \(u\), and propagating. When you later read that HPL's residual check passes when \(\|Ax-b\|_\infty / (\varepsilon(\|A\|_\infty\|x\|_\infty + \|b\|_\infty)N) < 16\) with \(\varepsilon = 1.110223\times10^{-16} = 2^{-53}\), recognize the \(2^{-53}\): it is this same \(u\), and the threshold 16 is "a handful of \(u\)-scaled roundings per operation row, with room for growth factors." The benchmark's definition of *numerically correct* is literally a rounding-error budget.

**Directed rounding.** IEEE 754 also defines directed modes — toward zero, toward \(+\infty\), toward \(-\infty\) — which deliberately round the *wrong* way. Their purpose is interval arithmetic: run a computation twice, once rounding up and once rounding down, and the two results bracket the exact answer with mathematical certainty. Directed rounding trades the unbiasedness of round-to-nearest for rigor; you will meet it again if you study verified numerical computing. For HPC dense linear algebra, round-to-nearest-even is the working assumption of every bound in this curriculum.

**Why this topic is first among today's five.** Overflow (1.2) is what happens when the exact result has no representable *neighborhood* to round into. Non-associativity (1.3) is the observation that the rounding target depends on the magnitude of the intermediate result, so grouping matters. Accumulation (1.4) is the composition of many \(\delta\)'s. The FMA (1.5) is a hardware feature that removes one rounding from a multiply-add pair. One mechanism, five consequences.

### 1.2 Overflow and underflow: the edges of the grid (HPL-3.6)

**The problem.** The exponent field is finite: 11 bits in binary64, giving roughly \(10^{\pm308}\) of dynamic range. Two distinct failures live at the edges. **Overflow**: an exact result larger than \((2-2^{-52})\cdot2^{1023} \approx 1.798\times10^{308}\) has no nearest representable value. **Underflow**: an exact result *smaller* in magnitude than \(2^{-1022} \approx 2.225\times10^{-308}\) (the smallest normal) falls below the region where the format provides full 53-bit significands.

**Overflow mechanics.** IEEE 754 maps overflow to signed infinity, and infinity is *sticky*: it propagates through subsequent arithmetic, and combinations like \(\infty - \infty\) produce NaN. A single overflow in a norm computation can therefore poison an entire result silently — no trap, no exception unless you ask for one (the standard's exception flags exist precisely so hot loops can run unmolested and be checked afterward).

The classic dense-linear-algebra victim is the naive Euclidean norm. Verified in binary64: for a vector of ten entries each \(10^{160}\), computing \(\|x\|_2^2 = \sum x_i^2\) overflows to `inf`, even though the true norm \(\sqrt{10}\times10^{160} \approx 3.16\times10^{160}\) is perfectly representable. Squaring is a range *amplifier*: any entry above \(\sqrt{1.8\times10^{308}} \approx 1.34\times10^{154}\) overflows when squared. This is why every production norm routine — LAPACK's `dlange`, BLAS `nrm2` — computes a *scaled* norm: find \(m = \max|x_i|\), compute \(\sum (x_i/m)^2\), multiply back by \(m\). The mathematics is identical; the floating-point behavior is completely different. When HPL's residual check computes \(\|Ax-b\|_\infty\), \(\|A\|_\infty\), \(\|x\|_\infty\), it is implicitly relying on the implementers having done exactly this kind of range management inside the norm and residual kernels.

**Underflow mechanics.** Below \(2^{-1022}\), the format could either (a) snap to zero, or (b) continue with reduced precision: keep the exponent clamped and let the significand's leading bits become zero, producing **subnormals** down to \(2^{-1074} \approx 5\times10^{-324}\) with progressively fewer significant bits. IEEE chose (b), *gradual underflow*, and the reason is algebraic more than arithmetic: with subnormals, \(x = y\) if and only if \(x - y = 0\). Without them, two distinct tiny values subtract to exactly zero, and identities that error analysis leans on — like the Sterbenz-type exactness results you will meet in compensated arithmetic — break at the bottom of the range. Gradual underflow makes floating-point arithmetic a *closed, consistent* system at the cost of some extra hardware cases.

**The performance catch.** Subnormal inputs and results are the slow path of nearly every floating-point unit. On many x86 processors, an operation producing or consuming a subnormal traps to a microcode assist — the instruction stalls for tens to over a hundred cycles instead of its usual latency (Intel's compiler documentation devotes a section to exactly this; Agner Fog's instruction tables quantify it per microarchitecture). The classic production failure is a feedback loop or decaying signal in DSP/audio whose tail drifts into the subnormal range: the code's throughput collapses by orders of magnitude while producing numerically fine answers. The standard escape hatches are the **FTZ** (flush-to-zero) and **DAZ** (denormals-are-zero) mode bits — e.g. in the x86 `MXCSR` register — which trade gradual underflow for uniform speed. Compilers expose them through flags like `-ftz`; Intel's documentation treats them as a standard performance-tuning knob for exactly the workloads that can tolerate zero instead of \(10^{-310}\).

For dense linear algebra proper, overflow and underflow are mostly *engineering hygiene*: scale your norms, avoid forming \(\|x\|^2\) naively, know whether your BLAS was built with FTZ. But they move to center stage in mixed precision. FP16's exponent is 5 bits: everything above 65504 overflows, everything below \(\sim 6\times10^{-5}\) goes subnormal. A matrix whose entries span many orders of magnitude — routine in PDE discretizations — cannot be factored in FP16 without **scaling**, which is why diagonal scaling and range management are first-class citizens of the HPL-MxP algorithm design you will study in module 14 (dynamic range is curriculum topic HPL-14.6 in its own right). Range is a resource, budgeted just like mantissa bits.

### 1.3 Non-associativity: grouping is a numerical variable (HPL-3.7)

**The problem.** Real addition is associative: \((a+b)+c = a+(b+c)\) exactly, for all values. Floating-point addition is commutative — \(\mathrm{fl}(a+b) = \mathrm{fl}(b+a)\), since rounding depends only on the exact value — but it is **not associative**, because each partial sum is rounded at its own magnitude. Change the grouping, change the intermediate magnitudes, change the roundings.

**Verified examples, in increasing subtlety.** In binary64:

- The textbook case: \((1 + 10^{20}) - 10^{20} = 0.0\) but \(1 + (10^{20} - 10^{20}) = 1.0\). The first grouping rounds the exact \(10^{20}+1\) back to \(10^{20}\) — at magnitude \(10^{20}\) the grid spacing is \(2^{66-52} = 2^{14} = 16384\), so 1 is invisible — and the information is gone before the subtraction.
- The near-cancellation case: with \(x = 1\), \(y = 1+2^{-52}\), \(z = -(1+2^{-51})\), we get \((x+y)+z = 0.9999999999999996\) but \(x+(y+z) = 0.9999999999999998\). Two different answers, both *wrong* (exactly \(1 - 2^{-52}\)), differing by one ulp, from the same three numbers — and here no intermediate ever leaves the interval \([1,2)\). Even without dramatic magnitude gaps, plain roundings at slightly different partial sums produce different bits.
- The quiet case: in binary32, \(8192 + 0.0004 = 8192.0\) exactly (verified). The spacing at \(2^{13}\) is \(2^{-10} \approx 9.77\times10^{-4}\); half an ulp is \(\approx 4.9\times10^{-4}\), and \(0.0004\) falls below it. A correction of size \(4\times10^{-4}\) added to a running total near 8192 does *nothing* — not because of a bug, but because of where it landed on the grid.

**The system consequences — this is why HPC cares.**

1. **Reduction order is part of the numerical specification.** A dot product of length \(n\) can be summed in \(n!\)-ish different groupings; a parallel reduction across \(p\) processes fixes one of them, and the choice is made by the *scheduler*, the process grid, the kernel's split-\(k\) strategy — parameters you thought were purely performance knobs. This is precisely what the HPL documentation states: every algorithmic variant the benchmark offers is "rigorously equivalent" numerically, "even though the result may slightly differ (bit-wise)." The benchmark's authors are telling you non-associativity is unavoidable at scale, so the correctness criterion is the residual, not the bits.
2. **Bitwise reproducibility is not free.** Run the same GEMM twice with different thread counts, or on a GPU where `atomicAdd` ordering varies run-to-run, and you get different bits. For debugging, validation, and regression testing this is a genuine problem — serious enough that reproducible summation became a research field (ReproBLAS) and that IEEE 754-2019 standardized *augmented* arithmetic operations explicitly to make order-independent summation fast (section 1.5 and the citations).
3. **Ordering is an accuracy knob.** Summing from smallest to largest keeps partial sums small relative to incoming terms. Verified in binary32 for the harmonic sum \(\sum_{i\le 30000} 1/i\): ascending order (largest terms first) gives relative error \(1.96\times10^{-6}\); descending order (smallest first) gives \(2.35\times10^{-7}\) — an 8× improvement from reordering alone, zero extra arithmetic.
4. **Tree (pairwise) summation is both a parallelism and an accuracy win.** Summing \(n=10^5\) random values in binary32: sequential order gave relative error \(1.45\times10^{-5}\); a pairwise tree gave \(1.16\times10^{-6}\) — 12× better. This is not a coincidence of the data; see the next section, where the bounds explain exactly this factor. The happy fact that the *fastest* parallel reduction (a tree) is also the *most accurate* grouping is one of the quiet synergies of parallel numerical computing — and it is why blocked algorithms, which naturally compute partial sums over blocks before combining, inherit good summation behavior for free.

One practical corollary: this is what compiler flags like GCC's `-ffast-math` (specifically its reassociation enabling) secretly change. A compiler that reassociates your loop is making a numerical decision, not just a scheduling one; strict IEEE mode forbids it. When a code's results shift in the last bits after an "innocent" rebuild, non-associativity is almost always the culprit.

### 1.4 Error accumulation: from one δ to a benchmark threshold (HPL-3.8)

**The problem.** One rounding contributes at most \(u\). A dense LU factorization of order \(n = 10^6\) executes \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3 \approx 7\times10^{17}\) FLOPs. If errors compounded the way memory latencies do — every one paying full price, multiplicatively — no floating-point computation of scientific scale would ever produce a usable digit. Understanding why \(10^{18}\) roundings still yield ~11 correct digits is the heart of this topic.

**Worst-case bounds.** Apply the model of 1.1 to a sequential sum \(s = x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n\), each partial rounded once. Standard induction (Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, ch. 3–4) gives

$$
\Big| \mathrm{fl}\Big(\sum_i x_i\Big) - \sum_i x_i \Big| \;\le\; (n-1)\,u \sum_i |x_i| ,
$$

and the shorthand \(\gamma_n = nu/(1-nu)\) packages the same bound for products of many operations. The LU cousin of this bound (Wilkinson's backward error analysis, module 21 formalizes it) says the computed factors satisfy \(\hat{L}\hat{U} = P(A + \Delta A)\) with \(\|\Delta A\|\) on the order of \(\gamma_{3n} \rho \|A\|\), where \(\rho\) is the pivot growth factor. The punchline: the backward error of the *entire factorization* scales like \(n \cdot u\), not \(n^3 \cdot u\). Nearly all of the \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) roundings land on intermediate quantities and are *absorbed* into a perturbation of the input that stays proportional to \(n\). For \(n = 10^5\), \(3n \cdot 2^{-53} \approx 3\times10^{-11}\): eleven digits of backward accuracy from a trillion roundings. That is the miracle Wilkinson's framework bought us, and it is why HPL's threshold-16 residual check is generous rather than tight — real runs come in around 0.01.

**Why practice is even better than the bound: the random-walk model.** The worst-case bound assumes every rounding error pushes the same direction. In real data with mixed signs, roundings behave like independent mean-zero noise (this is where ties-to-even's unbiasedness from 1.1 earns its keep), so errors add like a random walk: magnitude \(\sim u\sqrt{n}\), not \(un\). The verified experiment from 1.3 confirms both regimes simultaneously, in binary32 with \(u = 2^{-24} = 5.96\times10^{-8}\), \(n = 10^5\):

| Summation strategy | Worst-case bound | Measured error |
|---|---|---|
| Sequential | \(n\,u \approx 6.0\times10^{-3}\) | \(1.45\times10^{-5}\) |
| Sequential, statistical model | \(\sqrt{n}\,u \approx 1.9\times10^{-5}\) | \(1.45\times10^{-5}\) |
| Pairwise tree | \(\log_2 n \cdot u \approx 9.9\times10^{-7}\) | \(1.16\times10^{-6}\) |

Read that table twice. The sequential sum's measured error sits dead on the \(\sqrt{n}\) statistical prediction and 400× below the worst-case bound. The pairwise tree's measured error sits at its own worst-case bound — because a tree has only \(\log_2 n\) rounding *levels*, its worst case is already tiny. This is the quantitative version of yesterday's qualitative claim: **blocking reduces error accumulation the same way it reduces communication — by shortening the chains**. And it is why the block size \(NB\) you tune in HPL has a numerical signature, however faint.

**Cancellation: the mechanism that actually kills computations.** The bounds above scale the error by \(\sum |x_i|\) — i.e., relative to the *total magnitude flowing through*, not the final answer. When the final answer is much smaller than the operands — subtracting nearly equal numbers — the relative error explodes. The key insight, often misstated: **cancellation does not create error; it reveals it**. By the Sterbenz lemma, if \(x\) and \(y\) are within a factor of 2 of each other, \(\mathrm{fl}(x-y) = x - y\) *exactly* — the subtraction itself is perfect. But the input errors already inside \(x\) and \(y\), each bounded only by \(u|x|\), are now measured against an output of size \(|x - y| \ll |x|\). Relative error is divided by a small number.

This is exactly the situation in a residual: \(r = b - Ax\). When \(x\) is a good solution, each component of \(Ax\) nearly equals the corresponding component of \(b\), and computing \(r\) in working precision leaves you with the *rounding noise of the multiplication* wearing the costume of a residual. This is why iterative refinement (module 15) computes residuals in extra precision, and why HPL's check normalizes by \(\varepsilon \|A\|\|x\|\) rather than by \(\|r\|\) alone: the benchmark is measuring error *relative to the noise floor of the computation itself* — a backward-error criterion, as module 21 will name it.

**A verified worst case that is not statistical at all.** Add \(1.0\) to an accumulator initialized to \(10^{16}\), ten million times, in binary64. The naive loop returns the accumulator *unchanged*: the grid spacing at \(10^{16}\) is exactly 2 (verified: \(10^{16} \in [2^{53}, 2^{54})\)), so each \(+1\) is a tie that rounds back down. Total gained: 0.0. Kahan's compensated summation — which carries a running correction term, at 4 FLOPs per add — returns exactly \(10{,}000{,}000.0\). When all errors push the same direction against a large accumulator, the worst case is not pessimism; it is the *actual behavior*, and only algorithmic countermeasures (compensation, reordering, higher intermediate precision) help. The classic real-world cousins are variance computations (the "textbook" \(\mathbb{E}[x^2] - \mathbb{E}[x]^2\) formula is a cancellation machine; the two-pass formula is not) and — as above — residuals.

**Forward pointers.** Everything here was in one precision. In HPL-MxP, the factorization's bulk runs at \(u\) of \(2^{-11}\)-ish (FP16/TF32-class inputs): the same accumulation mathematics applies with a unit roundoff four billion times coarser, which is why the accuracy-recovery pass (module 15) exists and why its convergence theory is a race between correction gains and the coarse-\(u\) noise floor.

### 1.5 The fused multiply-add: one rounding, and the key to exactness (HPL-3.9)

**The mechanism.** The FMA computes

$$
\mathrm{fma}(a, b, c) = \mathrm{fl}(a \times b + c)
$$

with the product \(a\times b\) formed to *full internal precision* and entering the addition unrounded. Only the final result is rounded. Compare the unfused sequence: \(\mathrm{fl}(\mathrm{fl}(a\times b) + c)\) — two roundings, and crucially the product has already been damaged before the addition sees it.

Why does hardware provide it? Both halves of the FLOP story:

- **Performance:** one instruction retires two FLOPs. Peak FP64 throughput on a modern CPU is literally (FMA units) × 2 × (clock); the "FLOP" in FLOP/s *is* the FMA's half. HPL's reported GFLOP/s counts a multiply-add pair as two operations — the benchmark's arithmetic unit and the hardware's arithmetic unit are the same object. GPUs expose it as `__fmaf_rn` and contract `a*b+c` expressions automatically (controllable via `-fmad=`), and it entered the IEEE standard with 754-2008, after originating in the IBM RS/6000 line in 1990.
- **Accuracy:** one rounding instead of two, and — the deep part — *the unrounded product can cancel against \(c\) exactly*, preserving information that the unfused sequence destroys.

**A verified example where the difference is total.** Take \(a = b = 1 + 2^{-52}\), \(c = -(1 + 2^{-51})\), in binary64. The exact value of \(a\cdot b + c\) is \(2^{-104}\): expanding \((1+2^{-52})^2 = 1 + 2^{-51} + 2^{-104}\), the leading terms cancel against \(c\) to the last bit. Executed both ways:

- Unfused: \(\mathrm{fl}(a\cdot b)\) rounds \(1 + 2^{-51} + 2^{-104}\) to \(1 + 2^{-51}\) (the \(2^{-104}\) is far below half an ulp); then \((1+2^{-51}) - (1+2^{-51}) = \mathbf{0.0}\) exactly. Relative error: 100%.
- FMA: \(\mathbf{2^{-104}} = 4.93\times10^{-32}\), *exact*. (Verified by execution: `0x3970000000000000`.)

The unfused path is not "less accurate" — it returns a value with no correct digits. The FMA returns the exact answer. This is the sharpest possible demonstration that the fused operation is *mathematically different*, not merely an optimization.

**Error-free transformations: the FMA as an error microscope.** Because the FMA sees the unrounded product, it can be used to *extract the rounding error of a regular multiply*:

$$
p = \mathrm{fl}(a \cdot b), \qquad e = \mathrm{fma}(a, b, -p), \qquad a\cdot b = p + e \text{ exactly.}
$$

Verified: for \(a = 0.1, b = 0.2\), \(p = 0.020000000000000004\) and \(e = -1.665\times10^{-18}\), and \(p + e\) equals the infinitely precise product *exactly*. The pair \((p, e)\) is called **twoProduct**; its addition-only sibling **twoSum** (\(s = \mathrm{fl}(a+b)\), \(e = b - (s-a)\), exact when no overflow — verified on \(1.0 + 2^{-53}\), recovering \(e = 2^{-53}\) exactly) needs no FMA at all and goes back to Møller and Knuth in 1965.

This is the hinge between today's topic and everything downstream:

- **Kahan compensation** (1.4's fix) is twoSum applied at every step.
- **Double-double arithmetic** represents a number as an unevaluated sum of two doubles (head + tail) and implements arithmetic via error-free transformations — a software 106-bit format.
- **Compensated dot products and GEMM** (Ozaki et al.'s original 2012 line of work) make *accurate and reproducible* matrix multiplication out of twoSum/twoProduct building blocks.
- **IEEE 754-2019** standardized the pattern as recommended operations — `augmentedAddition`, `augmentedSubtraction`, `augmentedMultiplication` — motivated substantially by *reproducible* summation: if each step yields its exact error as data, you can accumulate order-independently (Ahrens, Demmel & Nguyen, ARITH-25 2018, showed a hardware target that would put reproducible dot products within ~2× of ordinary ones).

**Where the FMA lives in the machines you benchmark.** Three levels:

1. **CPU FP64 pipelines:** the FMA *is* the vector FP unit. LU factorization's trailing update — \(\sim\)85–90% of HPL's FLOPs — compiles to FMA streams; per-section 1.1, each FMA deposits at most one \(u = 2^{-53}\) relative error, which is the atom counted by the backward-error analysis of 1.4.
2. **GPU tensor cores:** a tensor-core instruction multiplies FP16/BF16/TF32 *fragments* and accumulates in FP32. Here is the precision fact to carry forward: an FP16 significand is 11 bits, so the product of two FP16 significands needs at most 22 bits — and an FP32 significand has 24. **Tensor-core products are exact in the FP32 accumulator.** The accumulation of many exact products rounds only per accumulation step. HMMA is, in effect, a batched, hardware-parallel cousin of "multiply exactly, round once" — the FMA principle at matrix scale.
3. **Integer matrix units:** INT8 tensor cores accumulate INT8×INT8 partial products into INT32 *with no rounding at all* — integer arithmetic is exact until the accumulator overflows. Keep this in mind; it is the foundation of today's first development.

**The caveat.** FMA contraction changes results bitwise relative to separate multiply and add (compilers may contract with or without your consent — C99's `FP_CONTRACT` pragma exists for this), so it is one more source of the run-to-run bit differences of 1.3. And the FMA only protects the *one* product it fuses; if \(c\) already carries prior error (as in a long accumulation), the fused operation faithfully rounds \(a b + c\) — garbage included. It extends exactness locally, not globally.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 FP64 emulation ships: the Ozaki scheme moves from papers into cuBLAS

**What happened.** In October 2025 (CUDA Toolkit 13.0 Update 2), NVIDIA shipped FP64 matrix-multiply *emulation* in cuBLAS on Blackwell — DGEMM executed on INT8 tensor cores via the **Ozaki scheme**, with an automatic dynamic precision (ADP) framework that inspects the inputs, chooses the decomposition parameters, guarantees accuracy equal to or better than native FP64, and falls back to native execution when emulation would not win (NVIDIA developer blog, Brower, Gunnels & Lopez, Oct 24, 2025). Reported application results include a 1.5× end-to-end speedup for Quantum ESPRESSO (nearly 3× when relaxing to 39 mantissa bits) and ZGEMM acceleration in BerkeleyGW. Since then, NVIDIA's published platform specifications have gone a step further: Rubin lists ~33 TFLOP/s of native FP64 alongside an explicit *emulated DGEMM* figure of ~200 TFLOP/s. Reports in September 2026 (attributed to Shibaura Institute of Technology) indicate the CRT-based **Ozaki Scheme II** has now entered CUDA via the 13.4 developer-preview line, with emulated DGEMM up to ~175 TFLOP/s on B200 and ~295 TFLOP/s for ZGEMM; NVIDIA's own blog had already committed to Ozaki-II integration, though I could not verify the 13.4 specifics against NVIDIA's release notes directly — treat the September figures as reported, not confirmed.

**What technically changed.** The Ozaki scheme is today's section 1.5 scaled from scalars to matrices — an *error-free transformation of an entire GEMM*. Scheme I slices each FP64 operand's significand into INT8-sized limbs (fixed-point, after exponent alignment), computes all limb-pair products with INT8 tensor cores — where products accumulate *exactly* into INT32, zero rounding in the inner loop — and reconstructs the FP64 result from the exact partial products. Every rounding is deliberately pushed out of the hot loop into a cheap, controlled reconstruction step. The number of limb-pairs is data-dependent (it grows when the matrix's exponents span wide ranges — the fixed-point alignment needs more bits), which is precisely why cuBLAS needs the ADP machinery to decide, per call, whether emulation is safe and profitable, and why a fallback path exists. The lineage runs directly through today's topics: Ozaki's 2012 scheme grew out of accurate/reproducible matrix multiplication via error-free transformations (twoSum/twoProduct), first ported to FP16 tensor cores by Mukunoki et al. (2020), then to INT8 units, with Scheme II (Ozaki, Uchino & Imamura, 2025) replacing limb-splitting with modular residues under the Chinese remainder theorem, and research implementations (e.g. EmuGEMM, 2026) fusing the decomposition/reconstruction into single kernels to recover up to 81% of INT8 peak — beating even cuBLAS TF32 on large shapes.

**Why it matters.** Blackwell's INT8:FP64 throughput ratio is roughly 112:1 (EmuGEMM's measured accounting; native FP64 DGEMM measured dropping from ~58 TF on GH200 to ~36 TF on B200, and to ~1.3 TF on B300-class parts where FP64 silicon nearly vanishes). If FP64 can be *synthesized* from the INT8/FP8 units that AI economics keeps making cheaper, the FP64 starvation of AI-centric GPUs stops being a hard wall — which is exactly how NVIDIA is positioning it, and why DOE's Genesis Mission has identified Ozaki-style emulation as its fallback path for FP64 science on AI hardware.

**Connection to today's curriculum, precisely.** The whole construction stands on: exact low-precision products (the 22-bits-fit-in-24 argument of 1.5, in integer form), a single controlled rounding at reconstruction (1.1's correctly-rounded ideal), and the recognition that the accuracy *budget* is data-dependent — matrices spanning orders of magnitude need more limbs, exactly the magnitude-gap sensitivity of 1.3/1.4 showing up as an *engineering cost* rather than a silent error. What AMD's critics (next item) call "not IEEE-compliant" is, in today's vocabulary: the emulation does not reproduce the exact rounding sequence of native FP64 hardware — it produces a *different*, bounded error, which is a different contract than 1.1's per-operation guarantee.

**What to watch.** Whether emulation coverage expands beyond GEMM-shaped work into the vector/streaming kernels that dominate general HPC codes, whether ADP-style input analysis becomes a standard BLAS feature, and whether the next TOP500 HPL submissions begin running on emulated DGEMM paths — which would make the FP64 list itself a software-numerics artifact.

### 2.2 AMD's counter-bet: the MI430X and the case for native FP64

**What happened.** At its Advancing AI event in July 2026, AMD unveiled the Instinct **MI430X**, an HPC-focused sibling of the MI455X, specifying **288 TFLOP/s of native FP64**, 432 GB HBM4, and 23.3 TB/s of memory bandwidth, with availability in early 2027. It is slated for ORNL's Discovery system (2028) and France's Alice Recoque. AMD's positioning is explicit: "This is hardware double precision, not emulation... That distinction is everything" (Vamsi Boppana, AMD) — nearly 9× Rubin's 33 TFLOP/s native FP64.

**What technically changed.** The announcement is the public crystallization of a genuine technical disagreement. AMD Fellow Nick Malaya's critique of Ozaki-style emulation (HPCwire, March 2026): (i) it is not IEEE-compliant and does not reproduce FP64 hardware results; (ii) matrices whose elements span orders of magnitude suffer accuracy problems — the data-dependence of the limb count again; (iii) on non-square matrices the emulation can drop below native FP64 speed; and (iv) fewer than 10% of real HPC applications are structured around DGEMM enough to benefit — vector and irregular kernels get nothing. AMD's design principle is the *byte-to-FLOP ratio*: raise FP64 throughput in lockstep with HBM bandwidth (MI430X raises both ~2.5–3.7× over MI355X) so the roofline shape of existing codes is preserved, rather than collapsing FP64 and relying on software recovery.

**Why it matters.** This is the precision-vs-speed trade of tomorrow's topic (HPL-3.10) being litigated with silicon budgets. NVIDIA's argument: FP64 pipes that cannot be fed by available bandwidth are wasted area — provision FP64 to saturate the memory system, and synthesize the rest from tensor cores. AMD's argument: rounding guarantees (1.1) and the decades of backward-error analysis built on them (1.4) are load-bearing for validated scientific codes; substituting a different error contract (2.1) makes every code's numerical certification a re-validation project. Both companies will ship both paths — AMD says it will support Ozaki emulation on its own chips as software — so the disagreement is about the *default*, which is a bet on what the workload mix of 2028–2030 actually is.

**Connection to today's topics.** Every clause of the debate is today's material: what a rounding guarantee is worth (1.1), what happens when operand magnitudes span ranges (1.3, 1.4), and what exact-arithmetic hardware primitives buy you (1.5). The reader who understood sections 1.1–1.5 can evaluate both vendors' claims directly, without marketing as an intermediary — which is precisely the skill this curriculum is building toward HPL-21 and HPL-24.

**What to watch.** Discovery's and Alice Recoque's HPL and HPCG results (native-FP64 machines competing with emulation-heavy ones on the same lists); whether DOE's Genesis Mission systems deploy Ozaki paths in production codes; and independent (not vendor) studies of Ozaki accuracy on real application matrices — the current gap in the public record.
