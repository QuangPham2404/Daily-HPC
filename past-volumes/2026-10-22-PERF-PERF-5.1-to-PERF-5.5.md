## 0. Concise Summary

Yesterday's PERF module closed with a taxonomy of limits: compute, memory bandwidth, latency, communication, synchronization. Today we turn the first two of those into a quantitative, visual instrument — the **Roofline model**, introduced by Williams, Waterman, and Patterson in 2009.

The model rests on one kernel property and two machine properties. The kernel property is **arithmetic intensity** (PERF-5.1): FLOPs performed per byte moved from a stated memory level. The machine properties are the **compute roof** (PERF-5.3) — peak arithmetic throughput — and the **bandwidth roof** (PERF-5.4) — the sloped line `performance = intensity × bandwidth`. Plotted on log-log axes (PERF-5.2), these form the eponymous "roof": a flat ceiling and a diagonal ramp meeting at the **ridge point** (PERF-5.5), the intensity at which the dominant limit switches from data movement to arithmetic.

The ridge point is the single most useful number the model gives you. On an NVIDIA B200 it is about 4.6 FP64 FLOP/byte; a CSR sparse matrix-vector product runs at roughly 0.15, a 7-point stencil around 0.2–0.6, and a large GEMM above 1000. That one-line comparison explains why HPL achieves a large fraction of peak while HPCG — running essentially the same multiply-accumulate arithmetic on the same hardware — gets only a few percent, and why no amount of kernel tuning changes that gap: those kernels sit on different sides of the ridge.

In the developments section we use the day's concepts as an analytical lens on the current hardware cycle: the arrival of HBM4-class memory (AMD's MI400 series advertises 23.3 TB/s per GPU — nearly 3× a B200, lifting the sloped roof for every memory-bound code), an SC'25 workshop paper that extends Roofline to tightly-coupled CPU-GPU superchips by adding chip-to-chip links as extra diagonals, and a 2026 preprint arguing that on "FP64-starved" AI GPUs the FP64 compute roof falls so low that the ridge point collapses to ~0.16 FLOP/byte, turning even stencils into compute-bound kernels — a contested claim, but a perfect ridge-point exercise.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 From "which limit?" to "how far from the limit?"

Module 4 gave you a vocabulary for bottlenecks: PERF-4.4 said a kernel is compute-limited when arithmetic throughput dominates, PERF-4.5 said it is memory-bandwidth-limited when data movement dominates. That classification is binary — it tells you *which* wall you hit, but not *how hard* you are hitting it, and it gives no hint of the best achievable performance for a specific kernel on a specific machine.

The Roofline model answers the two questions that naturally follow:

1. **What is the best performance this machine could possibly deliver for this kernel?**
2. **How far below that best is my current implementation, and in which direction should I optimize?**

The insight that makes the model work is that both questions are governed by a single kernel-side quantity — arithmetic intensity — interacting with two machine-side ceilings. Everything else in today's lesson is just careful bookkeeping around those three numbers.

### 1.2 PERF-5.1 — Arithmetic intensity

**Definition.** The arithmetic intensity of a kernel is

$$I = \frac{W}{Q}$$

where *W* is the number of floating-point operations the kernel performs and *Q* is the number of bytes it moves **from a stated memory level**. The units are FLOP/byte. The "stated memory level" qualifier is not pedantry — it is the hook on which the entire hierarchical extension (PERF-5.7, next week) hangs. For now, assume the level is the GPU's HBM or the CPU's DRAM, i.e. the level behind the last-level cache.

**Why it exists.** A machine offers two currencies of performance: FLOP/s and byte/s. A kernel's intensity is the exchange rate between them. If a kernel does 2 FLOP per byte, then feeding it 1 TB/s of data supplies enough operands for 2 TFLOP/s of work — whether the arithmetic units can consume that much is a separate question. Intensity is the natural coordinate along which every such question is asked.

**Counting rules.** Two conventions matter, and mixing them is the most common source of wrong roofline reasoning:

- **FLOPs.** One fused multiply-add (FMA) counts as 2 FLOP. Libraries and benchmark rules (HPL's 2n³/3 for LU, GEMM's 2mnk) are the authority; when in doubt, count what the algorithm *requires*, not what the compiler emitted.
- **Bytes.** Count actual traffic at the stated level, not allocation size. Caches mean DRAM traffic can be less than the sum of array sizes — but only for *reused* data. The compulsory traffic — every element read at least once, every output written at least once — cannot be cached away.

**Worked examples.** It is worth computing the intensity of a few canonical kernels once, by hand, because they anchor your intuition for everything that follows. All examples assume 64-bit floating point (8 bytes/element).

*STREAM Triad (`y = a·x + y` on vectors of length n).* Work: 2n FLOP. Traffic: read x (8n bytes), read y (8n), write y (8n); the scalar a is negligible. Intensity: 2n/24n ≈ **0.083 FLOP/byte**. This is close to the floor of what is achievable — almost pure streaming.

*Sparse matrix-vector product (SpMV), CSR format.* For each nonzero aᵢⱼ we must read its value (8 bytes) and its column index (4 bytes, 32-bit int), do one multiply-add (2 FLOP), and eventually write yᵢ (amortized, one write per row). The vector x is accessed irregularly; assume it is either cached or re-read — even being generous, x-traffic per nonzero is small on average. A defensible estimate is 12–16 bytes per 2 FLOP, giving I ≈ **0.13–0.17 FLOP/byte**. Notice what determines this number: not the algorithm's FLOP count, but the *storage format* — the index stream is pure overhead, 4 bytes per nonzero carrying zero arithmetic. This is the fundamental reason sparse linear algebra is memory-bound on every machine that exists: Bell and Garland's classic SC09 paper on GPU SpMV states the design goal explicitly — since an efficient SpMV kernel *should* be memory-bound, the measure of success is the fraction of peak bandwidth achieved.

*7-point stencil (one 3-D grid update).* Work: 7 multiply-adds per cell = 14 FLOP. Traffic without any cache reuse: 7 input values + 1 output per cell = 64 bytes → I ≈ 0.22. With perfect spatial blocking, each neighbor value can be reused from cache when it is itself a center cell, so the compulsory DRAM traffic drops to roughly "read the grid once, write it once" ≈ 2–3 values per cell ≈ 16–24 bytes → I ≈ **0.6–0.9**. The true value sits between these and depends on the cache hierarchy — a fact that will matter enormously in PERF-5.7.

*Blocked GEMM, square n×n, C = A·B + C.* Work: 2n³ FLOP. Traffic: read A, read B, write C ≈ 3·8n² = 24n² bytes *if the blocking reuses each element enough* — more precisely, DRAM traffic is O(n²) for any reasonable blocking, and the ideal is each matrix element read/written a small constant number of times. Taking ~24n²–32n² bytes: I ≈ 2n³/32n² = **n/16 to n/4 FLOP/byte**. For n = 4096 that is roughly 256–1024 FLOP/byte. The key contrast with the stencil: GEMM's reuse is *algorithmic* — the blocking structure multiplies each loaded byte through many output elements — so intensity grows with problem size.

**Intensity is a property of the kernel, not the machine.** But it does depend on things you control: data layout (CSR vs. ELLPACK changes the index overhead), precision (halving element size from FP64 to FP32 halves the bytes per element and *doubles* intensity for the same FLOP count), and loop structure (fusion merges two kernels' traffic streams, see below).

| Kernel (FP64) | Approx. arithmetic intensity | Character |
|---|---|---|
| STREAM Triad | ~0.08 FLOP/B | pure streaming floor |
| CSR SpMV | ~0.13–0.17 FLOP/B | format-overhead dominated |
| 7-pt stencil | ~0.2–0.9 FLOP/B | cache-reuse dependent |
| Blocked GEMM (n=4096) | ~256–1024 FLOP/B | algorithmic reuse |

Keep these four numbers in mind; we will place them on real machines shortly, and the placement will explain most of what you observe in HPL versus HPCG.

### 1.3 PERF-5.2 — Roofline axes

The Roofline chart is a two-dimensional log-log plot:

- **x-axis:** arithmetic intensity I, in FLOP/byte, log scale;
- **y-axis:** attainable performance P, in FLOP/s, log scale.

The model itself is one line of algebra. A kernel cannot exceed the compute hardware's peak, and it cannot exceed what the memory system can feed it:

$$P \leq \min\left(P_{max},\; I \times B\right)$$

where P_max is the peak arithmetic throughput and B the bandwidth of the memory level you chose when computing I. Plot both bounds on the chart: `P = P_max` is a horizontal line, and `P = I·B` is a line of slope 1 in log-log space (a decade of intensity buys a decade of performance). Together they trace the shape of a roof — flat ceiling meeting diagonal ramp — and the model takes its name from that silhouette.

**Why log-log?** Because the two quantities span orders of magnitude. Intensity ranges from ~0.08 (Triad) to ~1000 (large GEMM) — four decades; achievable performance similarly spans TFLOP/s ranges across precisions. On linear axes the entire left half of the chart would be crushed against the origin and the model would be unreadable. The log transformation also converts the product `I·B` into a straight line, which is what makes the graph usable as a *visual bound*: you can eyeball whether a measured point sits under the roof.

**How to read a kernel on the chart.** Williams, Waterman, and Patterson's metaphor is the cleanest: draw a vertical line at the kernel's intensity — a "column" — and see where it hits the roof. If it hits the *slanted* part, the kernel's performance ceiling is set by the memory system; if it hits the *flat* part, the ceiling is set by arithmetic throughput. Then plot the kernel's *achieved* performance as a dot on the same column. The vertical distance from dot to roof is unrealized potential; which segment of roof the column hits tells you what limits the ideal case. The original 2009 CACM paper puts it succinctly: a kernel with intensity 2 FLOP/byte on their example machine is compute-bound, one with intensity 1 is memory-bound — and the roof itself doesn't move when you change kernels, so one chart characterizes a machine for its whole workload mix.

```
 FLOP/s (log)
   │
Pmax ┤────────────────────────  compute roof (flat)
   │                      ╱
   │                  ╱   bandwidth roof (slope 1)
   │              ╱        P = I · B
   │          ╱
   │      ╱        •  kernel dot (achieved)
   │  ╱           │  ← gap = unrealized potential
   ┼──────────────┼───────────────→ I (FLOP/byte, log)
              I_kernel   I_ridge
```

Two more reading conventions matter. First, the chart is a *bound*, not a prediction: it says you cannot be above the roof, not that you will reach it. Second, both axes are per-kernel — the dot for the whole application is a weighted average that usually hides everything interesting, which is why roofline analysis is done per kernel or per loop nest.

### 1.4 PERF-5.3 — Compute roof

The compute roof is the flat line: the maximum rate at which the arithmetic units can execute the kernel's operations. Its exact value depends on what exactly you count, and there is an honest hierarchy of choices.

**Theoretical peak** (PERF-4.1) is computed from the hardware description: cores × clock × per-core issue width × units. For a GPU this is straightforward to state per precision path. Take the NVIDIA HGX B200's individual Blackwell GPU as a concrete machine (datasheet numbers):

| Precision path | Peak (per GPU) |
|---|---|
| FP64 / FP64 Tensor Core | 37 TFLOP/s |
| FP32 | 75 TFLOP/s |
| FP16/BF16 Tensor Core (dense) | 2,250 TFLOP/s |
| FP8 Tensor Core (dense) | 4,500 TFLOP/s |
| FP4 Tensor Core (dense) | 9,000 TFLOP/s |

**The roof you draw must match the path your kernel actually uses.** A hand-written FP64 stencil uses the vector FP64 pipe (37 TFLOP/s on this part); a cuBLAS FP8 GEMM rides the tensor cores (4,500 TFLOP/s). Comparing an FP64 kernel's dot to the FP8 roof produces a meaningless gap. This sounds obvious, yet a large fraction of "my kernel is only at 2% of peak" complaints dissolve once the correct roof is drawn — 37 of 4,500 is about 0.8%, and the kernel may be doing fine *for its precision path*.

**Ceilings below the roof.** Williams et al. drew a useful distinction between the roof (what ideal code achieves) and *ceilings* — progressively lower flat lines obtained by adding realistic constraints: no FMA (halve effective FLOP issue), no vectorization (SIMD lanes idle), non-associative reductions forcing different instruction mixes, and so on. Each constraint lowers the applicable horizontal line; a well-optimized kernel's dot should touch the lowest ceiling that its constraints genuinely impose, and the distance between adjacent ceilings is the payoff available from removing that constraint (e.g. getting the compiler to vectorize).

**Empirical vs. theoretical.** For honest work, prefer an *empirical* compute roof: the best measured rate of a highly optimized representative kernel (PERF-4.2's "sustained peak") — in practice a vendor-tuned GEMM at the target precision, which typically lands at 85–95% of theoretical. Doing so moves the roof down slightly but removes the systematic overstatement, and — as we will see with the ridge point — the ridge moves with it.

### 1.5 PERF-5.4 — Bandwidth roof

The sloped line `P = I·B` expresses a conservation law: you cannot compute on data you have not moved. If the memory system delivers B bytes/s and the kernel needs I FLOP per byte, then the FLOP supply rate is at most I·B — regardless of how fast the arithmetic units are.

**B is per memory level, and the level must match the one used for I.** If intensity counts bytes from DRAM, the roof uses DRAM bandwidth; if it counts bytes from L2, the roof uses L2 bandwidth (which is several times higher). Mixing levels — DRAM intensity against an L2 roof — is the second classic roofline error, and it always flatters the kernel. The per-level refinement becomes the full hierarchical roofline in PERF-5.7; today, stay at the DRAM/HBM level.

**Peak vs. achieved bandwidth.** The peak figure comes from the specification: 8 TB/s HBM3e on a B200; AMD's new Instinct MI455X specifies 23.3 TB/s of HBM4. But the *achievable* fraction depends on access pattern, and the dependence is severe. Bell and Garland's SC09 measurements on a saxpy kernel with varying stride are the canonical demonstration: unit-stride accesses achieved more than ten times the effective bandwidth of large-stride (33-element) accesses on the same hardware. A roof drawn at 8 TB/s is the *upper* sloped line; a kernel with scattered accesses may be unable to exceed 2 TB/s no matter what — in which case the honest roof for that access pattern is lower, and the gap to the spec roof is telling you something about coalescing (a PERF-12 topic), not about arithmetic.

**Empirical bandwidth.** The classical model's authors deliberately measured sustainable bandwidth with progressively optimized microbenchmarks (STREAM-style, as in the CACM paper) rather than trusting spec sheets, and drew the roof at the measured value. NVIDIA's roofline tooling in Nsight Compute follows the same philosophy, plotting a kernel's achieved point against the machine's limits — and the same blog shows the subtlety: in double precision on a V100, the quoted *machine balance point* is 7.5 FLOP/byte, computed against realistic bandwidth rather than the theoretical number you would get from dividing peak FLOP/s by peak bytes/s. Empirical roofs shift the whole sloped region down and the ridge leftward — slightly, but consistently.

One important asymmetry with the compute roof: the bandwidth roof is largely *not* under your control. You can raise intensity (next topic) or improve access efficiency, but the byte/s a given memory level can physically deliver is fixed by the hardware you are sitting on. That is why HPC procurement arguments are, to a first approximation, arguments about where the sloped roof sits.

### 1.6 PERF-5.5 — Ridge point

The two roofs meet where `P_max = I·B`, i.e. at intensity

$$I_{ridge} = \frac{P_{max}}{B}$$

— the **ridge point** (also called the machine balance point). It is a property of the machine *and* the precision path, and it partitions the intensity axis into two regimes with different physics:

- **Left of the ridge (I < I_ridge):** the memory-bound regime. The kernel's column hits the sloped roof. Even *perfect* code cannot exceed I·B, because the arithmetic units would starve waiting for operands.
- **Right of the ridge (I > I_ridge):** the compute-bound regime. The column hits the flat roof. Even with infinitely fast memory, the arithmetic units saturate first.

**Concrete ridge points.** Compute a few, using the machines already on the table:

| Machine / path | P_max | B | Ridge point |
|---|---|---|---|
| HGX B200 GPU, FP64 | 37 TFLOP/s | 8 TB/s | ~4.6 FLOP/B |
| HGX B200 GPU, FP8 tensor | 4,500 TFLOP/s | 8 TB/s | ~562 FLOP/B |
| HGX B200 GPU, FP4 tensor | 9,000 TFLOP/s | 8 TB/s | ~1,125 FLOP/B |
| V100, FP64 (NVIDIA blog, empirical bandwidth) | — | — | ~7.5 FLOP/B |
| GH200, FP64 vector (SC'25 paper figures) | 33 TFLOP/s | 3.8 TB/s HBM | ~8.7 FLOP/B |
| GH200, FP64 vector, *via C2C link* (same paper) | 33 TFLOP/s | 0.4 TB/s C2C | ~82.5 FLOP/B |

Three lessons fall out of this table.

**First, the same chip has many ridge points.** A B200's FP64 ridge is 4.6 FLOP/byte — comfortably above SpMV (~0.15) and stencils (~0.2–0.9), so those kernels are hard memory-bound on it. But its FP8-tensor ridge is 562 — *far above even a large GEMM* (~1000 is only just above it; most real GEMMs at typical sizes sit below). This is the arithmetic behind a familiar industry pattern: AI accelerators are built with enormous tensor roofs relative to their bandwidth, so AI workloads (GEMMs at moderate-to-high intensity) land compute-bound, while scientific FP64 codes land memory-bound on the same silicon. The ratio P/B *is* the machine's design philosophy in one number.

**Second, the ridge moves when either roof moves.** Double the bandwidth and the ridge halves (more kernels become compute-bound — their columns now hit the flat part); double the compute roof and the ridge doubles (more kernels become memory-bound). When AMD's MI455X raises HBM bandwidth to 23.3 TB/s — nearly 3× a B200 — every memory-bound kernel's ceiling rises proportionally *with zero code changes*, and simultaneously the FP64 ridge (on the HPC-oriented MI430X, 288 TFLOP/s of hardware FP64) shifts relative to where it sat on previous generations. Tracking a vendor's P and B tells you, before any benchmark, which of your kernels will get faster on the new machine "for free."

**Third, the denominator can be a link, not a memory.** The GH200 row computed against the C2C link (0.4 TB/s) rather than HBM (3.8 TB/s) comes from the SC'25 superchip roofline study discussed below: when the GPU executes FLOPs on data resident in CPU memory, the binding constraint is the chip-to-chip link, and the effective ridge jumps to ~82 FLOP/byte — a regime in which almost nothing is memory-bound *by HBM standards* but almost everything is *link-bound*. Same formula, different bottleneck.

**The ridge point as an optimization compass.** Once you know a kernel's intensity and the machine's ridge, the model converts directly into strategy:

| Situation | Diagnosis | First-line actions |
|---|---|---|
| I < I_ridge, dot far below sloped roof | memory-bound, bandwidth-inefficient | fix access pattern: coalescing, layout, avoid wasted traffic |
| I < I_ridge, dot on the sloped roof | memory-bound, bandwidth-optimal | only raising I helps: blocking/tiling, kernel fusion, precision |
| I > I_ridge, dot far below flat roof | compute-bound, arithmetic-inefficient | instruction mix, vectorization, tensor cores, reduce non-FMA work |
| I > I_ridge, dot on the flat roof | at the machine's limit | only more hardware, or a different algorithm, helps |

Note the second row carefully, because it is the roofline's most important practical verdict: **a memory-bound kernel sitting on its sloped roof is already perfect with respect to bandwidth.** The only escape from the I·B ceiling is to raise I — block for cache reuse, fuse producers and consumers so intermediate arrays never touch DRAM, or move to narrower precision. The NVIDIA blog's BerkeleyGW case study walks exactly this path in reverse: a double-precision kernel at intensity 7.39 FLOP/byte on a V100 whose balance point is 7.5 sat a hair on the memory-bound side; restructuring the loop raised intensity to ~20 (past the ridge, onto the flat roof), and separately replacing high-latency division instructions raised the achieved rate from 2.5 to 2.9 TFLOP/s and halved runtime. Two mechanisms, two separate lines of attack — and the roofline told them which to pull first.

**Why this explains HPL vs. HPCG.** You have run both on real hardware, so anchor here. HPL's inner life is a stream of GEMMs at intensity in the hundreds: deep on the compute-bound side of any FP64 ridge. That is why a tuned HPL run reaches a large fraction of the FP64 roof — the arithmetic units are the binding resource and they can be kept busy. HPCG's core is sparse matrix-vector products and stencil-like sweeps at intensity ~0.1–0.5: deep on the memory-bound side, *below the sloped roof's crossing on every machine ever built*. No kernel engineering moves a dot that is on the sloped roof; HPCG's few-percent-of-peak results are not a failure of optimization but a faithful measurement of where those kernels live on the roofline. The two benchmarks are, in effect, a matched pair that measures both halves of the roof.

**What the ridge point does not tell you.** The roofline is a throughput bound. It knows nothing of latency (PERF-4.6): a kernel at intensity 100 on a machine with ridge 5 can still run far below the flat roof because dependent chains stall the pipelines — a dot below the roof is *evidence of* unrealized potential, never an *explanation* of it. It also knows nothing of synchronization (PERF-4.8), communication (PERF-4.7), or occupancy-driven latency hiding, and its two-roof picture assumes the denominators (FLOPs, bytes) are unambiguous — which cache hierarchies complicate. These gaps are exactly the next two lessons: PERF-5.6–5.7 (computing intensities in practice, with per-level roofs) and PERF-5.8 (where and why the model's predictions fail).

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 HBM4 arrives in volume: the bandwidth roof rises ~3×

**What happened.** The HBM4 generation has moved from standardization to shipping products. JEDEC's HBM4 standard doubles the interface per stack to 2048 bits (against 1024 for HBM3/HBM3E), specifies 24 Gb and 32 Gb layers, and allows 4- to 16-high stacks, with initial speed bins to 6.4 Gb/s and higher rates subsequently standardized — roughly up to 8 Gb/s across 2048 bits, i.e. up to ~2 TB/s per stack. At its Advancing AI event in July 2026, AMD launched the Instinct MI400 series: the MI455X carries 12 stacks / 432 GB of HBM4 with 23.3 TB/s peak theoretical bandwidth per GPU, and the HPC-oriented MI430X provides up to 288 TFLOP/s of hardware FP64.

**What technically changed.** The generational jump is mostly *width*, not pin speed: doubling the interface means per-stack bandwidth roughly doubles even at HBM3-class data rates — a departure from the previous cadence of pushing pins harder each generation. For a roofline reader the consequence is one number: on an MI455X, the sloped roof for DRAM-level intensity sits at 23.3 TB/s, against 8 TB/s on a B200-class part.

**Why it matters and how it connects.** Every kernel left of its ridge gets a proportional ceiling raise for free — which is precisely the population that HPC occupies (Section 1.6: SpMV, stencils, most of HPCG's life). Simultaneously, the machine balance shifts: if FP64 throughput grows more slowly than bandwidth across generations (as it has on AI-focused silicon), ridge points *fall*, pulling formerly compute-bound moderate-intensity kernels into the memory-bound regime. Two cautions for reading vendor numbers: these are *peak theoretical* figures — the empirical roof (measured triad/stream-class bandwidth) will land lower, as Section 1.5 argued; and peak bytes/s says nothing about the latency-bound small-message regime. Watch: measured STREAM-class results on MI400 systems, and how the FP64 ridge on MI430X compares with its predecessors once independent numbers exist.

### 2.2 Roofline for superchips: SC'25 workshop study on MI300A and GH200

**What happened.** A paper in the SC'25 workshops (*Roofline Analysis of Tightly-Coupled CPU-GPU Superchips: A Study on MI300A and GH200*, published November 2025) extends the classical model to AMD's MI300A APU and NVIDIA's Grace-Hopper, and reports empirical peaks for their shared memory interfaces under concurrent CPU+GPU execution.

**What technically changed.** Two extensions beyond the textbook two-roof picture. First, on GH200 — which has *two* memory systems (Grace's LPDDR and the GPU's HBM) joined by a 0.4 TB/s C2C link — the authors add the link as another diagonal below the 3.8 TB/s HBM roof, with the GPU's 33 TFLOP/s FP64 vector peak fixed on top. A GPU kernel operating on CPU-resident data is then bounded by the C2C diagonal, an effective ridge of ~82 FLOP/byte: the kernel is "memory-bound" with respect to a *link*, not a memory. Second, when CPU and GPU run concurrently they contend for both shared bandwidth and power in a power-constrained package, so the authors measure *contention-aware* roofs — the practical observation being that concurrently-executing producers and consumers can degrade each other non-linearly, and the roofline chart is the right place to expose that degradation as a lowered, workload-dependent roof.

**Why it matters.** This is the model from Section 1 adapting to topology: PERF-5.4's rule — "B is per memory level, and it must match the level used for I" — generalizes from cache levels to *links between memories*. As superchip designs (and CXL-attached memory, same principle) spread, the question "which roof am I under?" stops having a single answer, and tools increasingly draw multiple diagonals (Intel's cache-aware Advisor roofline does this for L1/L2/DRAM; Nsight Compute's hierarchical roofline sections for L1/L2/HBM). The study's benchmark data also quantifies an effect worth remembering: input *data values* matter on power-limited parts — random data costs measurably more energy per bit than zeros, so the achievable roof itself depends on the workload's bit patterns.

### 2.3 A preprint turns ridge-point arithmetic into a provocation: FP8 emulation of FP64

**What happened.** A 2026 arXiv preprint (arXiv:2606.06510, *FP8 is All You Need (Part 1)*) argues that on AI-optimized GPUs with weak native FP64 — the authors cite a B300-class part with ~1.3 TFLOP/s native FP64 against 8 TB/s of HBM — the FP64 ridge point collapses to 0.16 FLOP/byte. Their Tensor–Memory Equilibrium (TME) model extends the classical Roofline with three emulation parameters (compute multiplier α, bandwidth multiplier β, reconstruction latency γ; a fourth, deconstruction cost, was added after technical review) to analyze replacing FP64 arithmetic with FP8 tensor-core matrix ops composed via the CRT-based Ozaki Scheme II.

**What is claimed.** With the ridge at 0.16, *everything* above that intensity becomes compute-bound on the native FP64 pipe — including a 7-point stencil at intensity ≈0.5, which "should" run at the 4 TFLOP/s memory roof but is capped at ~1.3. The bandwidth the application needs is physically present but cannot be consumed by the arithmetic units. The paper's central claim is that FP8-tensor emulation lifts the FP64 compute ceiling back above the memory roof, restoring memory-bound kernels to their "natural" bandwidth-bound regime at full FP64 accuracy — projecting emulated FP64 throughput in the hundreds of TFLOP/s on B300-class and Rubin-class parts.

**How to read it.** Treat this as a preprint making a contested engineering argument, not settled fact — the specific B300 FP64 floor and the accuracy contracts of the scheme are exactly the parts the community will fight over, and vendor datasheets should be checked before repeating any single number. But as an *exercise in ridge-point reasoning*, it is impeccable and directly instructive: it is the first development in this series where the compute roof falls so far that the crossing point moves *left* of nearly all real kernels, inverting the usual AI-pattern of compute-bound GEMMs. The lesson to keep, independent of the paper's fate: ridge-point collapse is what happens on any part where one precision path is starved while bandwidth keeps growing — and the roofline tells you this from two specification numbers before you write a line of code. Watch: independent measurements of native FP64 rates on Blackwell-Ultra-class parts, and any published Ozaki-scheme implementations on real hardware.
