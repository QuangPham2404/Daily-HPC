## 0. Concise Summary

Saturday's volume ended with the definition of a collective — a group-coordinated communication operation with no tags, matched by program order, whose wire pattern is chosen by the library. Today we open the toolbox. Module 4's next five topics are the **fundamental data-movement collectives**: broadcast (one rank's data replicated to all), reduce (all ranks' data combined to one), all-reduce (combined and delivered to all), scatter and gather (one rank's buffer split into distinct pieces, and the reverse), and all-gather (everyone's distinct pieces concatenated for everyone). These five are not five unrelated calls. They are one family, organized by two questions: *who ends up holding data* (one rank or all ranks), and *what exactly is delivered* (an identical copy, a distinct segment, or a reduced combination).

The technical engine of the whole volume is a single idea: **every collective has multiple implementations, and message size decides which one wins**. Tree-shaped algorithms (binomial tree, recursive doubling) finish in ⌈log₂ p⌉ steps — optimal for latency — but each step pushes the *full* message across a link, so their bandwidth cost grows like n·log p. Ring-shaped, decomposed algorithms (scatter-then-roll, reduce-scatter-then-all-gather) take p−1 steps but move only 1/p of the message per step, holding the per-rank byte cost near 2(p−1)/p·n — essentially independent of p. This is why MPICH switches algorithms at a size threshold, why HPL exposes a six-way `BCASTs` tuning parameter, and why NCCL ships an internal cost model that picks among Ring, Tree, NVLS, CollNet, and PAT for every call.

The through-line that makes the family cohere is **composition**: long-message broadcast *is* scatter plus a ring roll; long-message reduce *is* reduce-scatter plus gather; and all-reduce — the workhorse of data-parallel training — *is* reduce-scatter plus all-gather, which is exactly the celebrated "ring allreduce" that crossed from the HPC literature into deep learning in 2017 and now drives most of the engineering attention in GPU communication stacks.

Two developments anchor the lesson in the real world: **NCCL 2.30/2.31** (mid-2026), which is busy moving these very collectives off GPU SMs and into copy engines, NVLink multicast, and device-initiated networking; and the **2017 Baidu ring-allreduce moment**, the historically important transfer of HPC collective know-how into deep learning that explains why an MPI textbook algorithm now runs on hundred-thousand-GPU clusters daily.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

We take the five topics in an order that lets each operation build on the previous one — broadcast → scatter/gather → all-gather → reduce → all-reduce — with curriculum IDs marking where each topic is covered. Throughout, p is the number of ranks, n the message size in elements, α the per-message latency, β the per-element transfer cost, and γ the per-element cost of the reduction arithmetic — the same α-β vocabulary from DIST-1.6 and DIST-1.7, extended with γ because reduction collectives do real computation, not just data movement.

### 1.0 The family picture

Before any algorithm, organize the family. Every classic collective answers two questions: how many ranks end up holding data (one or all), and what is delivered (copies of the same bytes, distinct segments, or values combined by an operation).

| Operation | Input state | Output state | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Broadcast** (DIST-4.2) | root holds n | all hold the same n | 1 → all, identical |
| **Scatter** (DIST-4.5) | root holds p·s | rank i holds segment i | 1 → all, distinct |
| **Gather** (DIST-4.5) | rank i holds segment i | root holds p·s | all → 1, distinct |
| **All-gather** (DIST-4.6) | rank i holds segment i | all hold p·s | all → all, distinct |
| **Reduce** (DIST-4.3) | each rank holds n | root holds ⊕ of all | all → 1, combined |
| **All-reduce** (DIST-4.4) | each rank holds n | all hold ⊕ of all | all → all, combined |
| *(Reduce-scatter, DIST-4.7 — next time)* | each rank holds n | rank i holds reduced segment i | all → all, combined |

Two structural facts fall out of this table. First, **duality**: gather is scatter with the arrows reversed, and Chan, Heimlich, Purkayastha, and van de Geijn show in their 2007 taxonomy that an implementation of any collective can be transformed into its dual by reversing the communication pattern (and adding or deleting reduction steps). Implementations exploit this literally — a library's gather is often its scatter algorithm run backward. Second, **the operations nest**: a broadcast is a scatter in which all segments happen to be identical; an all-gather is a gather whose result everyone keeps; an all-reduce is a reduce whose result everyone needs. This nesting is not just taxonomy — it is the blueprint for the bandwidth-optimal algorithms in sections 1.1 through 1.5.

Recall from DIST-1.7 that messages live in two regimes: small messages are latency-dominated (α rules), large messages are bandwidth-dominated (β·n rules). Every collective below therefore has (at least) two algorithms, and the guiding rule of the day is:

> **Trees minimize the number of steps; decomposition into segments plus ring movement minimizes the bytes each rank must push. The crossover point is a message size, and the library — not you — usually picks the side.**

Keep the α-β model from DIST-1.6 in mind as the yardstick: a p-step algorithm costs ≈ (steps)·α + (bytes on the critical path)·β.

### 1.1 Broadcast (DIST-4.2)

**The problem.** One rank — the *root* — holds a block of data that every rank must end up holding: initial conditions replicated to all ranks, a read-only lookup table, the neural-network weights at the start of data-parallel training, or, in HPL, the current panel of factored columns that the whole process grid needs before the trailing update can proceed. In MPI:

```c
MPI_Bcast(buffer, n, MPI_DOUBLE, root, comm);
```

Every rank in `comm` calls it with the same `root`, `count`, and datatype; on the root `buffer` is the source, elsewhere it is the destination. Like every collective, it is matched by call order within the communicator, not by tags (DIST-4.1).

**Naive: the linear root.** The root simply sends n bytes to each of the p−1 other ranks. Cost: (p−1)·(α + βn). The flaw is the root's network interface: it must emit (p−1)·n bytes through one NIC, and if the sends are issued one at a time, each pays a full α. On a 4,096-rank job that is 4,095 sequential message latencies before the last rank even starts.

**Binomial tree: latency-optimal.** Instead, let everyone who has the data forward it. At step k, every rank that already holds the message sends it to one rank that does not, doubling the set of holders each step:

```text
p = 8, message size n, 3 = ⌈log₂ 8⌉ steps:

step 0:   0 ──► 1
step 1:   0 ──► 2     1 ──► 3
step 2:   0 ──► 4     1 ──► 5     2 ──► 6     3 ──► 7
```

Cost: ⌈log₂ p⌉·α + ⌈log₂ p⌉·n·β. The latency term is optimal — information can double its reach at best once per hop. But look at the bandwidth term: **every step, the full n bytes crosses a link**, and the root (and every early receiver) re-sends the entire message at each step it participates. For p = 1,024 the critical path carries 10·n bytes. For a 100 MB panel on a fabric where each hop costs real time, that factor of log p is pure waste. A balanced binary tree caps any single rank's egress at 2n, but the critical path still moves the full message once per level — the log p·n·β term survives every tree variant.

**Long messages: scatter + ring roll.** The fix is to stop broadcasting n bytes and start broadcasting *segments*. Split the message into p chunks of n/p. Phase 1: **scatter** the chunks so rank i holds chunk i — a binomial-tree scatter, costing ⌈log₂ p⌉ steps that move n/p-sized pieces. Phase 2: **roll** the chunks around a ring for p−1 steps; at each step every rank sends its current chunk to a neighbor and receives a new one, so after p−1 steps every rank has seen all p chunks. Total per-rank egress: about (p−1)/p·n from each phase, ≈ 2(p−1)/p·n ≈ 2n — **independent of p**. The latency term grows to roughly (p−1)·α, which is why this loses for small messages and wins decisively for large ones.

This is not a textbook curiosity — it is a tuning dial you have already turned. HPL's `BCASTs` parameter selects among six virtual panel-broadcast topologies: increasing ring, increasing ring (modified), two variants of a two-proc ring, and two "Long" bandwidth-reducing variants. The netlib HPL algorithm documentation describes the Long variant precisely in the terms above: the panel "is chopped into Q equal pieces that are scattered across the Q processes" (scatter phase on a binary tree), and "the pieces are then rolled in Q−1 steps" using mutual exchanges — odd steps pair 0↔1, 2↔3, even steps pair Q−1↔0, 1↔2 — with "the total volume of communication independent of Q". The documented rationale: this algorithm "becomes competitive when the nodes are 'very fast' and the network (comparatively) 'very slow'" — a pure statement of the bandwidth-bound regime. Typical tuning guidance (e.g., Bright Computing's HPC notes) recommends the increasing-ring-modified variant in general, with the Long variants reserved for fast-node/slow-network systems. When you tuned `BCASTs` on the cluster, you were hand-picking the crossover point of this exact analysis.

**Modern footnote.** On NVSwitch systems the copy itself can be pushed into the fabric: NVLink multicast lets one write fan out inside the switch, which is the hardware version of "replication is cheaper than p sends." File that thought for DIST-7.5 (GPUDirect RDMA) and DIST-8.x.

### 1.2 Scatter and gather (DIST-4.5)

**Scatter.** The root holds p·s elements; rank i must receive its own distinct segment of s elements. This is the primitive for *distributing* work-decomposed data from a serial origin: a root reads a matrix from disk and scatters block-rows to the ranks that will factor them.

```c
MPI_Scatter(sendbuf, s, MPI_DOUBLE, recvbuf, s, MPI_DOUBLE, root, comm);
MPI_Scatterv(sendbuf, counts[], displs[], MPI_DOUBLE, ...);  // unequal segments
```

Note the API detail that trips everyone once: `sendcount` is the count sent *to each rank*, not the total. The `v` variants matter because real distributions are jagged — block-cyclic layouts, boundary rows, ragged partitions — and `MPI_Scatterv`/`MPI_Gatherv` carry per-rank counts and displacements so the library can still use its fast paths.

**A lower bound with teeth.** Broadcast could amortize: rank 1 can forward to rank 2 the same bytes it received. Scatter cannot. Every byte destined for rank i exists *only* at the root, so every one of the (p−1)·s bytes that leave the other ranks' final segments must first pass through the root's NIC. Root egress ≥ (p−1)·s ≈ the entire buffer — no tree, ring, or clever topology removes it. Trees still help *time*: a binomial scatter parallelizes the post-root hops and pipelines the root's own emissions, and the best the root can do is stream its (p−1)·s bytes back-to-back, giving roughly α + (p−1)·s·β. The lesson: scatter of large buffers is inherently root-bandwidth-bound, and if you find yourself scattering gigabytes per iteration, the fix is usually to stop having a root — which is precisely what parallel I/O and collective reading are for.

**Gather.** The exact mirror: rank i sends its s elements; the root assembles p·s in rank order.

```c
MPI_Gather(sendbuf, s, MPI_DOUBLE, recvbuf, p*s, MPI_DOUBLE, root, comm);
```

By duality, gather inherits scatter's analysis with ingress substituted for egress: the root must *receive* (p−1)·s bytes, so large gathers are root-ingress-bound. And by the duality result of Chan et al., implementations really do realize gather as a reversed scatter — the same tree, arrows flipped.

Gather is everywhere in practice: assembling distributed residuals at rank 0 for a convergence decision, collecting per-node statistics, writing parallel data to a serial file, or — in HPL — the row-swapping phase, where pivot-determined rows must be redistributed across process rows; the netlib documentation describes spreading panel rows across the process grid "with respect to the pivot array", a scatter/gather pattern whose counts are only known at runtime (the `MPI_Scatterv` situation, not the `MPI_Scatter` one).

**The anti-pattern that motivates the rest of the family.** The most common beginner composition is *gather to root, let the root compute something, broadcast the result* — a poor man's reduction. It works, but it serializes: the root ingest (p−1)·s bytes, computes alone, then re-emits the answer. The next three topics exist to kill this pattern.

### 1.3 All-gather (DIST-4.6)

**Semantics.** Every rank contributes its s-byte segment; every rank ends up with the full ordered concatenation of all p segments. It is "gather, and everyone keeps a copy" — equivalently, p simultaneous gathers, or a broadcast in which all p ranks are roots of their own segment:

```c
MPI_Allgather(sendbuf, s, MPI_DOUBLE, recvbuf, p*s, MPI_DOUBLE, comm);
```

All-gather turns *distributed* state into *replicated* state: each rank computes its piece of a preconditioner's diagonal block, then all-gather gives every rank the assembled global diagonal; each GPU holds one shard of a vector, and all-gather reconstructs the full vector everywhere. (The stencil-code halo exchange is the *neighborhood* cousin of this operation — MPI's `MPI_Neighbor_allgather` on a graph topology — same idea, restricted to a sparse participant set.)

**Ring all-gather.** Arrange the p ranks in a logical ring. Each rank starts holding its own segment. At each of p−1 steps, every rank forwards the newest segment it received to its right neighbor. After p−1 steps, every rank has received all p−1 foreign segments. Per-rank egress: exactly (p−1)·s — which is *optimal*, because each rank must receive (p−1)·s bytes and total egress equals total ingress, so the average per-rank egress is (p−1)·s and the ring achieves the average for every rank simultaneously. Cost: (p−1)·α + (p−1)·s·β.

**Recursive doubling.** At step k, rank r exchanges its accumulated block (2^k segments) with rank r ⊕ 2^k, then both concatenate. Steps: ⌈log₂ p⌉. Per-rank egress: s·(1 + 2 + 4 + … + p/2) = (p−1)·s — the *same* optimal byte count, reached in logarithmically fewer steps: log₂ p·α + (p−1)·s·β. The catch is bookkeeping: the received block must be interleaved into the right slots of the output buffer, and non-power-of-two p requires either padding or a different algorithm (Bruck's index-digit trick), which is why implementations keep a menu. MPICH-family libraries size-switch among recursive-doubling-style, ring, and Bruck-style algorithms for exactly this reason — Thakur, Rabenseifner, and Gropp's 2005 paper built that menu for every collective, with the explicit goal of "minimizing latency for short messages and minimizing bandwidth use for long messages."

The pattern from broadcast repeats exactly, and that is the point: **ring = fewest bytes, tree/doubling = fewest steps**, with the same per-rank byte lower bound (p−1)·s and the same size crossover. Learn it once here and you can predict the algorithm zoo for every collective that follows.

### 1.4 Reduce (DIST-4.3)

**Semantics.** Each rank holds n elements; an associative operation ⊕ (sum, max, logical AND, …) combines them; the result is delivered to the root only.

```c
MPI_Reduce(sendbuf, recvbuf, n, MPI_DOUBLE, MPI_SUM, root, comm);
```

MPI predefines the common operations (`MPI_SUM`, `MPI_PROD`, `MPI_MAX`, `MPI_MIN`, `MPI_LAND`, …) and lets you build custom ones with `MPI_Op_create(function, commute, &op)`, where you must declare whether your operation is commutative — the library uses that promise to widen its scheduling freedom.

**Why associativity is the whole game.** Tree reduction is legal only because (a⊕b)⊕c = a⊕(b⊕c) *mathematically*. But floating-point addition is **not** associative: (10²⁰ + 1) − 10²⁰ ≠ 10²⁰ + (1 − 10²⁰) in IEEE arithmetic. MPI does not mandate a reduction order, so `MPI_SUM` over the same data can legitimately produce different last-bit answers depending on rank count, algorithm choice, or run-to-run scheduling. For most codes this is noise; for reproducibility-sensitive work (verification, regression tests, bitwise-reproducible solvers) it is a real constraint, and the standard mitigation is a *fixed* reduction schedule — e.g., reduce within each node in rank order, then across nodes in a fixed tree — sacrificing some parallelism for determinism. Note the modern echo: when NVIDIA added symmetric-memory kernels in NCCL 2.27, they specified that reductions "are computed using FP32 accumulators," explicitly to "improv[e] both accuracy and determinism" — the same tension, resolved by fixing the arithmetic.

**The compute term.** Unlike the pure data-movement collectives, reduce does real arithmetic: combining two n-element vectors costs about n·γ (memory-bound element-wise work). Cost models therefore carry a γ term — Thakur et al.'s formulas all do.

**Short messages: binomial tree.** Pair up ranks, combine, pair up the winners: ⌈log₂ p⌉ steps, each moving (and reducing) the full n-element partial result. Cost ≈ log₂ p·(α + n·β + n·γ). As with broadcast, the full vector crosses the critical path once per level: the bandwidth term carries the log p factor.

**Long messages: reduce-scatter + gather (Rabenseifner).** The long-message reduce in the MPICH lineage since Thakur et al. is Rabenseifner's composition, and it needs the one idea we must preview from DIST-4.7: **reduce-scatter** — reduce the data, but instead of the whole result at one rank, leave each rank holding the fully-reduced version of just its 1/p slice. Implemented by *recursive halving*: at each step, split your current block in half, send the half your partner owns, keep and reduce the other half; after log₂ p steps each rank transmits n/2 + n/4 + … = (p−1)/p·n bytes total and holds one finished slice. Then a binomial-tree **gather** collects the p slices to the root. Thakur et al. report the crossover concretely: for reduce with predefined operations, MPICH used Rabenseifner for long messages (> 2 KB) and the binomial tree for short (≤ 2 KB). The structure mirrors Van de Geijn's long-message broadcast from section 1.1 — the paper says so explicitly — and that symmetry is the duality of section 1.0 showing up in production code.

Where you meet reduce in the wild: global residual and norm computations for convergence tests (each rank reduces its local dot product; the root decides; then — tellingly — the decision usually has to be broadcast, which is the next topic's cue), distributed dot products, and inside HPL's panel factorization, where the pivot search requires small collectives among the processes factorizing the panel (the rocHPL exascale-optimization paper describes exactly this pattern).

### 1.5 All-reduce (DIST-4.4)

**Semantics.** The same combination as reduce, but every rank receives the result. This is *the* collective of data-parallel computing: synchronous SGD needs the average of all p gradient tensors on all p GPUs, every iteration; iterative solvers need global inner products and norms replicated so every rank can make the same branching decision without a follow-up broadcast.

```c
MPI_Allreduce(sendbuf, recvbuf, n, MPI_DOUBLE, MPI_SUM, comm);
```

**Naive: reduce + broadcast.** Run section 1.4, then section 1.1. Cost ≈ 2·log₂ p·α + 2·log₂ p·n·β. Correct, and quietly terrible for large n: the log p bandwidth poison is paid *twice*, and the root is a hotspot in both phases. (Gather-then-broadcast is the same trap for all-gather.)

**Short messages: recursive doubling.** One phase, ⌈log₂ p⌉ steps. At step k, rank r exchanges its current partial result (n elements covering its whole group) with rank r ⊕ 2^k, both reduce, and the covered group doubles. Every rank ends with the complete reduction. Cost ≈ log₂ p·α + log₂ p·n·β — the egress is n per step (n·log p total), which is why this shape is only for small n where α dominates.

**Long messages: reduce-scatter + all-gather.** Apply the section-1.4 insight, but replace the final gather-to-root with an *all*-gather, so everyone assembles the full result from the p reduced slices. Two constructions dominate:

- **Rabenseifner (recursive halving + recursive doubling):** cost ≈ 2·log₂ p·α + 2·(p−1)/p·n·β + (p−1)/p·n·γ. Bandwidth-optimal-ish with logarithmic steps — the MPICH-family choice for long messages.
- **Ring allreduce (ring reduce-scatter + ring all-gather):** the same two phases, both executed around a ring: cost ≈ 2·(p−1)·α + 2·(p−1)/p·n·β. Identical byte count, more steps, but three virtues: it works for any p with no power-of-two gymnastics, the data movement is a perfectly regular pipeline of n/p chunks that maps beautifully onto GPU kernels, and each rank's traffic is exactly equal. It is the default large-message algorithm in NCCL and the algorithm that made distributed training scale.

Trace it once, small enough to see whole. Four ranks, four chunks (n/4 each); at step t of reduce-scatter, rank i sends chunk (i − t) mod 4 to rank (i+1) mod 4 and adds what it receives:

| Rank 0's chunks | c0 | c1 | c2 | c3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| start | 1 contrib | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| after step 0 (recv c3 from R3) | 1 | 1 | 1 | **2** |
| after step 1 (recv c2 = {3,2} from R3) | 1 | 1 | **3** | 2 |
| after step 2 (recv c1 = {3,2,1} from R3) | 1 | **4 ✓ complete** | 3 | 2 |

Symmetrically, rank i finishes holding the *complete* chunk (i+1) mod 4. Then all-gather rolls the four complete chunks around the same ring for 3 steps, and everyone holds all four. Per-rank budget: 3·(n/4) sent in each phase, 2·(3/4)·n = 1.5n total. In general 2(p−1)/p·n, approaching 2n from below as p grows — *independent of the number of ranks*. Compare the naive reduce+broadcast at p = 1,024: its critical path is 2·10·n = 20n; the ring's is ≈ 2n. A 10× difference that came entirely from choosing the right composition.

A real number, from the source we will meet again in Section 2: Baidu's 2017 ring-allreduce write-up considers a 300M-parameter language model — 1.2 GB of FP32 gradients per GPU. Ring allreduce moves ≈ 2·(p−1)/p·1.2 GB ≈ 2.4 GB per GPU *no matter how many GPUs*, versus (p−1)·1.2 GB per GPU for a naive star of pairwise exchanges. On ~6 GB/s InfiniBand links that is ≈ 0.4 s of communication per iteration (my arithmetic from their figures) — the difference between a training run that scales and one that drowns.

**Where this is going.** NCCL exposes the full menu we have built today — Ring, Tree, NVLS (in-NVSwitch reduction), CollNet (in-network reduction on SHARP fabrics), and the newer PAT — with LL/LL128/Simple wire protocols, selected per call by an internal cost model over topology, message size, and rank count. The α/β reasoning you just did is, at its core, that cost model. And once you see all-reduce as reduce-scatter + all-gather, the newest wrinkle in large-model training stops being mysterious: fully-sharded data parallelism (FSDP/ZeRO) does gradient reduction with reduce-scatter and parameter materialization with all-gather — it is the ring allreduce taken apart at the seams and run as two independent, overlappable phases. The decomposition is the algorithm.

**Wrap-up of the five.** One table now summarizes the entire volume:

| Collective | Latency-optimal shape | Bandwidth-optimal shape | Per-rank bytes (large n) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broadcast | binomial tree (log p steps) | scatter + ring roll | ≈ 2(p−1)/p·n |
| Scatter / gather | pipelined from root | same (root-bound regardless) | (p−1)·s at the root |
| All-gather | recursive doubling | ring | (p−1)·s |
| Reduce | binomial tree | reduce-scatter + gather | ≈ 2(p−1)/p·n |
| All-reduce | recursive doubling | reduce-scatter + all-gather (ring/Rabenseifner) | 2(p−1)/p·n |

Next time (DIST-4.7) reduce-scatter finally gets its own treatment, followed by all-to-all, barrier, and the formal cost models of DIST-4.10/4.11 that put precise numbers on everything we estimated today.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 NCCL 2.30/2.31 (2026): collectives move off the SMs and into the fabric

**What happened.** NVIDIA's collective library has been shipping a rapid sequence of releases through 2026. NCCL 2.30.7 (listed June 2026) added *zero-SM collectives*: hierarchical AllGather and All-to-All implementations that run inter-node communication through an RMA CPU proxy and intra-node movement through the GPUs' copy engines — DMA hardware — instead of CUDA kernels burning streaming multiprocessors, enabled with the `NCCL_CTA_POLICY_ZERO` flag. The current 2.31.2 release extends this with a **Compute Fabric Transport (CFT)**: host and device APIs for registering window memory with CUDA logical endpoints and issuing device-side Put, Get, Red, and NVLS operations directly from kernels, on Blackwell GPUs with CUDA 13.3+. The same release enhances the PAT (Parallel Aggregated Tree) algorithm for reduce-scatter/all-gather with hierarchical kernels — NVLS within the node, PAT across nodes — and adds per-collective configuration (`ncclCollConfig_t`), letting a caller pin the algorithm, CTA/CGA sizes, and CTA policy for each individual `allreduce`/`broadcast`/`gather`/`scatter` call.

**What technically changed.** Three shifts are worth naming. First, the *execution resource* for a collective is no longer assumed to be SMs: copy engines, the CPU's proxy path, and switch-resident logic (NVLink multicast for small-message AllGather) now carry collectives that previously cost 16+ SMs per GPU — NCCL 2.27's SHARP-based paths already cut that to ≤ 6, and the zero-SM work takes it to zero, freeing the SMs for the model itself. Second, the *initiator* moved: GIN (GPU-Initiated Networking) and CFT let a device kernel issue communication itself, with strong/weak signal semantics for fencing — the collectives become composable from inside other kernels rather than being host-launched islands. Third, *selection became a public interface*: per-collective algorithm configuration is precisely the message-size crossover of Section 1 exposed as an API, motivated by community request (GitHub RFE #1916).

**Why it matters and how it connects.** Every operation in this release — AllGather, ReduceScatter, AllReduce, Broadcast, Gather, Scatter — is a topic covered today, and every algorithm name in it (Ring, Tree, NVLS, PAT) is a point on the latency-versus-bandwidth trade-space of Section 1. At 100,000-GPU scale, a collective that occupies 16 SMs per GPU for the duration of a gradient reduction is stealing a few percent of the world's most expensive FLOPs; offloading to switches and copy engines is the same old α-β optimization, now with SM occupancy as a third cost term. Watch whether the MPI implementations follow the same path — compare Open MPI 6's GPU-collectives work from Saturday's volume — and note the trajectory we flagged then in the "Every µs Matters" small-message allreduce work: the frontier has moved from *bytes on the wire* to *latency and occupancy of the on-ramp*.

### 2.2 The ring allreduce crosses into deep learning (2017)

**What happened.** On February 21, 2017, Andrew Gibiansky of Baidu's Silicon Valley AI Lab published "Bringing HPC Techniques to Deep Learning," arguing that synchronous data-parallel training was bottlenecked not by compute but by gradient communication, and that the fix was a textbook HPC collective: the bandwidth-optimal ring allreduce. Baidu released a C library and a TensorFlow patch; within a year, Uber's Horovod (Sergeev and Del Balle, arXiv:1802.05799) generalized the approach, swapping the hand-rolled ring for NCCL 2, which had just gained multi-node support.

**What technically changed.** Before: the common pattern gathered gradients to a parameter server (or reduced them through a star), so per-GPU traffic grew linearly with p and the center node saturated. After: every GPU contributes to a ring reduce-scatter and ring all-gather — the exact algorithm traced in Section 1.5 — sending 2(p−1)/p·n bytes each, bounded by the slowest link in the ring and independent of p, a property Patarasuk and Yuan proved bandwidth-optimal for clusters in 2009. The 300M-parameter / 1.2 GB / ≈2.4 GB-per-GPU arithmetic in Section 1.5 is from that post. Horovod's contribution was to show the same trick worked unchanged across frameworks, driven by MPI launch and NCCL kernels.

**Why it matters and how it connects.** This is the clearest case study in modern HPC history of a collective-communication algorithm *changing industries*: an algorithm from the MPI collective literature (Rabenseifner-lineage reduce-scatter + all-gather, bandwidth-optimized by Patarasuk and Yuan in 2009) became the load-bearing primitive of large-scale AI training, and the reason a vendor library like NCCL — with the ring as its historical default — receives the level of engineering attention visible in Development 2.1. For you, the practical takeaway is the connection: gradient averaging is `MPI_SUM` over a big FP32/BF16 tensor; the tensor-chunking that NCCL does internally is the n/p slicing you traced by hand; and when large-model training moved to reduce-scatter + all-gather under sharded data parallelism, it did so by pulling apart exactly the composition you learned today. Understanding *why* the ring is bandwidth-optimal is understanding the communication economics of every large training run currently in existence.

1. **R. Thakur, R. Rabenseifner, and W. Gropp**, "Optimization of Collective Communication Operations in MPICH," *Int'l Journal of High Performance Computing Applications* 19(1):49–66, 2005 — the size-switched algorithm menu, binomial-vs-Rabenseifner crossovers, and the >2 KB / ≤2 KB reduce threshold. https://web.cels.anl.gov/~thakur/papers/ijhpca-coll.pdf
2. **E. Chan, M. Heimlich, A. Purkayastha, and R. van de Geijn**, "Collective Communication: Theory, Practice, and Experience," *Concurrency and Computation: Practice and Experience* 19(13):1749–1783, 2007 (DOI 10.1002/cpe.1206) — the redistribution/consolidation taxonomy and the duality transformation. https://www.cs.utexas.edu/ftp/techreports/tr06-44.pdf
3. **P. Patarasuk and X. Yuan**, "Bandwidth optimal all-reduce algorithms for clusters of workstations," *Journal of Parallel and Distributed Computing* 69(2):117–124, 2009 — bandwidth-optimality of the ring allreduce.
4. **A. Gibiansky (Baidu Research)**, "Bringing HPC Techniques to Deep Learning," Feb 21, 2017 — the ring-allreduce-for-training argument and the 1.2 GB / 2.4 GB worked example. https://andrew.gibiansky.com/blog/machine-learning/baidu-allreduce/
5. **A. Sergeev and M. Del Balle**, "Horovod: fast and easy distributed deep learning in TensorFlow," arXiv:1802.05799 — the adoption trajectory and NCCL 2 multi-node ring. https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05799
6. **Netlib HPL**, "Algorithm" — the six `BCASTs` topologies and the Long (scatter + roll) broadcast description. https://netlib.sandia.gov/benchmark/hpl/algorithm.html (tuning guidance for `BCASTs` also reflects typical cluster documentation, e.g., Bright Computing's HPL notes)
7. **MPI Forum**, *MPI: A Message-Passing Interface Standard, Version 5.0* (June 2025) — collective semantics; nonblocking collectives arrived in MPI-3.0, persistent collectives and large-count interfaces in MPI-4.0. https://www.mpi-forum.org/docs/mpi-5.0/mpi50-report/mpi50-report.htm
8. **NVIDIA NCCL**, releases and release notes 2.27–2.31.2 — symmetric-memory kernels and FP32-accumulator determinism (2.27); zero-SM collectives and GIN (2.30.7); CFT, per-collective `ncclCollConfig_t`, and hierarchical PAT (2.31.2). https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl/releases and https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/release-notes/rel_2-31-2.html
9. **arXiv:2304.10397**, "Optimizing High-Performance Linpack for Exascale Accelerated Architectures" — panel factorization's small collectives (pivot search) in rocHPL. https://arxiv.org/html/2304.10397v1

### Further Reading

- **Thakur, Rabenseifner & Gropp (2005)** — research paper, deep dive; the single best companion to this module (60–90 min).
- **Chan et al. (2007)** — theory + a full MPI-compatible implementation; read for the duality proofs and the cost tables (long read).
- **Gibiansky (2017) blog post** — 20 minutes; the clearest illustrated walkthrough of ring allreduce chunk flow ever written.
- **Netlib HPL algorithm page** — 10 minutes; maps today's broadcast analysis directly onto the `BCASTs` parameter you tune in practice.
- **NCCL release notes (2.30.7, 2.31.2)** — 15 minutes of scanning; the current frontier of the exact algorithms learned today.
