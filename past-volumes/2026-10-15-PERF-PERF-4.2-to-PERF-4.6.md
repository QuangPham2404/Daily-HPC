# 0. Concise Summary

Module 4 continues from last Thursday's theoretical peak (PERF-4.1) with the three questions that follow immediately once you have a denominator: **what can real, well-tuned code actually reach** (PERF-4.2, sustained peak)? **how close is *your* code to that** (PERF-4.3, application efficiency)? and **which resource is stopping it** (PERF-4.4–4.6, the first three of the five limit classes: compute, memory bandwidth, latency — communication and synchronization limits complete the set next time)?

The day's central technical connection is that these are not five independent concepts but one **ladder of references**. A theoretical peak is what silicon could do if every slot were full every cycle; a sustained peak is what the best-known kernel (a vendor-optimized DGEMM, a STREAM Triad, a tuned HPL) actually achieves on that silicon — typically 70–95% of theoretical; application efficiency then locates *your* code somewhere between 0 and the sustained ceiling, and the remaining gap decomposes into limits. The distinctions matter because the fixes differ: a compute-limited kernel needs fewer or faster arithmetic operations (or a faster engine for the same math); a bandwidth-limited kernel needs to move fewer bytes (reuse, fusion, layout); a latency-limited kernel needs more concurrency (deeper pipelines, batching, prefetch) — applying the wrong fix does nothing, which is precisely why the curriculum classifies limits *before* Module 26 optimizes them.

The concrete arithmetic anchors the whole issue. On an NVIDIA H100 SXM (67 TF/s FP64 Tensor Core, 3.35 TB/s HBM3), DGEMM sustains on the order of 60 TF/s — about 90% of peak — implying a *machine balance* of roughly 18 FP64 FLOPs per byte of DRAM traffic: dense linear algebra is compute-bound by design. STREAM Triad on the same device reads + writes 24 bytes per 2 FLOPs and runs at HBM speed; a SpMV reads 12 bytes per 2 FLOPs and is bandwidth-bound by nature. El Capitan's HPL at 1.809 EF/s against a 2.883 EF/s Rpeak is **62.7% application efficiency against theory but ≈96% against what GEMM-class kernels sustain** — the two ratios tell completely different stories about where optimization headroom lives.

Two developments connect this to the real world. The **November 2025 TOP500/Green500 round** (JUPITER Booster reaching exactly 1.000 EF/s, KAIROS taking Green500 #1 at 73.28 GF/W) is a public, machine-scale exercise in efficiency ratios — including the cautionary tale that a system can be #1 in Flop/s per Watt while running HPL at only ~50% of its theoretical peak: energy efficiency and compute efficiency are different fractions with different denominators. And the **Empirical Roofline Toolkit's approach to machine characterization** — measuring each cache level's bandwidth ceiling with micro-kernels rather than trusting datasheets — is sustained peak generalized to the whole memory hierarchy, and it previews how Module 5's Roofline turns the bandwidth roof into a two-axis diagnostic.

Why this matters: nearly every performance number you will ever publish, compare, or be judged by — a thesis benchmark, a TOP500 submission, a "we made it 2× faster" claim — is a ratio whose numerator you measured and whose denominator you chose. Today is about choosing the denominator honestly and reading what the ratio tells you.

# 1. Learn the Concepts

## 1.1 Sustained peak (PERF-4.2)

The theoretical peak of PERF-4.1 is an *invariant of the hardware spec sheet*: multiply slots by clock, assume every slot full. The sustained peak is an *empirical property of the software–hardware system*: the throughput that a highly optimized, representative kernel actually achieves on the machine. It is sometimes written R* or called "achievable peak," and its defining feature is that it is **kernel-class-specific**. Concretely:

| Kernel class | What it measures | Typical % of theoretical | Why the gap exists |
|---|---|---|---|
| DGEMM (vendor BLAS) | Matrix-multiply sustained compute | ~85–95% | Tiling, pipeline fill/drain, tail effects, power |
| STREAM Triad | Sustained DRAM bandwidth | ~85–95% | Refresh, protocol, read/write turnaround |
| HPL (whole machine) | Full-system dense solve | ~60–80% | Everything above, plus communication and panel phases |
| HPCG (whole machine) | Sparse/irregular capability | ~1–5% | Latency, bandwidth, irregularity — by design |

The percentages are order-of-magnitude guides, not laws; the *mechanism* is what to internalize. Even a perfect GEMM cannot hit 100% of theoretical peak because of **pipeline fill and drain** (the first wave of loads and the last wave of stores do math-free work), **tail quantization** (an M×N×K problem never divides perfectly into tile shapes), **power and thermal response** (dense FP64 at full issue rate draws maximal current, and clocks bend), and **intrinsic instruction-mix overhead** (address arithmetic, loop control, and data movement instructions share issue slots with the FLOPs they serve). STREAM cannot hit 100% of datasheet bandwidth because DRAM has refresh cycles, bus turnaround penalties between reads and writes, and protocol overhead — and because the "peak" figure vendors quote is usually the raw pin rate of the memory interface, not the payload rate the memory controller can sustain.

This is exactly the distinction John McCalpin built the STREAM benchmark to expose. His 1995 survey, *Memory Bandwidth and Machine Balance in Current High Performance Computers*, opens with the observation that **vendors very seldom publish enough hardware detail to estimate sustainable memory bandwidth — because the sustainable number is generally quite poor relative to the quoted one** — so the community had to measure it themselves. STREAM's four kernels (Copy, Scale, Add, Triad) are deliberately trivial arithmetically: `a(i) = b(i) + scalar*c(i)` performs 2 FLOPs and moves 24 bytes (read 8+8, write 8) per iteration, so Triad's *compute* content is negligible and its execution rate is a direct readout of sustainable bandwidth. The benchmark's longevity — three decades of continuously updated results, now hosted at the University of Virginia — comes from measuring the one quantity that (a) dominates a huge class of real codes and (b) vendors systematically overquote.

**Why the sustained peak deserves "peak" in its name.** It is not merely "a benchmark result." It functions as the *effective ceiling for its kernel class*: no ordinary code in that class should expect to beat a hand-tuned library kernel doing the same operation, so sustained-peak numbers become the honest denominators for efficiency claims. When AMD or NVIDIA publishes a DGEMM efficiency plot, the denominator is usually their own best-achieved DGEMM, not the spec-sheet peak. The same logic applies at machine scale: El Capitan's 1.809 EF/s HPL result is a *single application's* number; what the machine could sustain on GEMM-class work is higher, and what it sustains on HPCG-class work (17.41 PF/s, under 1% of Rpeak) is dramatically lower.

**A practical caution: sustained peak drifts with software.** A machine's DGEMM ceiling rises when the vendor library improves, without any hardware change. This is one of the reasons benchmark records (PERF-3.7) must capture library versions: "90% of sustained peak" in 2024 and "90% of sustained peak" in 2026 may be different absolute performances, because the *denominator moved*. When LLNL retuned El Capitan's HPL from 1.742 to 1.809 EF/s between June and November 2025 — same hardware, +2.7% more APUs, ~1.1% genuine tuning, as last volume's decomposition showed — part of that tuning gain is exactly this effect: better library kernels lift the sustained ceiling the application climbs toward.

## 1.2 Application efficiency (PERF-4.3)

Application efficiency is the ratio that puts your measurement in context:

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{measured performance}}{\text{reference peak}} \quad\text{where the reference is either theoretical or sustained.}$$

The formula is trivial; the discipline is in the denominator, and there are three honest choices:

1. **Against theoretical peak** — answers "how much of the machine's physical capability does this use?" El Capitan HPL: 1.809/2.883 = **62.7%**. This is the TOP500-style ratio, the right one for machine-vs-machine comparison and for asking "is the hardware being wasted?"
2. **Against sustained peak** — answers "how close is my code to what the best code of this class achieves?" If well-tuned GEMM sustains ~90% of theoretical on MI300A-class silicon, then an application whose hot phase is a DGEMM running at 60 TF/s per device is at ~96% of the *sustainable* rate, and its remaining headroom is a few percent — further optimization of that phase is nearly exhausted. The same absolute number is 90% of one denominator and 96% of the other; neither is wrong, but they imply opposite next actions.
3. **Against a baseline** (PERF-3.8) — answers "did my change help?" This is the only denominator under your direct experimental control, and the only one that certifies *your* specific contribution.

Note what efficiency is **not**: it is not utilization (PERF-1.6 — fraction of *time* resources are busy; a kernel can be 100% memory-busy and still be moving bytes inefficiently), and it is not speedup (PERF-6.1 — a ratio against a *baseline*, not against a *limit*). The three get conflated constantly in vendor material; keeping them separate is half of today's lesson.

**The chain of ratios at machine scale.** Read El Capitan's published numbers as one application's position on the ladder:

| Ratio | Value | Denominator | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rmax/Rpeak (HPL) | 1.809/2.883 = 62.7% | Theoretical FP64 vector | Machine-scale HPL efficiency; TOP500 convention |
| Rmax/(0.9 × Rpeak) | ≈ 70% | Assumed GEMM-sustainable | Headroom *within* GEMM-class work |
| HPL-MxP / FP16 theory | 16.7/44.1 = 37.9% | Theoretical FP16 matrix | Mixed-precision run against its own precision's peak (computed last volume) |
| HPCG/Rpeak | 17.41 PF/2.883 EF = 0.6% | Theoretical FP64 vector | The "irregular workload" collapse — different kernel class, different world |

Each row is a true statement about the same machine on the same day. The 0.6% HPCG figure is the most instructive: it is *not* evidence of a badly tuned HPCG — it is evidence that the machine's capability profile (huge dense compute relative to bandwidth and latency tolerance) is mismatched to sparse, latency-dominated work. Efficiency ratios measure the **fit between a machine's design point and a workload's demand profile** as much as they measure tuning quality. This is why TOP500 publishes HPL, HPCG, and HPL-MxP side by side: one machine, several rulers, and the *pattern across rulers* is the real characterization. (LineShine, the June 2026 #1, inverts the pattern: 80.3% HPL efficiency — CPU vector pipes stay full on dense work — but a modest 3.6× HPL-MxP speedup over its HPL, because a CPU-only design has no low-precision matrix engines to exploit. The ratio *pattern* reveals the architecture.)

**Efficiency denominators must state their precision.** An HPL-MxP score of 16.7 EF/s is neither 579% of the FP64 peak (absurd — different precision, different hardware path) nor honestly comparable to the HPL number without labeling. The rule from last volume generalizes: every efficiency ratio is a tuple **(value, denominator, precision, measurement context)**, and only the tuple is a fact.

## 1.3 The three limits, and why classify before optimizing (PERF-4.4, 4.5, 4.6)

PERF-4.4 through PERF-4.8 enumerate five limit classes; today covers the first three. The premise: at any moment, a kernel's performance is bounded by whichever resource saturates first, and *identifying that resource is the diagnosis step of performance engineering* — Module 26's optimization workflow explicitly routes "classify the bottleneck" before "optimize." The five classes and their signatures:

| Limit | Saturating resource | Signature in measurement | What helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Compute** (4.4) | FP/vector/tensor pipelines | High issue-slot/pipe utilization; near sustained-peak FLOP/s | Fewer ops, better instruction mix, faster engine |
| **Memory bandwidth** (4.5) | DRAM/HBM link | Near-peak bytes/s; low compute utilization | Fewer bytes: reuse, fusion, layout, precision |
| **Latency** (4.6) | Dependency chains, startup costs | Low utilization of *everything*; time dominated by waits | More concurrency: batch, pipeline, prefetch |
| Communication (4.7) | Network, PCIe, NVLink | Wait time correlated with transfers | Overlap, fewer messages, faster fabric (next volume) |
| Synchronization (4.8) | Barriers, lockstep phases | Idle time at join points | Balance load, reduce sync frequency (next volume) |

The three limits today are worth distinguishing carefully because **bandwidth-bound and latency-bound are different diseases with different cures**, and both are routinely misdiagnosed as "the GPU/CPU is slow."

### Compute limit (PERF-4.4)

A kernel is compute-limited when arithmetic execution capacity is the binding constraint: the FP pipes (or tensor/matrix cores) are saturated and performance scales with issue rate. The prototype is DGEMM. Consider the arithmetic-intensity arithmetic on modern hardware, using an H100 SXM as the reference device (NVIDIA datasheet: 67 TF/s FP64 Tensor Core, 3.35 TB/s HBM3):

- A square DGEMM, $C = A \cdot B$ with $n \times n$ matrices, performs $2n^3$ FLOPs while reading and writing $O(n^2)$ elements. Per FLOP, the bytes moved vanish as $n$ grows — at $n = 8192$, roughly $2n^3 = 1.1\times10^{12}$ FLOPs against $\sim3\times 10^8$ bytes of unavoidable traffic, an arithmetic intensity of thousands of FLOPs per byte. Even after accounting for the cache-line granularity of *actual* loads and the fact that each output element's traffic is amortized across $n$ multiply-adds, the kernel's demand on DRAM is tiny relative to its demand on the FP64 units.
- So DGEMM runs at the *compute* roof: vendor-tuned FP64 GEMM on H100 sustains on the order of 60+ TF/s, ~90% of the 67 TF/s peak. The last 10% is lost to pipeline fill/drain, tile tails, and the address arithmetic that competes for issue slots.

The compute-limit test is not "is the kernel doing lots of math?" — it is "does performance improve when we give it *more* arithmetic capability at unchanged bandwidth, and fail to improve when we give it more bandwidth?" The GEMM-vs-GEMM comparison across generations with equal memory bandwidth but different FP64 rates behaves this way, which is why the compute-limit classification is safe for dense linear algebra and essentially never correct for sparse kernels.

**Machine balance** is the quantity that decides in advance which limit a kernel class will hit. Define it as peak FLOP/s ÷ peak bytes/s (FLOPs per byte, sometimes expressed per 8-byte word):

| Device (FP64) | Peak compute | Peak bandwidth | Machine balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| H100 SXM (Tensor Core FP64) | 67 TF/s | 3.35 TB/s | ~20 FLOP/byte |
| MI300A APU | 61.3 TF/s (vector) | 5.3 TB/s | ~11.6 FLOP/byte |
| El Capitan (whole machine) | 2.883 EF/s | 8.7 TB/s aggregate | — (nodes linked by Slingshot-11) |

McCalpin was already tabulating exactly this ratio — "the number of FP operations that can be performed during the time for an average memory access" — in 1995, warning that it was growing rapidly. The modern consequence: **a kernel whose arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved) is below the machine balance cannot escape the bandwidth limit**, and no amount of compute-side tuning changes that. H100's ~20 FLOP/byte balance means even a kernel doing 10 FLOPs per 8-byte word read (intensity 1.25 FLOP/byte) is bandwidth-bound by a factor of 16. The gap has only widened as compute has scaled faster than bandwidth for three decades.

### Memory bandwidth limit (PERF-4.5)

A kernel is memory-bandwidth-limited when the DRAM/HBM link saturates: every additional FLOP must wait for data that cannot arrive faster. The prototypes are STREAM itself and every sparse kernel:

- **STREAM Triad**: 2 FLOPs per 24 bytes → intensity 0.083 FLOP/byte. On H100: even perfect execution yields $3.35\,\text{TB/s} \times 0.083 = 278$ GF/s — 0.4% of the FP64 peak. The kernel is *incapable* of being compute-bound.
- **SpMV** ($y = A \cdot x$, CSR): per nonzero, read 8 bytes of value + 4 bytes of column index (+ $x$ vector traffic, amortized by cache) and write amortized; ≈ 2 FLOPs per ~12 bytes → intensity ≈ 0.17 FLOP/byte. Bound at $3.35 \times 0.17 \approx 560$ GF/s — again a small fraction of compute peak. This is why SpMV on GPUs runs at a few percent of FP64 peak *regardless of tuning quality*: the ceiling is the wire, not the ALU.

The bandwidth-limit signature in measurements: bytes/s near the STREAM-sustainable rate, arithmetic units mostly idle, and — the diagnostic gold — **performance invariant to compute-side changes** (different FMA arrangement, unrolling) but responsive to byte-side changes (blocking that increases reuse, data layout that improves coalescing, precision reduction that shrinks operands). When you observe that pattern, you have classified the limit, and Module 12's remedies (tiling, fusion, layout) are the correct lever set.

Note the subtlety STREAM teaches: *sustainable* bandwidth is itself kernel- and access-pattern-dependent. Read-mostly streams, write-dominated streams, strided accesses, and read-modify-write patterns each sustain different fractions of the pin rate, and NUMA or multi-channel interleaving can change the picture entirely. This is why the sustained-peak concept of §1.1 generalizes: there is no single "the bandwidth" of a machine — there is a family of sustainable rates, and honest efficiency claims against bandwidth state which access pattern was measured. McCalpin's own results tables separate standard vs. tuned runs precisely because the achievable rate depends on how well the stream of accesses matches the memory system's preferences (unit stride engages prefetching; scattered accesses defeat it).

### Latency limit (PERF-4.6)

The third limit is the subtlest: a kernel is latency-limited when neither throughput resource saturates, and time is consumed by *waiting for dependent operations to complete or operations to start*. The distinguishing symptom: **low utilization of both compute and bandwidth**. The machine is not full and the wire is not full — the kernel is simply not keeping enough work in flight to cover the latency of its own dependency chains.

The canonical model is Little's Law applied to memory: to sustain $B$ bytes/s against a memory latency of $L$ seconds, you need

$$\text{bytes in flight} = B \times L,$$

organized as $\lceil B \times L / \text{(bytes per request)} \rceil$ independent requests. Concrete numbers make the scale vivid. Against HBM latency on the order of ~600 ns (HBM3-class devices; latency has been nearly flat across generations while bandwidth doubled and doubled again) at 3.35 TB/s:

$$3.35\times10^{12}\,\text{B/s} \times 600\times10^{-9}\,\text{s} \approx 2\,\text{MB of requests in flight}.$$

Two megabytes of independent loads outstanding — that is what it takes to *saturate* an H100's HBM through latency-hiding alone. A single thread issuing loads sequentially and consuming each result before issuing the next keeps ~one request in flight and achieves $\text{bandwidth} \approx \text{request size}/L$ — a few hundred bytes per ~600 ns — i.e., megabytes per second instead of terabytes per second, six orders of magnitude below the wire. Latency-boundedness is thus a *concurrency deficit*, and its cures are all concurrency cures: deeper pipelines (each thread issues multiple independent loads), more threads (each with its own chain), wider vectors (more bytes per request), prefetching (issue the load before the dependency needs it), and batching/persistent kernels for the startup-cost variant (a kernel launch, an MPI round-trip, or a network message has a fixed latency floor that small operations cannot amortize — PERF-13's launch-bound workloads are the degenerate case).

The same reasoning explains why latency dominates small-message communication (PERF-17.2's territory) and why HPCG is so brutal: each sparse-matrix row has a pointer-chase dependency, and no quantity of raw bandwidth or raw FLOP/s fixes a pointer chase — only more independent rows in flight do, and irregular sparsity limits how many you can find. A machine with 2.9 EF/s of dense compute and HPCG at 0.6% of that is a machine whose *latency tolerance* is the scarce resource for sparse work.

### Putting the three together: the bound

The three limits combine into a single upper bound — the intuition behind the Roofline model that Module 5 formalizes:

$$\text{Performance} \;\le\; \min\!\Big(\underbrace{P_{\text{peak}}}_{\text{compute}},\;\; \underbrace{B \cdot I}_{\text{bandwidth}},\;\; \underbrace{f(\text{concurrency})}_{\text{latency}}\Big),$$

where $I$ is arithmetic intensity (FLOP/byte) and the latency term resists a closed form — it depends on the dependency structure of the code, which is exactly why Roofline (compute vs. bandwidth, two axes) is an *insightful first-order model* rather than a complete one, and why PERF-5.8 lists latency among its acknowledged limitations. For today, the operational summary is:

1. Compute the kernel's arithmetic intensity from its algorithm (Module 5's PERF-5.6 does this carefully).
2. Compare against machine balance: below it → bandwidth-bound; far above it → compute-bound candidate.
3. Check measured utilization of both resources: if *both* are low, suspect latency/dependency limits before anything else.
4. Only then choose the remedy class — because the remedy classes do not overlap.

A final connection to HPL practice: the reason HPL has been *the* machine-classifying benchmark for three decades is that it sits near the compute limit by construction — blocked GEMM raises arithmetic intensity above machine balance on every modern architecture — so Rmax/Rpeak measures the machine's dense-compute capability, while HPCG's 1–5% exposes everything else. The two benchmarks together are a crude two-point probe of a machine's limit profile; Modules 5 (Roofline) and 24 (HPL phase analysis) sharpen the probe into a diagnostic instrument.

## 1.4 Connections and what's next

Today's five topics are the statics of performance limits: each limit is defined, each has a measurement signature, and the ladder theoretical → sustained → application efficiency gives every measurement a defensible denominator. Two threads continue from here:

- **PERF-4.7 and 4.8** (communication and synchronization limits) extend the classification to distributed codes, where the "resource" saturating can be a network link or a barrier rather than a local pipe — and where exposed vs. overlapped communication (PERF-17.8) becomes the key distinction.
- **Module 5 (Roofline)** promotes today's min-bound into a two-axis model with predictive power: plot performance vs. arithmetic intensity, draw the compute and bandwidth roofs, and a kernel's position tells you its limit *and* its headroom in one glance. The ridge point — where the two roofs meet, at exactly the machine balance computed in §1.3 — is the single number that summarizes a machine's balance for HPC purposes. Williams, Waterman, and Patterson's original 2009 CACM paper found the ridge point "proved to be a better predictor of performance than clock rate or peak performance" across four architectures — a direct, published validation that today's balance arithmetic matters more than headline TFLOP/s.

## 2.1 The November 2025 TOP500/Green500 round: efficiency ratios at machine scale

**What happened.** At SC25 (November 17, 2025), the 66th TOP500 list confirmed El Capitan at No. 1 with 1.809 EF/s HPL (a retuned improvement over its 1.742 EF/s debut), Frontier No. 2 at 1.353 EF/s, Aurora No. 3 at 1.012 EF/s, and **JUPITER Booster** — Eviden's BullSequana XH3000 with NVIDIA Grace Hopper GH200 superchips at Jülich Supercomputing Centre — entering the exascale club at exactly **1.000 EF/s, the fourth exascale system and the first in Europe**. In the Green500, three GH200-based BullSequana systems swept the podium: KAIROS (CALMIP, Toulouse) at **73.28 GF/W**, ROMEO-2025 at 70.9 GF/W, and Levante GPU extension at 69.4 GF/W, with JUPITER rated the most energy-efficient exascale-class system at ~63 GF/W.

**What technically changed or was demonstrated.** JUPITER's round-number 1.000 EF/s is itself a lesson in Rmax/Rpeak bookkeeping: the Booster's four-quad-rail NDR200 InfiniBand GH200 partition was measured at almost exactly the point where its installed hardware crosses the exaflop line against its theoretical peak — a deliberate, symbolic measurement rather than a maximum-effort HPL campaign (the system was newly fully installed). More technically interesting is the efficiency pattern: JUPITER achieves ~63 GF/W while El Capitan achieves 60.94 GF/W — near-parity between a GPU-arm system and an APU system — but their HPL *compute* efficiencies differ (El Capitan 62.7% of Rpeak; JUPITER's 1.000 EF/s is roughly half of its Rpeak in its full Booster configuration). The Green500's GF/W metric divides HPL performance by whole-system power, so it rewards exactly what today's curriculum discusses: achieving high *sustained* rates on the work you actually run, per unit energy, regardless of whether the theoretical peak is exploited.

**Why it matters and the connection.** This is application efficiency (PERF-4.3) and sustained peak (PERF-4.2) as public, machine-scale data. The same machine's HPL efficiency, HPL-MxP speedup, HPCG rate, and GF/W tell four different stories because each has a different denominator — and the November 2025 round shows a system (JUPITER) being celebrated primarily for its *energy* efficiency ratio while its compute-efficiency ratio against its own Rpeak is modest. Both facts are true simultaneously, and an engineer reading the press releases needs today's distinctions to avoid concluding "JUPITER is more efficient than El Capitan" full stop — more efficient *per watt on HPL*, at a different scale, with a different architecture; not more efficient *against its own theoretical peak*. **What to watch next:** the June 2026 list (already published as this volume goes out) is the natural follow-up experiment — JUPITER's second measurement and El Capitan's response to LineShine's debut at 2.198 EF/s will show how much of the first-round efficiency picture was tuning-stage artifacts versus architecture.

## 2.2 STREAM at three decades: the sustained-bandwidth benchmark that refuses to die

**What happened.** Nothing new — which is the point. John McCalpin's STREAM benchmark, first released from the University of Delaware in 1991 and maintained as a continually updated technical report (1991–2007) now hosted by the University of Virginia, remains *the* standard reference for sustainable memory bandwidth in HPC, with vendor results tables maintained to the present. Every "GB/s of DRAM bandwidth" claim an HPC engineer trusts — or distrusts — traces to STREAM-style measurement.

**What it demonstrates technically.** STREAM's design is today's curriculum in miniature. Its kernels are chosen so the arithmetic is trivial (Triad: 2 FLOPs, 24 bytes), which makes the benchmark a *pure probe of the bandwidth limit* — performance is bound by $B \times I$ with $I = 0.083$, so far below machine balance that the compute roof is irrelevant. Its insistence on "sustainable" (long vectors, sizes chosen to exceed cache, repeated trials) is the sustained-peak discipline: the number reported is what the memory system delivers in steady state, not a burst rate. And its 1995 companion paper introduced *machine balance* — the FLOP-to-bandwidth ratio tabulated per machine — which is precisely the quantity that determines whether a given kernel class can ever escape the bandwidth roof (§1.3). McCalpin's core 1995 finding, that the balance ratio was growing rapidly with significant implications for algorithm design, has proven monotonic for three decades: today's H100 at ~20 FP64 FLOP/byte is a more extreme imbalance than anything in his original tables.

**Why it matters and the connection.** STREAM is the canonical sustained peak for the bandwidth axis, exactly as DGEMM is for the compute axis — and it explains why "my kernel achieves only 3% of peak" is often not a bug report but a physics report. It is also the direct ancestor of the Empirical Roofline Toolkit (see Further Reading): when the Roofline model needed per-level bandwidth ceilings (L1, L2, HBM), the community generalized STREAM's measure-one-access-pattern approach into micro-kernel sweeps over the whole hierarchy. **What to watch next:** hierarchical, empirically measured ceilings are now standard in vendor profilers (Intel Advisor's Roofline feature, NVIDIA Nsight Compute's per-level byte metrics) — the STREAM philosophy absorbed into the tools you use daily.

## 2.3 El Capitan's mixed rulers: one machine, several peaks, 62.7% or ~96% depending on the question

**What happened.** Also a standing fact rather than an event: LLNL's El Capitan, No. 1 from November 2024 through November 2025 (No. 2 as of June 2026), publishes performance numbers that let any reader compute efficiency against multiple denominators — and the spread is the lesson. HPL 1.809 EF/s against 2.883 EF/s Rpeak (62.7%); HPL against a GEMM-sustainable ceiling (roughly 96% if ~90% of vector-theoretical is sustainable on MI300A — plausible but not independently published per-device at full scale, so treat as estimate); HPL-MxP 16.7 EF/s against the machine's FP16 theoretical peak 44.1 EF/s (~38%); HPCG 17.41 PF/s against the same FP64 Rpeak (0.6%).

**What it demonstrates technically.** The ~96% figure is the interesting one because it is the *sustained-peak denominator in action*: if the best DGEMM-class kernels sustain ~90% of vector theoretical on this silicon, then HPL's GEMM-dominated phases are essentially at the ceiling for their kernel class, and the remaining 37 points of theoretical-peak gap are dominated by the *non-GEMM* parts of the algorithm — panel factorization, communication, load-balance tails — not by faster GEMM. That redirects optimization effort: you do not chase "make GEMM faster" (exhausted); you chase the structural phases (Module 24's territory). Meanwhile HPCG's 0.6% on the same machine quantifies the *kernel-class dependence* of everything: the machine's balance point and latency tolerance make sparse work fundamentally bandwidth/latency-limited, and no tuning crosses that line.

**Why it matters and the connection.** This is the exact reasoning chain an HPC engineer runs on their own code at small scale: measure → compute efficiency against theoretical → compute efficiency against sustained → attribute the gap to limit classes → optimize the class that actually binds. El Capitan's public numbers let you rehearse the chain on the world's fastest-class hardware. **What to watch next:** the HPL-MxP spread across architectures (El Capitan 9.2× over its HPL, Aurora 11.5×, Frontier 8.4×, JUPITER 6.3×, CHIE-4 24.4×) is a live dataset in *precision-path efficiency* — how much of each machine's low-precision hardware the mixed-precision benchmark actually recruits — and it feeds directly into Module 24's HPL-MxP analysis topics.

### Further Reading

- **McCalpin's 1995 machine-balance paper (§Citation 5) — Foundation, ~20 min.** The historical root of everything in §1.3; short, and still the clearest statement of why FLOP/s alone stopped being a meaningful machine metric.
- **Williams, Waterman & Patterson, CACM 2009 Roofline paper (§Citation 10) — Foundation, ~45 min.** The bridge from today's limits to Module 5. Read §"Attainable performance" and the ridge-point discussion; the four-architecture comparison shows balance predicting outcomes where peak FLOP/s fails.
- **STREAM benchmark home page (§Citation 6) — Reference, 10 min.** Browse the results tables for any architecture you use; compare "peak" vs. STREAM-sustained bandwidth and internalize the gap.
- **Charlene Yang, arXiv:2009.02449 (§Citation 11) — Technical, ~40 min.** How hierarchical (per-cache-level) Roofline data is actually collected with tools you have access to; the natural next step after Module 5 formalizes the model.
- **HPL-MxP results page (§Citation 4) — Data, 10 min.** The speedup-over-HPL column across architectures is a compact dataset in precision-path efficiency; try explaining each row's ratio using today's machine-balance reasoning before Module 24 arrives.
