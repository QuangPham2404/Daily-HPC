## 0. Concise Summary

Last Sunday's SYS volume ended at the node and fabric level: what a compute node contains, why login, service, and compute nodes are separated, and which networks tie a cluster together. Today we climb one level up and ask how thousands of those nodes become **one machine** — and how you can tell, from the outside, what kind of machine you are looking at.

Five concepts carry the issue:

1. **The management plane (SYS-1.6).** A 10,000-node system needs a parallel, lower-bandwidth "computer for the computer": head nodes that provision OS images, authenticate users, collect telemetry, and run the scheduler. Its most interesting member is the **baseboard management controller (BMC)** on every node — a tiny out-of-band computer that can power-cycle a crashed machine without touching its OS, today via the DMTF **Redfish** RESTful API (spec DSP0266, current version 1.24.0, May 2026).
2. **System topology (SYS-1.7).** A supercomputer is several hierarchies at once — compute topology (how switches and nodes are wired), storage topology, and management topology — and performance depends on co-locating ranks along the right one. We dissect the **dragonfly** group structure of Frontier's Slingshot network as a concrete specimen.
3. **Capability versus capacity (SYS-1.8).** The same 30-megawatt machine is either running one job that needs all of it (capability: time-to-solution for the biggest solvable problem) or thousands of smaller jobs (capacity: throughput). Centers must do both, and the tension shapes scheduling, queueing, and even failure mathematics.
4. **Building blocks (SYS-2.1).** Cabinets, nodes, switches, storage tiers, cooling loops, and power distribution — the physical anatomy, with Frontier's published numbers (74 compute cabinets, ~29 MW, water-cooled DIMMs and NICs, 37 PB of node-local NVMe) as the worked example.
5. **Homogeneous versus heterogeneous (SYS-2.2).** The central architectural fork: CPU-only designs (massive core counts, uniform programming, poor low-precision throughput) versus accelerator-based designs (huge FLOP density and efficiency, but added data-movement and programming complexity) — and a new third form, **system-level heterogeneity**, where one supercomputer is assembled from purpose-built partitions.

The developments section is unusually well matched to the curriculum. The **June 2026 TOP500** (announced at ISC in Hamburg) put a CPU-only system, China's LineShine, at No. 1 with 2.198 exaflop/s from 13.79 million cores — the first system above 2 EF/s on CPUs alone — while the four GPU-accelerated exascale systems behind it each use ~5–11 million cores. That single list is a controlled experiment in the homogeneous-versus-heterogeneous trade-off, and its HPL-MxP column (LineShine 3.6× versus El Capitan 9.2× mixed-precision speedup) shows mechanically what tensor hardware buys. Meanwhile, **Los Alamos installed the first NVIDIA Vera CPU systems** (Oct 1, 2026) in its Darwin testbed as the CPU half of the forthcoming Mission/Vision/Veritas systems — and Mission, built from three purpose-built partitions, is heterogeneity elevated from the node to the whole machine. Finally, **JUPITER** remains No. 5 at exactly 1 EF/s while being the most energy-efficient exascale system — a reminder that building blocks (warm-water direct liquid cooling, waste-heat reuse) are now first-class design constraints, not afterthoughts.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 The management plane: a computer for the computer (SYS-1.6)

**Problem.** Last week we established why user-facing work is pushed to login nodes and heavy computation to compute nodes. But who installs the operating system on 9,400 compute nodes? Who notices that node c7-1142 has crashed at 3 a.m., pulls it out of the scheduler's pool, and brings it back? Who checks that the person submitting a job is actually allowed to? None of these functions can live *on* the compute nodes themselves — they must work precisely when the compute nodes are broken, empty, or misbehaving.

The answer is a set of **head nodes** — small but highly available servers that form the cluster's *management plane*. The compute fabric moving your MPI messages is the *data plane*; the management plane is a second, logically separate computer system whose product is "a healthy, accounted, correctly configured set of compute nodes."

A production site typically runs these management roles (sometimes on separate machines, sometimes consolidated):

- **Provisioning / image service.** Nodes boot over the network (DHCP + TFTP + PXE), receive a disk image or a read-only image mounted over the network, and get their per-node configuration (hostname, IP, kernel arguments). Tools in this space include xCAT and Warewulf, both packaged in the OpenHPC project. The key mechanism is *statelessness*: if a node's OS is suspected corrupted, the standard cure is not debugging — it is a reimage, which takes minutes and returns the node to a known state.
- **Authentication and directory services.** Central directories (commonly LDAP; Kerberos for strong authentication) hold the user/group database so that your UID is identical on every node. Your home directory and identity follow you across 9,400 machines because none of them owns that information — they all ask the directory.
- **Time service.** NTP keeps every node's clock aligned. Scheduling logs, filesystem timestamps, job accounting, and TLS handshakes all silently break when clocks drift apart.
- **Monitoring and telemetry.** Metric collectors (node health, temperatures, power, link errors) feed a time-series stack — in practice most sites run Prometheus with exporters plus Grafana dashboards — and log aggregation collects kernel, driver, and scheduler messages. On clusters with accounting, tools like Slurm's own `sacct`/`sreport` (or XDCDB/XDMoD-class analytics systems) turn job history into utilization and fair-share data.
- **The scheduler head.** This is the machine running the batch scheduler's brain. In Slurm, that is `slurmctld`, the central controller that "monitors state of resources, manages job queues, [and] allocates resources," typically with a standby backup controller for failover; beside it runs `slurmdbd`, the database daemon that stores job accounting and pushes limits, QoS, and fair-share information back to the controller, backed by MySQL/MariaDB (SchedMD's own overview decks describe exactly this split). Each compute node runs only a small `slurmd` that spawns `slurmstepd` shepherds per job step — deliberately lightweight, because the node's job is to run *your* code, not the cluster's.

Two design principles are worth internalizing:

**First, the management plane must be more available than the thing it manages.** If `slurmctld` dies, no new jobs start anywhere — so it gets a hot-standby backup, redundant power and network, and its own small cluster of head nodes. When Slurm's database daemon is unreachable, the controller *caches* accounting data and keeps scheduling rather than failing the cluster (SchedMD documents this fallback explicitly). Degraded bookkeeping is preferred over a dead machine.

**Second, out-of-band beats in-band.** The most interesting management device is not a head node at all — it is the **baseboard management controller (BMC)**, a small processor on every server motherboard with its own network port on a dedicated management VLAN. The BMC works when the OS is dead: it holds the power button, the console output, the sensor readings, and the firmware. The classic protocol here was IPMI (1998-vintage, its own session crypto, awkward to script); the modern replacement is **Redfish**, a DMTF standard (DSP0266) that exposes platform management as a RESTful API — JSON resources over HTTPS, addressed by URI, discovered from a single `/redfish/v1/` ServiceRoot that links to collections like `Systems` (power state), `Chassis` (thermal and power sensors), and `Managers` (the BMC itself). The current specification is version 1.24.0 (published with the May 2026 DMTF release), and it mandates TLS 1.2+. The practical meaning for an HPC engineer: node lifecycle is now scriptable. A provisioning pipeline can interrogate 9,400 BMCs with plain HTTP clients, collect firmware versions, push updates, and power-cycle a node that hung mid-job — without an OS on that node ever participating. (DMTF even publishes a Redfish MCP server now, so agent-based automation can drive the same interface.)

```
             MANAGEMENT PLANE (separate VLANs)         
 users ──SSH──► login nodes                            
                  │ sbatch/salloc                      
                  ▼                                   
            ┌────────────┐   failover   ┌────────────┐ 
            │ slurmctld  │◄────────────►│ backup     │ 
            │ (head node)│              └────────────┘ 
            └─────┬──────┘                             
                  │ accounting, limits, fair-share     
            ┌─────▼──────┐    ┌─────────────┐          
            │  slurmdbd  │───►│ MySQL/MariaDB│         
            └────────────┘    └─────────────┘          
 provisioning (PXE/DHCP/TFTP) · LDAP/Kerberos · NTP     
 Prometheus ◄── node exporters, GPU (DCGM), BMC Redfish 
      │                                                 
      ▼      out-of-band: BMC on EVERY node            
 compute nodes (slurmd only; no cluster state)          
```

Concrete failure walk-through, to make it mechanism-level: a compute node hard-hangs during your 4,000-rank job. The node stops responding to the scheduler's health checks, so `slurmctld` marks it DOWN and (depending on configuration) the job either continues shrunken or is killed and requeued. Monitoring sees the node's metrics go flat through its last scrape. The operator opens the node's BMC via Redfish, reads the system event log and last console lines, decides it was a GPU driver panic, and issues `ComputerSystem.Reset` (a POST to the node's reset action URI) rather than walking to the machine room. The node PXE-boots, is reimaged, health-checks pass, and the scheduler marks it IDLE again. Nothing in this loop required the node's OS to be alive — that is the entire point of the management plane.

### 1.2 System topology: one machine, several hierarchies (SYS-1.7)

**Problem.** "Where is my data?" is the question topology answers. Inside a node, the answer is the memory hierarchy — NUMA domains, caches, HBM (covered in the GPU theme). At cluster scale the same question returns: is the process that needs this data on the *same node*, the *same switch*, the *same cabinet group*, or across the machine? The cost differences are enormous — nanoseconds within a node, microseconds within a group, and single-digit-to-tens of microseconds across a large dragonfly — so topology-aware software (rank placement, collective algorithms) is a first-order performance lever.

A supercomputer is not one hierarchy but several overlaid ones:

- **Compute topology** — how nodes attach to switches and switches to each other (the fabric from SYS-1.5, now at whole-machine scale).
- **Storage topology** — how the parallel filesystem's servers (metadata, object storage) attach: to the same fabric as compute, or to a dedicated one.
- **Management topology** — the out-of-band network from §1.1 plus provisioning and telemetry VLANs, deliberately independent of the compute fabric.

The interesting engineering is in the compute topology, and the best way to see the mechanisms is a real one. Frontier (OLCF) uses Cray Slingshot 11 in a **dragonfly** arrangement. From ORNL's own architecture materials:

- 74 compute cabinets ≈ 74 dragonfly **groups**, each holding 128 nodes and 32 switches;
- each switch is a high-radix **64-port** device (~12.8 Tb/s aggregate);
- groups are connected **all-to-all**: any two groups have 4 links between them at 25 GB/s each;
- each node has 4 NIC links (4 × 200 Gbps ≈ 100 GB/s injection) into its group's switches.

```
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ GROUP g  (e.g., one cabinet: 128 nodes, 32 switches)│
   │   ┌────┐ ┌────┐        ┌────┐   64-port switches,   │
   │   │ S1 │ │ S2 │  ...   │ S32│   fully meshed inside │
   │   └──┬─┘ └─┬──┘        └─┬──┘   the group          │
   │      └─────┴─────┬───────┘                          │
   │            ┌─────┴──────┐                           │
   │            │ 128 nodes  │  each node: 4 NIC links   │
   └────────────┴────────────┴───────────────────────────┘
        │
        │ 4 × 25 GB/s to EVERY other group (all-to-all)
        ▼
     groups 1 … 73
```

**Why dragonfly, mechanically?** Compare with a fat-tree/Clos. A three-level Clos over 9,408 radix-limited nodes needs many switch-to-switch hops and a very large number of long cables, and every packet crosses the upper levels. A dragonfly exploits high switch radix to cut the diameter: packets take at most **one hop within the source group, one inter-group hop, one hop within the destination group** — a maximal path length of ~3 switch stages. The price is that the all-to-all group wiring makes cable length and cable *count* a physical-facility problem (ORNL cites ~90 miles of Slingshot cabling for Frontier), and that global congestion can appear when many simultaneous flows collide on the same inter-group links — which is why Slingshot relies on adaptive routing (packets deflect to less loaded links) rather than the fixed paths of a pure fat-tree. This is a nice instance of a general HPC pattern: **topology choice = trading diameter and cable economics against congestion control complexity.**

**From topology to behavior.** The hierarchy gives software a notion of *distance*, and the scheduler can use it: Slurm's topology plugins (configured via `topology.conf` with a switch hierarchy, as in SchedMD's documentation) let jobs be packed onto electrically close nodes, reducing latency and congestion for latency-sensitive phases. For collective-heavy codes — allreduces in HPL/HPL-MxP, halo exchanges in CFD — group locality directly changes observed bandwidth. This is also why TOP500-style whole-machine runs are brutal: they are the one case where *every* group talks to *every* group simultaneously, saturating the inter-group links that normal job mixes leave half-used.

One more layer deserves a sentence: **electrical and physical topology interact with the logical one.** A cabinet is not just "a dragonfly group" — it is a physical object with a power feed, a cooling loop, and a weight (a Frontier cabinet is ~8,000 lb). Logical topology, power distribution, and cooling often share boundaries (cabinet = group = cooling zone), which matters when a power or cooling event takes out an entire *logical* region of the machine at once.

### 1.3 Capability versus capacity computing (SYS-1.8)

**The distinction.** A supercomputer can be used in two fundamentally different modes:

- **Capability computing**: run the largest possible job — the biggest grid, the finest resolution, the largest matrix — even if it occupies the whole machine. The metric is *time-to-solution for problems that cannot be solved anywhere else*. A whole-machine HPL run, a first-principles weapon simulation, a kilometer-scale global climate run: capability science.
- **Capacity computing**: run as many medium and small jobs as possible per day. The metric is *throughput* — core-hours delivered to the user community. Parameter sweeps, ensemble members, per-user analysis jobs: capacity science.

The same hardware serves both, but the operating requirements differ in ways that ripple through the whole stack:

**Scheduling.** A capability job requesting 90% of the machine cannot start until 90% of the machine is simultaneously free. Under FIFO that could mean days of queueing while small jobs continually "steal" nodes — so real schedulers combine priorities, reservations, and **backfilling** (letting small jobs run *now* only if they provably do not delay the big job). This is precisely why a cluster can look half-idle while a giant job waits (a phenomenon we will formalize when the curriculum reaches SYS-3.x: fragmentation, gang scheduling, utilization-versus-latency).

**Failure mathematics.** Here is a mechanism-level taste of why capability mode is hard. Suppose each node fails on average once every 4 years (illustrative number, not a claim about any specific system). A fleet of 9,408 nodes then experiences

  λ = 9,408 / (4 × 8,760 h) ≈ 0.27 failures/hour → one node failure every ~3.7 hours.

A 48-hour whole-machine run would expect to lose over a dozen nodes along the way — so capability runs demand checkpoint/restart, and the checkpoint interval is itself a trade-off (write state too rarely and you lose hours of work per failure; too often and checkpoint I/O dominates runtime). This is the SYS-18 module's opening problem, stated here at machine scale.

**Procurement and allocation.** Centers justify capability machines to funders with science that *cannot* be done otherwise, then sell time in core-hour allocations to thousands of users — capacity mode. Many leadership systems literally split the machine into a capability track and a capacity track. Modern practice also splits *hardware*: JUPITER, for instance, pairs its GPU "Booster" partition (the exascale part) with a separate CPU cluster partition aimed at capacity-style workloads — one procurement, two modes.

**A compact example.** Consider a 1 EF/s machine with 10,000 nodes and a 10-day allocation period. Option A: one capability campaign using 9,000 nodes for 5 days straight. Option B: 250 concurrent 40-node jobs, each 12 hours, ~500 jobs/day. Both deliver ~1.08 billion node-hours... but they are different products: A produced *one* answer to a question no other machine could address; B produced thousands of independent answers. Neither is "better" — the center needs both, and the scheduler is where the conflict is arbitrated.

### 1.4 The building blocks of a supercomputer (SYS-2.1)

Zoom out to the machine-room view. A leadership system is assembled from these blocks, and — this is the teaching point — **each block's numbers constrain the others**. Use Frontier as the specimen (ORNL/ORNL-DOE published figures):

- **Compute cabinets.** 74 HPE Cray EX compute cabinets, 128 nodes each, 9,408 nodes at launch (the OLCF user guide currently lists 77 Olympus racks / 9,856 nodes as installed). A cabinet is a physical unit: ~8,000 lb, ~400 kW supported, front and rear doors plumbed into the facility's water loop.
- **Nodes/blades.** Each node: 1 × 64-core AMD EPYC "Trento" CPU + 4 × AMD Instinct MI250X (8 Graphics Compute Dies, each with 64 GB HBM2 at ~1.6 TB/s), 512 GB DDR4, 4 × 200 Gbps Slingshot NICs — one NIC per GPU package, so HBM-resident data reaches the network without crossing the CPU. Node peak ≈ 190 TFLOP/s FP64 vector.
- **Interconnect.** The Slingshot-11 dragonfly from §1.2.
- **Storage tiers.** Node-local NVMe (~37 PB total) for scratch and staging; the center-wide Orion Lustre filesystem (~700 PB, multi-tier HDD+flash) for persistent, shared data. The tiering exists because checkpoint and I/O bursts behave differently from steady-state analysis (SYS-8/9/10 will build this out).
- **Power.** ~29 MW at launch for ~2 EF/s peak. Rule-of-thumb arithmetic (ours, not ORNL's): at $0.10/kWh, 1 MW costs ≈ 8,760,000 kWh/yr × $0.10 ≈ $0.88M/yr — so Frontier's power bill alone is on the order of $25M/yr. Power is not a utility detail; it is a *design input* that fixed the machine's shape.
- **Cooling.** Frontier is fully water-cooled — ORNL's materials note even the DIMMs and NICs are liquid-cooled — because 400 kW cabinets are far beyond what air can carry away. Warm-water direct liquid cooling moves heat into water at temperatures useful for facility heat reuse.
- **Software environment.** An OS and stack *as a block*: e.g., TOSS (the purpose-built Linux environment on LLNL's El Capitan), Red Hat Enterprise Linux on JUPITER, Kylin OS on China's LineShine, plus the scheduler, MPI, compilers, and container runtime. The point for a systems view: the software environment is as much a building block as the cabinet — machines are procured *with* their stacks.

The end-to-end walk ties the blocks together. Your `sbatch` script asks for 512 nodes; `slurmctld` allocates them (ideally group-local, §1.2); the job launches on nodes PXE-booted into a known image (§1.1); it stages inputs from Orion onto node-local NVMe; it runs, checkpointing periodically (§1.3's failure math); telemetry flows to Prometheus and power draw to the facility; on completion, accounting goes to `slurmdbd` and results are written back to Lustre. Every block appears in one job's life.

### 1.5 Homogeneous versus heterogeneous systems (SYS-2.2)

**The fork.** Given a power and dollar budget for FLOP/s, there are two canonical ways to spend it:

- **Homogeneous**: many identical CPU-only nodes. Every node is a complete, general-purpose computer; the programming model is uniform (processes + MPI, flat memory per node); there is no host-device boundary; every core does FP64 at full rate.
- **Heterogeneous (node-level)**: weaker host CPUs plus many accelerators (GPUs/APUs) per node. The accelerators concentrate enormous FLOP/s and FLOP/s/W but introduce a second architecture per node, a host↔device data path that must be managed, and (usually) a much larger ratio of compute to host memory and I/O bandwidth.

**Why accelerators win on efficiency — the mechanism.** A GPU's advantage is not magic; it is *specialization*. Matrix-heavy FP arithmetic is embarrassingly regular, so a GPU spends transistors on many simple lanes and matrix units rather than on out-of-order control, branch prediction, and large caches per core. Per watt, that structure delivers far more FP64/FP16/FP8 throughput than a general-purpose core — but only for code whose parallelism and memory access match it. Sparse, branchy, irregular codes (graph traversal, unstructured meshes, event-driven simulation) leave accelerators underutilized, while dense linear algebra, stencils, and tensor workloads saturate them. This is the same arithmetic-intensity story from the HPL theme, now expressed as an *architecture choice*: heterogeneous nodes trade generality for throughput on regular kernels.

**The costs, mechanically:**

- *Data placement.* Host DRAM ↔ accelerator HBM transfers are explicit programming work (and a bandwidth bottleneck — the reason NVLink/C2C-class links and unified-memory designs like MI300A and Grace Hopper exist).
- *Balance drift.* Four GPUs per node concentrate ~190 TFLOP/s of compute behind ~100 GB/s of network injection — the node's compute:communication ratio changes shape, which is why strong-scaling limits arrive earlier per node and why collectives and overlap matter more (the DIST theme's territory).
- *Software.* Two toolchains, two programming models (CUDA/HIP/SYCL + MPI), version-matched drivers and runtimes — a real cost that SYS-6/7 (environments, containers) will quantify.
- *Memory capacity.* HBM is fast but small per FLOP; capacity-bound jobs (large in-core sparse matrices) can prefer CPU nodes.

**Heterogeneity at the system level.** There is a newer, third form: instead of making every node a compromise, build the *machine* out of multiple node types — each purpose-built for a workload class — behind one scheduler and one storage system. That is exactly Los Alamos's announced Mission design (see §2.2): three partitions (Vera-CPU nodes, Rubin NVL-4, Rubin NVL-72) in one supercomputer. System-level heterogeneity concedes that no single node architecture is best at 3D multiphysics *and* massive tensor AI, and stops pretending otherwise. The price is a harder software problem: applications must be portable *within* one machine, which is why performance-portable programming models (Kokkos, RAJA, SYCL — SYS-12/13) and early testbeds exist.

**Reading the evidence like an engineer.** The cleanest empirical comparison in years is sitting in the June 2026 TOP500 (details in §2.1): LineShine reached 2.198 EF/s with 13.79M CPU cores and no accelerators; El Capitan holds 1.809 EF/s with 11.34M cores of APU (CPU+GPU in one package). LineShine's path: enormous, simple, uniform parallelism at 1.55 GHz. El Capitan's path: fewer, fatter nodes with matrix hardware. And the mixed-precision column exposes the difference at the mechanism level: on HPL-MxP (which exploits low-precision matrix units), El Capitan jumps 9.2× over its HPL score while LineShine moves only 3.6× — because tensor cores accelerate low-precision math and general CPU cores largely cannot. Same benchmark, two architectures, and the ratio *is* the specialization premium. Meanwhile the efficiency column (El Capitan 60.94 GF/W, LineShine 52.07 GF/W at much larger scale) shows accelerators delivering more FLOP/s per watt — at 42.2 MW for LineShine versus ~29.7 MW for El Capitan, the design trade-offs are written in megawatts.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 The June 2026 TOP500: LineShine at No. 1, and a live experiment in system design

**What happened.** At ISC 2026 in Hamburg (June 23), the 67th TOP500 list debuted **LineShine** at No. 1 — a previously unlisted system at the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen, China — with **2.198 exaflop/s** on HPL, ~80% of its 2.736 EF/s theoretical peak. It is the first system above two sustained exaflop/s using **CPUs only**, the first Chinese system to lead the list since Sunway TaihuLight (2017), and it raises the count of >1 EF/s systems to **five**, spread across Asia, Europe, and North America for the first time: LineShine (2.198), El Capitan (1.809), Frontier (1.353), Aurora (1.012), JUPITER Booster (1.000). Eni's new HPC7 debuted at No. 6 (571.5 PF/s, the same HPE Cray EX255a + MI300A architecture as El Capitan); Microsoft's Eagle, Eni's HPC6, Fugaku, and CSCS's Alps complete the top ten.

**What technically changed/demonstrated.** LineShine is built on the custom "LingKun" platform: 13,789,440 cores of 304-core LX2 processors at 1.55 GHz, a proprietary LingQi interconnect, Kylin OS, drawing ~42.2 MW (52.07 GF/W). It also took No. 1 on **HPCG** (22.0 PF/s, ahead of El Capitan's 17.41 and Fugaku's 16.0) — notable because HPCG's sparse, memory-bound kernels usually punish brute-force CPU designs relative to HPL. On **HPL-MxP**, LineShine managed only 7.92 EF/s (3.6× over HPL, versus El Capitan's 16.7 EF/s at 9.2×), consistent with a CPU-only design lacking low-precision matrix units. Green500 leadership was unchanged: KAIROS (CALMIP/Toulouse) at 73.28 GF/W, ahead of ROMEO-2025 and DKRZ's Levante GPU extension — three BullSequana XH3000 / Grace Hopper systems, whose order tracks system *size*, since smaller installs of identical technology edge out larger ones on efficiency.

**Why it matters and how it connects.** This list is the SYS-2.2 trade-off rendered in data: one No. 1 machine built the homogeneous way (13.79M simple cores, 42 MW), and four built the heterogeneous way (11.34M or fewer cores with accelerators, at higher GF/W and much higher mixed-precision throughput). It also illustrates SYS-1.8: LineShine's HPL run is the ultimate capability demonstration — one job on ~14M cores — while the same physical machine serves capacity workloads (its HPCG result) the rest of the year. And Eni's pairing of HPC6 + HPC7 (a combined 1,048 PF/s sustained per Eni's own announcements) is capability-class computing in *industry*, not a national lab.

**What to watch next.** The November 2026 list (SC26) — whether China submits more LingKun-class systems; whether JUPITER files an HPCG number; and whether any exascale system pushes efficiency past El Capitan's 60.94 GF/W without shrinking.

### 2.2 Los Alamos installs the first NVIDIA Vera CPU systems (Oct 1, 2026)

**What happened.** Los Alamos National Laboratory announced that the first **NVIDIA Vera CPU** systems — delivered in August — are installed in its Darwin testbed and are "up and running, supporting benchmarking and application readiness." The node is an air-cooled MGX server combining two Vera CPUs, each with 88 high-bandwidth "Olympus" cores, in a two-socket configuration joined by NVLink chip-to-chip links.

**What technically changed/demonstrated.** Vera is the CPU half of LANL's next-generation strategy: the forthcoming **Mission, Vision, and Veritas** supercomputers will all pair Vera CPUs with NVIDIA Rubin GPUs on HPE's GX5000 architecture, Quantum-X800 InfiniBand fabric. The most architecturally significant piece is **Mission**, the first NNSA Advanced Technology System built from *multiple purpose-built partitions* instead of one general-purpose architecture: **Ranger** (standalone Vera CPU nodes — ~2,300 of them per NVIDIA's announcement), **Sandstone** (Vera Rubin NVL-4), and **Starlight** (Vera Rubin NVL-72), sharing a storage and software environment. NVIDIA reports Vera outperforming the CPUs in LANL's Crossroads x86 system by over 3× on Branson, an open-source Monte Carlo heat-transfer code (a vendor/lab claim, not an independent measurement). Mission (~2027) will replace Crossroads for classified workloads; Starlight and Vision's GPU partitions could arrive as early as late 2026.

**Why it matters and how it connects.** This is system-level heterogeneity (SYS-2.2) arriving in a real flagship procurement: LANL is explicitly declining to force one node type to serve both high-bandwidth CPU-style multiphysics simulation *and* rack-scale tensor AI, and instead integrating three node types under one scheduler. Equally relevant to SYS-1.6: the way it is happening is a **testbed-first workflow** — install a small MGX system, stand up the software stack, benchmark and stabilize applications *before* the supercomputer exists. Darwin is the management-plane pattern (provisioning, monitoring, application readiness) applied as a risk-reduction instrument for a multibillion-dollar machine. The Vera node itself also continues the codesign lineage from Grace (which powers LANL's Venado) — a decade-plus ARM-based CPU codesign collaboration between the lab, NVIDIA, and HPE.

**What to watch next.** Starlight (NVL-72) delivery late 2026; the first independent performance data on Vera's Olympus cores; how a single Slurm installation expresses three partitions (likely as partitions/queues — SYS-4 material).

### 2.3 JUPITER: the efficiency face of exascale

**What happened.** With the June 2026 list, JUPITER's Booster (EuroHPC/Jülich Supercomputing Centre, ~24,000 NVIDIA GH200 superchips on Eviden's direct-liquid-cooled BullSequana XH3000, quad-rail NDR200 InfiniBand) held No. 5 at exactly 1.000 EF/s — its November 2025 milestone as Europe's first exascale system intact — while being ranked the **most energy-efficient exascale system in the world**, delivering more than 63 GF/W (FZ Jülich notes it beats the new No. 1 on energy per operation, and quotes >40 EF/s theoretical FP8).

**Why it matters and how it connects.** JUPITER is the SYS-2.1 lesson in one system: the building blocks are doing the talking. Warm-water direct liquid cooling (not the compute) is what makes >63 GF/W possible; the warm water is reused in the Jülich campus heating network, which converts the machine's waste heat from a cost into a resource. It is also SYS-2.2 in procurement form — a GPU Booster for capability-class simulation and AI, alongside a CPU cluster partition for capacity workloads. And its Green500 context (smaller GH200 systems like KAIROS at 73.28 GF/W beat it on efficiency) demonstrates the scale penalty: more nodes, more links, more conversion losses — efficiency is a function of system size, not just technology.

**What to watch next.** Whether JUPITER submits an HPCG result (it has not yet); early science stories from the AI Factory (JAIF) built around it.
