## 0. Concise Summary

Today's issue is about the single most important circuit block in a GPU: the **warp scheduler**. Everything else in this lesson — latency hiding, divergence penalties, synchronization cost, and unequal pipeline throughputs — is a consequence of what the scheduler can and cannot do.

An SM does not execute threads; it executes **warp instructions**, one per scheduler per cycle, chosen from the set of *ready* warps. A warp becomes unready when it waits on a register whose producing instruction has not finished (tracked by scoreboards) or on a memory access still in flight. Because issue is **in order, with no speculation**, a stalled warp simply stops — and the scheduler's salvation is that it has dozens of other resident warps to pick instead, with a **zero-cost context switch** made possible by a register file that is statically partitioned among all resident warps. Little's Law tells you how many independent instructions must be in flight to cover a given latency, and it explains why memory-bound kernels need far more resident warps than compute-bound ones.

When threads within a warp take different sides of a data-dependent branch, the hardware **serializes the paths** — the warp executes each side in turn with a partial active mask, and a July 2026 cross-generational measurement study confirms the cost is linear in the number of paths, cutting warp efficiency to roughly 32/k, independent of occupancy, and fully removable by compiler **predication**. When warps must cooperate, **barriers and fences** provide the two distinct services of execution ordering and memory visibility — and both have real architectural cost, because a barrier forces convergence and idles the SM's latency-hiding machinery.

Finally, the instructions that survive scheduling land in **unequal pipelines**: FP32 at full rate, INT32 at full or half rate, special functions at roughly 1/8 rate, and FP64 anywhere from 1/2 (datacenter) to 1/64 (consumer dies). Reading a kernel as a mix of pipeline consumers is the first step toward predicting its bottleneck before profiling.

Two key developments connect directly to this material: a 2026 arXiv study characterizing warp divergence from Pascal through Blackwell (finding the cost model unchanged even as the ISA machinery keeps evolving), and microbenchmark dissections of Blackwell showing its warp scheduler re-tuned for low-precision, high-ILP, clean-control-flow workloads — plus the end of warp-synchronous matrix instructions.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

Yesterday's lesson established the SM/Compute Unit as the GPU's building block. Today we go inside it and follow the life of one warp instruction — from being selected, to being stalled, to surviving a branch, to coordinating with other warps, and finally to the arithmetic pipeline where it actually executes.

### 1.1 Warp scheduling: choosing useful work every cycle (GPU-2.5)

**The problem.** A modern SM hosts up to 64 resident warps (2,048 threads on A100/H100-class parts; the Blackwell Tuning Guide reports 64 warps/SM for compute capability 10.0 and 48 for 12.0). The SM's execution resources are far too small to run all of them simultaneously — an Ada SM, for example, has 128 FP32 lanes. The hardware must therefore decide, **every issue cycle**, which warp gets to send its next instruction down the pipes. That decision is the warp scheduler's entire job.

**The hardware structure.** Since Fermi, NVIDIA's SM is divided into four **processing blocks** (sub-partitions). Each partition owns its own slice of the register file, its own L0 instruction cache, **one warp scheduler, one dispatch unit, and its own dedicated execution units**. The Ada whitepaper describes the pattern precisely: per partition, 16 dedicated-FP32 CUDA cores, 16 cores that can execute either FP32 or INT32, one fourth-generation Tensor Core, four load/store units, and one Special Function Unit. Each resident warp is statically assigned to exactly one partition's scheduler, so the four schedulers never contend over the same warp. The CUDA Programming Guide states the contract plainly: "At each instruction issue cycle, a warp scheduler selects a warp with threads ready to execute its next instruction … and issues the instruction to those threads." On modern architectures (CC 8.x onward) each scheduler issues at most one warp instruction per cycle — up to four per SM per cycle in total.

The cross-vendor picture is the same idea with different names and widths. An AMD Compute Unit contains four SIMD units, each 16 lanes wide; a 64-thread **wavefront** occupies one SIMD unit for four cycles per instruction, with a scheduler per SIMD selecting among the CU's resident wavefronts. An Intel Xe-core pairs vector engines with hardware threads that the issue logic round-robins. In every case: *a small scheduler picks from a pool of ready execution groups, in order, one at a time.*

**What "ready" means: scoreboarding.** GPU schedulers issue instructions **in order** — the CUDA Programming Guide notes that, unlike CPU cores, "SMs issue instructions in order and do not perform branch prediction or speculative execution." There is no register renaming and no out-of-order window. So before a warp instruction can issue, the hardware must verify that its operands are available. It does this with a **scoreboard** — a small table per scheduler that tracks, for each register, whether a pending instruction will still write it.

Conceptually there are two classes:

- **Short-scoreboard (fixed-latency) dependencies.** Arithmetic instructions have known latencies — the Volta Tuning Guide gives 4 cycles for dependent core FMA issue (down from 6 on Pascal). The scheduler simply counts down: a consumer FMA reading the producer's destination register cannot issue for 4 cycles.
- **Long-scoreboard (variable-latency) dependencies.** A load from global memory does not know when its data returns — it depends on cache hits, DRAM queues, and contention. The destination register stays *not ready* on the long scoreboard until the memory system writes the data back, which can be hundreds of cycles later.

NVIDIA's profiler exposes exactly this taxonomy as **warp stall reasons**. In Nsight Compute's Warp State Statistics, *Stall Long Scoreboard* is defined as warps "waiting on a scoreboard dependency on L1TEX (local, global, surface, tex) operation" — in practice, waiting for DRAM. Other states include *Stall Short Scoreboard* (fixed-latency dependency), *Wait* (fixed-latency pipe saturation), *Barrier* (waiting at `__syncthreads`), *Branch Resolving* (a branch's condition still computing), *Not Selected* (ready, but another warp was picked this cycle), and *No Instruction* (instruction fetch miss). In one NVIDIA blog case study, each warp spent on average 15.4 of the 33.3 cycles between issues — about 46% — stalled on a long scoreboard: a textbook memory-bound signature, localized by the profiler to the specific line loading ~4 GB of data.

**The selection policy.** Among ready warps, hardware typically uses round-robin or oldest-first (age-based) policies. The policy details vary by generation and are not fully documented, but the *purpose* is: spread issue slots fairly so no single warp starves, and prefer warps whose instructions are actually ready — never waste an issue slot on a warp that will immediately stall.

**A concrete cycle-by-cycle picture.** Consider one scheduler with 8 resident warps, where warp W0 issues a global load into register R5:

```
cycle 0:  W0 issues LDG R5 ← [addr]     (R5 marked busy on long scoreboard)
cycle 1:  W0's next instr needs R5 → not ready. Scheduler picks W1.
cycle 2:  W1 issues; W2 issues; W3 ...  (each ready warp takes a turn)
...
cycle 400: data returns from DRAM; R5 scoreboard cleared; W0 ready again
cycle 401: scheduler eventually picks W0 again
```

The 400-cycle DRAM latency was *invisible* — provided other warps had independent work. If all 8 warps had issued loads to the same congested memory system, the scheduler would have nothing ready: **issue starvation**, the SM's true failure mode. Note also what did *not* happen: no state was saved or restored when switching warps. Every resident warp's registers physically live in the register file simultaneously, statically partitioned at block-launch time. Warp switching costs nothing because there is nothing to switch. This is the pivotal architectural fact the next topic builds on.

### 1.2 Latency hiding: throughput from other people's work (GPU-2.6)

**The problem.** Latencies inside a GPU are brutal. Arithmetic dependent-issue latency is ~4 cycles; an uncached global-memory access is on the order of 500 cycles. A CPU attacks such latencies with a large out-of-order window, speculation, and deep speculative prefetch — enormous, power-hungry machinery per core. A GPU makes the opposite bet: keep the per-core issue logic tiny and in-order, and instead ensure that whenever one warp stalls, ten others are ready. This is the design decision that explains most of the CPU-vs-GPU transistor allocation you met in GPU-1.2.

**The mechanism, quantitatively: Little's Law.** A queueing-theory identity from 1961 does the heavy lifting:

> **concurrency = latency × throughput**

The number of independent operations that must be *in flight* equals the latency of each, multiplied by the completion rate you want to sustain. Apply it twice on V100-class hardware:

*Arithmetic.* The Volta Tuning Guide: dependent FMA issue latency is 4 cycles, and each scheduler can issue one instruction per cycle. To keep the FP32 pipe fed at one instruction per cycle you therefore need **4 independent instructions in flight per scheduler**. Those 4 can come from 4 different warps (thread-level parallelism, TLP) — or from a single warp whose instructions are independent of each other (instruction-level parallelism, ILP). NVIDIA states the consequence directly: core-math latencies "can be hidden by as few as 4 warps per SM, assuming 4-way instruction-level parallelism per warp."

*Memory.* Suppose we want to sustain V100's ~800 GB/s of HBM2 bandwidth against a ~500-cycle DRAM latency, with the memory clock at ~867 MHz. Bytes per cycle ≈ 800 GB/s ÷ 867 MHz ≈ 923 B/cycle. Bytes that must be in flight = 923 B/cycle × 500 cycles ≈ **460 KB**. Spread across 80 SMs, that is ~5.7 KB per SM of outstanding loads; a warp loading one float per thread generates 128 B, so on the order of *dozens of warps* per SM must have loads outstanding simultaneously. (This is an order-of-magnitude estimate — real rates depend on clocks, caching, and transaction granularity — but it explains the qualitative gap: hiding 500 cycles needs ~100× more in-flight work than hiding 4.)

Volkov's Berkeley dissertation (*Understanding Latency Hiding on GPUs*) measured this on Maxwell: roughly 24 warps per SM to hide arithmetic latency and about 30 to hide memory latency at full throughput — but crucially, with enough ILP per warp, far fewer warps suffice. His broader point, now standard practice: **occupancy and ILP are substitutable currencies**. A kernel at 25% occupancy with 4-way ILP per warp can outperform the same kernel at 100% occupancy with serially dependent instructions, because what matters is independent work in flight, not resident threads per se.

**Why more occupancy is not automatically better.** Maximum residency is capped by the register file (64K registers per SM shared across all resident threads — on Blackwell, 255 registers/thread maximum), by shared memory, and by block-slot limits. Pushing occupancy up often means forcing the compiler to use fewer registers per thread, which can introduce register spills to local memory — *new* long-scoreboard traffic. The occupancy-vs-ILP trade-off is a core theme of Module 5 (GPU-5.2, GPU-5.3); today you only need the causal chain: register demand → resident warps → in-flight independent work → ability to hide latency.

**When latency hiding fails, and software's role.** If profiling shows memory bandwidth *not* saturated and warps mostly stalled on `Long Scoreboard`, the SM is starving, not bandwidth-limited. NVIDIA's recommended remedies: unroll loops to expose more independent loads per warp (software pipelining), or explicitly **prefetch** the next iteration's data while computing on the current one — software prefetching substituting for the hardware prefetch engines a CPU would have, which GPUs omit as too expensive in silicon relative to their huge lane counts.

**Latency vs. throughput, one more time.** This is the mechanism-level answer to GPU-1.5's puzzle: a single warp's instruction may take 4–500 cycles to complete, yet the SM sustains near-peak issue every cycle, because completion latency of *any individual* instruction is irrelevant when enough *other* instructions are available. Throughput is a property of the pool; latency is a property of one instruction.

### 1.3 Branch divergence and predication (GPU-2.7)

**The problem.** A warp executes one instruction at a time for 32 lanes — effectively a 32-wide SIMD operation in hardware, whatever the SIMT programming illusion promises. If a data-dependent condition is true for some lanes and false for others, there is no single "next instruction" for the warp. The hardware must do something with the divergent control flow.

**The mechanism: serial execution with masks.** The warp splits its **active mask**. The taken side executes with lanes of side A active (side B's lanes disabled, idling); then the not-taken side executes with mask B; then the warp **reconverges**. If a branch splits a warp into k paths that each take roughly the same work, the warp does k times the work while using on average only 32/k of its lanes per pass: warp execution efficiency ≈ **32/k**. A 2026 cross-generational measurement study (today's first development) confirms this quantitatively: execution time grows as T(k) ≈ s·k — *linear* in the number of paths, with no super-linear reconvergence penalty — and the penalty is independent of occupancy, because the idled lanes are a per-warp property that no amount of other warps can recover.

**Reconvergence: where the paths rejoin.** The natural rejoin point is the **immediate post-dominator** of the branch — the earliest instruction every path must reach. Two hardware eras:

- **Pre-Volta (through Pascal):** a per-warp **reconvergence stack**. A divergent branch pushes the fall-through PC and the active mask; each side executes; the stack pops and restores the full mask at the reconvergence point. All 32 threads shared one program counter, so the warp was *always* in lockstep — with one notorious consequence: a thread spinning on a lock held by another thread of the *same warp* could never make progress, a structural deadlock.
- **Volta onward — Independent Thread Scheduling (ITS):** every thread gets its **own program counter and call stack**. The scheduler may interleave the two sides of a divergence at warp-instruction granularity, and hardware guarantees intra-warp forward progress (fixing the deadlock). But with no shared PC there is *no implicit reconvergence guarantee*, so the compiler must name reconvergence points explicitly — in SASS these are convergence-barrier instructions (`BSSY`/`BSYNC` arming and syncing a small file of barrier registers). The CUDA Programming Guide warns that ITS "can break code that relies on implicit warp-synchronous behavior" — synchronization-free intra-warp reductions written for pre-Volta GPUs must be revisited, and warp-synchronous intrinsics gained explicit masks (`__shfl_sync`, `__syncwarp`).

**Predication: eliminating the branch instead of paying for it.** For short divergent regions, the compiler can avoid control flow entirely. The condition is computed into a **predicate register** (`setp`), and each instruction in both paths is issued *predicated* — `@P instr` — so it executes for all lanes but commits its result only where the predicate holds. Value selection becomes a predicated select (`selp`): both `y = a` and `y = b` execute; lanes keep the one their predicate chose. The cost: you always execute both paths' instructions. The win: no mask serialization, no reconvergence machinery, and — as the 2026 study measured — the divergence penalty disappears entirely. Predication is profitable exactly when the paths are a few instructions; for long paths, executing both sides at full warp width would be slower than serializing.

**Worked example.** `if (x > 0) y = sqrt(x); else y = 0;` compiles to something like:

```
        setp.gt.f32  P0, x, 0.0
        sqrt.approx.f32  r, x          ; both paths execute
        selp.f32     y, r, 0.0, P0     ; per-lane select
```

No branch, no divergence — two paths of one instruction each are cheaper predicated than serialized. Contrast a divergent `for` loop with data-dependent trip counts per lane: the warp must run up to max(iterations) passes with shrinking masks; this is why data-dependent early-exit loops are among the worst GPU patterns.

**Making branches uniform.** The zero-cost case is a **uniform branch** — a condition with the same value for every lane in the warp (e.g., derived from `blockIdx`, kernel parameters, or shared scalars). The warp takes one path; the other never issues. Good GPU code pushes conditionals toward uniformity: restructure kernels so branchy phases run as separate kernel launches, sort or compact data so whole warps take the same path, or replace per-lane branches with warp votes (`__all_sync`, `__any_sync`, `__ballot_sync`) that reduce a per-lane predicate to a warp-uniform value.

**Where HPC codes hit this.** Sparse linear algebra (`if (a_ij != 0)` guards in SpMV/SpGEMM), adaptive mesh refinement (per-cell refinement decisions), particle-in-cell boundary guards, graph traversals with irregular degree, and Lanczos/CG convergence checks — each is a classic divergence source, and each has a known compaction or restructuring cure.

### 1.4 Synchronization within the GPU (GPU-2.8)

**The problem.** Latency hiding means warps run at unpredictable, interleaved rates. The moment warps cooperate — one warp produces a tile in shared memory that another consumes — *unpredictable interleaving becomes a correctness bug* unless the program can (a) make execution wait, and (b) make memory writes visible. These are two different services, and CUDA separates them.

**The block barrier: `__syncthreads()`.** Every thread of the block must arrive before any proceeds; it also orders shared-memory accesses across the block (a combined execution + memory guarantee, lowering to `bar.sync 0` in SASS). Implementation is in hardware: SMs provide a set of **named barriers** with arrival counters — each active block's `__syncthreads` uses one, counting arriving warps. Two rules with sharp teeth: all threads must reach the barrier (a divergent `__syncthreads` is undefined behavior — the barrier never completes for the missing warps' lanes), and the barrier is block-scoped only; blocks never synchronize with each inside a kernel (until cooperative-groups grid sync, which requires cooperative launch).

**Memory fences: ordering without waiting.** A fence orders this thread's memory operations *as observed by other threads*, without stopping execution. CUDA exposes three scopes, mapping to PTX `membar` levels:

| Primitive | PTX | Scope | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| `__threadfence_block()` | `membar.cta` | block | prior writes visible to the block |
| `__threadfence()` | `membar.gl` | device | prior writes visible to all threads on the GPU |
| `__threadfence_system()` | `membar.sys` | system | visible to host and peer devices |

The canonical pattern is **producer–consumer flag passing** across blocks (which no barrier can cover, since blocks don't share one):

```c
// producer block
data[i] = value;
__threadfence();          // order: data before flag
atomicExch(flag, 1);      // consumer sees flag==1 ⇒ data[i] is visible
```

Without the fence, the consumer may observe `flag == 1` while the data write is still in flight or invisible — writes from one SM may become observable in a different order than issued. (The scope concept — CTA / GPU / system — returns in Module 4 for atomics, and Hopper's thread-block clusters add a cluster scope on top.)

**Warp-level synchronization.** Post-Volta, implicit warp lockstep is gone, so intra-warp communication primitives carry explicit masks: `__syncwarp(mask)` is a convergence barrier for the named lanes; `__shfl_sync`, `__any_sync`, `__ballot_sync` internally converge their participating lanes. These are far cheaper than block barriers — a warp is already resident on one scheduler, so convergence is local.

**Cooperative Groups and split barriers.** Modern CUDA generalizes all of this: `cg::this_thread_block().sync()` is the typed `__syncthreads`; `tiled_partition<32>(block)` gives warp- and sub-warp-sized groups; and `cuda::barrier` supports **arrive/wait splitting** — a thread arrives at the barrier, performs independent work, and only then waits. Split barriers are the synchronization backbone of software pipelining on Hopper/Blackwell: producer warps deposit tiles and arrive; consumer warps wait only at the point of actual use, keeping latency-hiding machinery running during the synchronization window. (Hopper's TMA + `cuda::pipeline` formalizes this as *warp specialization* — producer warps move data while consumer warps compute, a preview of GPU-10.x.)

**Why synchronization has architectural cost.** A barrier is the anti-latency-hiding instruction: every warping waiting at a barrier is *deliberately* unready, so the scheduler's pool shrinks; deep memory pipelines mean the last write may take hundreds of cycles to become visible, and all arrivals must wait for the slowest straggler plus that visibility window. Named barriers are also finite hardware resources — one is consumed per synchronizing block (32 on recent SMs), a rare but real occupancy limiter. The engineering rule that falls out: **synchronize at the coarsest scope that is correct** — warp-sync inside a warp, block barrier inside a block, fences across blocks, and never a device-wide pattern where a block-level one suffices.

### 1.5 Arithmetic pipelines: where issued instructions actually go (GPU-3.1)

**The structure.** The instruction the scheduler picked now needs an execution unit, and here the SM is *not* symmetric. Take the verified Ada SM partition layout from §1.1 — 16 dedicated-FP32 lanes, 16 FP32/INT32 lanes, 4 load/store units, 1 SFU, 1 Tensor Core per partition — and read it as four parallel **pipes** with different widths:

| Pipe (per Ada SM, 4 partitions) | Lanes/cycle | Relative rate vs FP32 |
|---|---|---|
| FP32 FADD / FMUL / FFMA | 128 | 1× (full rate) |
| INT32 (via the 64 unified lanes) | 64 | ½×–1× depending on instruction |
| Load/store (address + data movement) | 16/SM–ish (4/partition) | issue-limited |
| SFU: rcp, rsqrt, sin, cos, lg2, ex2 | 16 | ⅛× |
| FP64 | ~2 (consumer GB203) | 1/64× |
| Tensor Core (matrix ops) | — | separate engine (Module 3.5) |

The SFU row deserves its mechanism. Since Fermi, NVIDIA has described the SFU as executing "one instruction per thread, per clock" with "a warp execut[ing] over eight clocks" — 4 SFU lanes per partition means a 32-lane transcendental takes 8 passes, hence the ~⅛ rate. The SFU pipeline is *decoupled* from dispatch, so other instructions issue while it grinds; but the throughput ceiling stands. The FP64 row shows the consumer/datacenter split starkly: the Blackwell microbenchmark study measured GH100 at 64 FP64 units/SM versus **2** on the consumer GB203 die — consumer chips carry almost no FP64 because graphics and inference do not buy any.

**Why the rates differ: silicon economics.** An IEEE FP64 FMA unit costs roughly 4× the area of an FP32 unit (wide exponent/mantissa datapaths, alignment shifters). SFUs use table-driven quadratic interpolation rather than full iterative dividers, buying small area at reduced accuracy (`__sinf` vs `sin`). Integer multiply and bit-manipulation historically ran at half or quarter FP32 rate; by Turing/Ampere, integer add and IMAD reached full rate because address arithmetic dominates real kernels. Turing's whitepaper quantifies why: profiling showed "an average of 36 integer operations for every 100 floating point operations," so Turing added an independent INT32 datapath beside every FP32 lane pair — integer address arithmetic no longer blocks FP32 issue, an effective ~36% FP32 throughput gain for typical mixes. Consumer Blackwell goes further, *unifying* INT32/FP32 in one lane group.

**Latency vs. throughput inside a pipe.** A pipe's **depth** (stages) sets dependent-instruction latency; its **width** (lanes) sets throughput. FP32 FMA: 4-cycle dependent latency, yet 1 instruction/cycle sustained — successive FMAs to *independent* registers pipeline perfectly; back-to-back dependent FMAs run at ¼ rate. This is exactly the short-scoreboard behavior from §1.1, now explained structurally: the scoreboard delay *is* the pipe depth.

**Reading a loop as a pipeline mix.** Take a typical inner loop body: one FFMA (FP32, 1×), one INT32 address add (1×), one FP compare (1×), one `rsqrtf` (⅛×), one 32-bit integer multiply for indexing (1×). Cost in "FP32-lane-cycles" per warp instruction: 32/128 for the FFMA (¼ SM-cycle of the FP32 pipe), 32/128 for the add, 32/128 compare, **32/16 = 2 SM-cycles** of SFU pipe for the rsqrt, 32/128 for the index multiply. The single `rsqrtf` costs as much pipe time as *eight* FMAs — which is why CUDA's fast-math intrinsics (`__frsqrt_rn` alternatives, polynomial approximations, `__expf`) and hoisting invariants out of loops matter, and why integer division or modulo (emulated in software — GPUs have no integer divide unit) should be replaced by multiplies/shifts in index math.

**A numerical example of the whole pipeline.** Suppose a kernel's inner loop is 90% FFMA + 10% SFU by instruction count. Per 10 instructions: 10 × ¼ = 2.5 FP32-pipe cycles and 1 × 2 = 2 SFU-pipe cycles… wait — 10 instructions, 1 of them SFU: SFU cost = 1 × 2 = 2 SM-cycles vs FP32 cost = 9 × ¼ ≈ 2.25. The pipes are nearly balanced — the SFU's 10% instruction share consumes ~47% of the bottleneck-relevant pipe time. Replace the SFU call with a cheap polynomial (back on the FP32 pipe: 3 FMAs ≈ 0.75 cycles) and the loop's pipe pressure drops by ~40%. This "cost the mix, not the count" analysis is precisely what Nsight Compute's pipe-utilization sections automate (Load/Store, Texture, Control Flow, Arithmetic as the four major logical pipe groups) — but you can now do the estimate on paper first.

**Connections.** The unequal-rate table is the foundation for three upcoming topics: FMA and FLOP counting (GPU-3.3 — why peak FLOP/s is counted as 2 per FMA per lane per cycle), precision-vs-throughput economics (GPU-3.2, GPU-3.7), and deriving theoretical peak (GPU-3.8: 128 FP32 lanes/SM × 2 flops × clock × SM count — the very exercise the Ada/Turing whitepaper TFLOPS figures exemplify). The load/store pipe's role in coalescing waits for Module 4.

### 1.6 The through-line

One circuit — the warp scheduler — ties today together. It can only pick *ready* warps, so scoreboards define what it sees (§1.1). It survives long latencies only by having other ready warps, quantified by Little's Law (§1.2). Divergence shrinks each pick's value to 32/k lanes (§1.3). Barriers deliberately make warps unready (§1.4). And the unequal pipes determine what each pick is worth (§1.5). Performance engineering on a GPU is, at bottom, the craft of keeping every scheduler pick cheap, ready, and wide.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 Cross-generational measurement study: warp divergence cost is stable from Pascal to Blackwell even as the ISA machinery keeps changing

**What happened.** A July 2026 arXiv study, *Characterizing Warp Divergence from Pascal to Blackwell* (arXiv:2607.23402), ran cycle-accurate microbenchmarks, hardware-counter measurements (the Nsight Compute metric `smsp__thread_inst_executed_per_inst_executed.ratio` — average active lanes per issued warp instruction), and static analysis of compiler-generated SASS across Ampere, Hopper, datacenter and consumer Blackwell, anchored against a pre-ITS Pascal part.

**What was demonstrated.** (i) Divergent paths serialize *linearly*, T(k) ≈ s·k, with no super-linear reconvergence penalty up to a full 32-way split, and warp efficiency falls as 32/k — the cost model taught in §1.3 is confirmed quantitatively. (ii) The penalty is independent of occupancy — resident warps cannot recover idled lanes of a divergent warp. (iii) Predication removes the serialization cost. (iv) Crucially, the same cost law already held on Pascal, showing the programmer-visible cost *predates* ITS. (v) What *has* changed is the compiler-emitted machinery: Pascal's per-warp SSY/SYNC stack became barrier-register instructions on Ampere/Hopper; Blackwell adds a two-tier convergence-barrier classification (a `.RELIABLE` class alongside `.RECONVERGENT`, in a 2-bit encoding field), uniform-branch instructions (`BRA.U` for provably non-divergent branches — 23 occurrences on Blackwell, zero on Ampere/Hopper), and explicit partial-mask warp synchronization. Deferred reconvergences beyond the immediate post-dominator fell from 29 cases on Ampere to 2 on Blackwell. Bit-flip experiments suggest the new barrier class is a static compiler classification with no observable runtime effect in their tests.

**Why it matters.** It cleanly separates what you must internalize (the stable 32/k linear cost model — a design constraint for any branchy kernel) from what is implementation detail (the reconvergence ISA, which changes every few generations). It also documents that NVIDIA's compiler is now *statically distinguishing uniform from divergent control flow* — meaning the "make your branches uniform" advice from §1.3 is becoming a compiler-visible, first-class distinction in the ISA. The authors are careful to note (and we should too) that the two-tier barrier finding rests on SASS reverse engineering, not vendor documentation.

**What to watch next.** Whether `BRA.U` and partial-mask sync expand in future ISAs, and whether the deferred-reconvergence trend continues — both affect anyone reading SASS to debug divergence.

### 2.2 Blackwell microbenchmarks: the warp scheduler re-tuned for low-precision, high-ILP work — and warp-synchronous matrix instructions retired

**What happened.** Two microbenchmark dissections of NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture appeared (arXiv:2507.10789, comparing an RTX 5080/GB203 against an H100 PCIe; and arXiv:2512.02189, an in-depth architectural analysis), joined by NVIDIA's own Blackwell Tuning Guide (CUDA 12.8).

**What was technically demonstrated.** The comparative study measures, per SM: Hopper GH100 has 128 FP32 + 64 INT32 + 64 FP64 units; consumer Blackwell GB203 **unifies INT32/FP32** and nearly eliminates FP64 (2 units/SM), while both keep a 256 KB register file. On scheduling behavior: "Blackwell's warp scheduler is optimized for low-precision, high-ILP workloads with clean control flow, while Hopper relies on bulk concurrency and deeper buffering to maintain performance under less regular conditions" — Blackwell reduces warp dispatch latency for divergent workloads, while GH100's larger unified L1/shared (256 KB vs 128 KB) gives it the edge for highly threaded kernels with dense reuse. The Tuning Guide confirms 64 warps/SM on CC 10.0 (48 on CC 12.0), 64K registers, 255 max per thread, 32 block slots. The second study documents a structural change directly relevant to §1.4: Blackwell's fifth-generation tensor path replaces warp-synchronous MMA — where all 32 threads (Hopper `wgmma`: a whole 128-thread warp group) had to synchronize before a matrix op — with `tcgen05.mma`, a **single-thread-issued** instruction feeding a new 256 KB per-SM Tensor Memory (TMEM, structured as 512 columns × 128 lanes). (These are preprint measurements of undocumented behavior, not vendor specs — treat specifics as paper claims.)

**Why it matters.** This is the §1.1–§1.6 through-line evolving in real time: NVIDIA is shifting the latency-hiding burden from *bulk warp concurrency* toward *per-thread throughput, ILP, and asynchronous single-thread engines*. The same register budget and warp residency, but a scheduler tuned to keep few, wide, clean instruction streams moving rather than many ragged ones. And the retirement of warp-synchronous MMA is synchronization architecture (§1.4) in action: synchronizing 128 threads around a matrix instruction was costing idle cycles exactly the way a barrier does, so the hardware deleted the requirement.

**What to watch next.** Whether Hopper-style "occupancy as insurance" kernels regress on Blackwell-class parts, and how warp-specialized producer/consumer code (now decoupled from warp-group MMA) restructures around TMEM and single-thread TMA-style issue.
