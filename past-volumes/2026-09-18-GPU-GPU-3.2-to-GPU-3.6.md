## 0. Concise Summary

Yesterday we looked at the arithmetic pipelines as pipes of different widths. Today we examine what actually flows through them: **numbers**, and the hardware that multiplies them. This is the compute datapath of a modern GPU, and it is the layer where most of the last decade's performance gains actually came from — not from more cores or higher clocks, but from *changing what the hardware computes with*.

The lesson moves through five connected ideas. First, **floating-point formats are hardware budgets**: an FP64 multiplier is vastly larger and hungrier than an FP16 one, so a fixed transistor budget buys 8–16× more low-precision multipliers than FP64 ones — which is exactly why a GPU's peak throughput table reads like an inverse price list for mantissa bits. Second, the **fused multiply-add** is the atom of scientific computing: one rounding instead of two, the unit in which FLOPs are counted, and the seed of mixed-precision methods like HPL-MxP. Third, a **warp/wavefront instruction executes across parallel lanes** inside the core — 32 NVIDIA lanes, four 16-lane cycles for an AMD wavefront, a 512-bit Intel vector engine — and understanding that mapping explains vectorized loads, predication, and issue pressure. Fourth, **matrix engines (Tensor Cores, Matrix Cores, XMX)** are not "faster FPUs" but datapaths *restructured around operand reuse*: each fetched element feeds many multipliers, which is why they outrun the scalar pipes by an order of magnitude. Fifth, a matrix instruction has a precise **dataflow contract** — fragments in registers, tiles staged through shared memory, accumulators that live across the whole K-loop — and Blackwell's new Tensor Memory is the latest step in a nine-year evolution of that contract.

The key developments all stress the same point from different directions: NVIDIA's Vera Rubin platform (announced at GTC and ISC 2026) pushes NVFP4 — a 4-bit format with 16-element block scaling — to 50 petaFLOPS per GPU while re-emphasizing native FP64 for science; cuBLAS now *emulates* FP64 matrix multiplication on INT8 tensor cores; and independent microbenchmark studies have begun measuring what Blackwell's FP4/FP6 units actually deliver (~96% of instruction-level peak in dense MMA chains) and whether NVFP4's finer-grained scaling genuinely beats the OCP MXFP4 standard. The format race, not the clock race, is where accelerator architecture now happens.

## 1. Learn the Concepts

### 1.1 Floating-point execution: formats as hardware budgets (GPU-3.2)

Every IEEE 754 floating-point value is a triple of bit fields:

```
value = (−1)^s × 1.m × 2^(e − bias)      (normal numbers)
```

The **exponent** field buys *dynamic range* — how many orders of magnitude you can span. The **mantissa** field buys *relative precision* — how finely you resolve a value within its magnitude. FP32 spends 8 bits on exponent and 23 on mantissa; FP64 doubles both to 11 and 52. Neither field is free: the heart of any FP unit is the mantissa multiplier, whose area and energy grow roughly with the *square* of the mantissa width, plus an alignment shifter and adder sized by the accumulator. An FP64 multiply-accumulate datapath is not "twice" an FP32 one — it is several times larger, which is the entire explanation for why GPU specification sheets look the way they do.

Here is the format landscape of a modern accelerator, including the "community" sub-8-bit formats now baked into silicon:

| Format | Sign | Exponent | Mantissa | Bits | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP64 | 1 | 11 | 52 | 64 | IEEE binary64; HPL, most CFD/physics |
| FP32 | 1 | 8 | 23 | 32 | the default of scientific code |
| TF32 | 1 | 8 | 10 | 19 | FP32's range, FP16's mantissa; A100+ tensor cores |
| BF16 | 1 | 8 | 7 | 16 | FP32's range, very coarse mantissa; training favorite |
| FP16 | 1 | 5 | 10 | 16 | fine mantissa, narrow range (±65,504) |
| FP8 E4M3 | 1 | 4 | 3 | 8 | ±448 max; inference-leaning |
| FP8 E5M2 | 1 | 5 | 2 | 8 | more range; training-leaning |
| FP6 E3M2 / E2M3 | 1 | 3 / 2 | 2 / 3 | 6 | MX-specified pair |
| FP4 E2M1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 4 | 15 values, max ±6, no Inf/NaN |
| E8M0 | — | 8 | 0 | 8 | power-of-two *scale*, not a value format |

Read the throughput columns of real datasheets and the pattern is unmistakable. On the H100 SXM datasheet: 34 TFLOPS FP64 (67 on the FP64 tensor cores), 67 TFLOPS FP32, ~495 TFLOPS dense TF32 tensor core, ~990 TFLOPS dense FP16/BF16, ~1,979 TFLOPS dense FP8 (NVIDIA's headline numbers double these with structured sparsity — a footnote you must always check). On the AMD side, the CDNA 3 whitepaper expresses the same idea per compute unit: 256 FP64, 1,024 TF32, 2,048 FP16/BF16, and 4,096 FP8 or INT8 FLOPS-per-clock-per-CU on the matrix cores. Every step down in precision roughly doubles throughput, because it roughly halves the width — and thus the area — of the multiplier arrays the vendor can afford to place.

**Why the format zoo exists.** FP16 has a fine mantissa but a narrow range: gradients in deep learning can underflow its minimum normal (2⁻¹⁴), which is why Google's TPU popularized BF16 — trading 3 mantissa bits for 3 exponent bits so activations and gradients keep FP32's range at 16-bit cost. TF32, introduced with the A100, is the same trick aimed at HPC: the tensor cores *read FP32 operands*, round them internally to a 19-bit format (8-bit exponent, 10-bit mantissa — the A100 whitepaper's exact definition), and produce a standard IEEE FP32 result. That makes TF32 acceleration invisible to legacy FP32 code, which is also why it is a *reproducibility* hazard for HPC: two runs of the "same" FP32 code, one with TF32 enabled and one without, differ at the 10-bit-mantissa level. For codes where bit-exact FP32 semantics matter, TF32 must be explicitly disabled.

**FP8 and below.** The two FP8 encodings (E4M3, E5M2) were standardized by the OCP 8-bit Floating Point specification and adopted identically by NVIDIA (Hopper) and AMD (MI300X) — a rare moment of cross-vendor agreement. FP6 and FP4 come from the follow-on OCP **Microscaling (MX) v1.0** specification (September 2023), which defines more than bare encodings: it defines *block-scaled* container formats. The problem it solves is that a lone 4-bit value with a ±6 range is useless for a tensor whose values span six orders of magnitude. MX groups elements into blocks of **k = 32**, stores each element as FP4/FP6/FP8/INT8, and attaches one shared 8-bit **E8M0 scale** (a pure power of two) per block. The effective value is `element × 2^scale`, so each 32-element block gets its own exponent window. AMD's MI350-class hardware implements MX formats natively; NVIDIA's Blackwell implements both the OCP MX family and its own **NVFP4** variant — FP4 (E2M1) elements in blocks of **16**, with an **E4M3 FP8 scale** per block (fractional scales, not just powers of two) plus an FP32 per-tensor second-level scale. NVFP4 costs 4.5 bits per element versus MXFP4's 4.25; what that extra quarter-bit buys is the subject of one of today's developments.

Two practical notes. First, subnormal (denormal) support: the MX spec *requires* gradual underflow in FP4 and roundTiesToEven rounding, because at these widths every representable value matters. Second, precision choice is as much a **memory** decision as a compute one: Blackwell's technical brief explicitly pitches FP4 as doubling "parameter bandwidth to HBM" — halving bytes moved per parameter is worth as much as doubling multipliers on inference workloads that are bandwidth-limited. We treat the accuracy side of this trade-off systematically in GPU-3.7 next week; today's job is the mechanism.

### 1.2 Fused multiply-add: the atom of scientific computing (GPU-3.3)

The single most executed instruction in dense linear algebra is not a multiply or an add but both at once:

```
FMA:  d = fl(a·b + c)        — one rounding at the end
vs.   d = fl(fl(a·b) + c)    — two roundings (mul, then add)
```

An FMA unit computes the product `a·b` **exactly** (the full-width product, before rounding), adds `c` to it exactly, and rounds only the final result — so its error is at most ½ ulp of the answer, versus up to a full ulp-plus for separate multiply and add. This "exact product" property is not just about accuracy: knowing that `a·b` is exact lets you *recover the rounding error* of a multiplication (the classic `TwoProd` trick: compute `e = fma(a, b, −ab)` and `e` is exactly the error that the rounded product `ab` discarded). Error-free transformations built from FMAs underpin compensated summation, extended-precision software libraries, and — directly relevant to this curriculum — the mixed-precision iterative-refinement idea behind **HPL-MxP**: factor in low precision (FP16/BF16/INT8 tensor cores), then use high-precision (FP64) residual corrections, each correction itself a chain of FMAs, to converge to FP64-quality answers.

Why dedicate hardware to it? Because of workload structure: inner products, matrix multiply, convolutions, polynomial evaluation — essentially all of dense numerical computing — have the form "accumulate products." If your multiplier must exist anyway, wiring its full-width product directly into the adder tree (instead of round-tripping through a register) costs little and removes half the rounding events and half the instruction traffic. Every vendor's FP pipe is an FMA pipe: CUDA's `FFMA`, AMD's `v_fma_f32`, Intel's `DP4A`-adjacent vector FMAs.

**FLOP counting.** Performance in HPC is denominated in floating-point *operations*, and the convention is:

- 1 FMA = **2 FLOPs** (one multiply + one add), even though it is one instruction;
- GEMM `C = A·B` with `A ∈ R^(m×k)`, `B ∈ R^(k×n)` = **2·m·n·k** FLOPs;
- LU factorization (the heart of HPL) ≈ **⅔n³ + O(n²)** FLOPs, which is why HPL's reported FLOP/s is `(⅔n³ + O(n²)) / time`.

This convention is also how you derive theoretical peaks (a preview of GPU-3.8). The A100 whitepaper's peak FP32 of 19.5 TFLOPS is nothing more than:

```
108 SMs × 64 FP32 FMA/clk/SM × 2 FLOPs/FMA × 1.41 GHz ≈ 19.5 TFLOPS
```

and its dense FP16 tensor-core peak of 312 TFLOPS is the same arithmetic with 1,024 FP16 FMAs per SM per clock — a 16× ratio over FP32 in identical silicon, achieved purely by narrowing the multipliers. When you see a GPU deliver 10 PFLOPS of FP4 (a GB200-class Blackwell GPU's dense rate, per the technical brief), that number is *made of the same FMAs*, just 4-bit-wide ones — thousands of tiny multipliers where a few fat ones used to be.

One subtle point for correctness: floating-point FMA is neither associative nor commutative, and reordered accumulation changes results. Tensor cores accumulate in a different order (and precision) than a scalar FMA loop, so mixed-precision results are reproducible only if you pin the instruction sequence — the reason libraries like cuBLAS expose explicit algorithm controls.

### 1.3 Vector/SIMD execution inside a GPU core (GPU-3.4)

A warp (NVIDIA), wavefront (AMD), or subgroup (Intel) instruction is executed by a **datapath of parallel lanes** inside the core. The mapping from threads to lanes to cycles is invisible in the programming model but shapes everything downstream.

**NVIDIA.** Each SM partition owns its FP32/INT32 lanes. An Ampere-class partition has 16 dedicated FP32 lanes plus 16 lanes that execute either FP32 or INT32; a 32-thread warp instruction therefore occupies a 16-lane unit for **2 cycles**, or is co-issued across both unit groups in 1 cycle when the mix allows (this is the hardware behind Ampere's "concurrent FP32 and INT32 execution"). A Hopper/Blackwell SM has 128 FP32 lanes total across its four partitions. The lanes share one program counter and one predicate mask: when a warp is partially diverged (GPU-2.7), inactive lanes are predicated off — they produce no results but still occupy the datapath slots, which is why divergence wastes throughput exactly in proportion to the inactive fraction.

**AMD.** A CDNA Compute Unit contains four **SIMD units, each 16 lanes wide**. A 64-thread wavefront instruction executes as **four consecutive machine cycles** on one SIMD unit — a wavefront is, in effect, a software-visible 4-cycle vector instruction. The CU's 64 FP32 lanes across its four SIMDs give the per-CU vector rates we quoted above (256 FP32 FLOPs/clk/CU).

**Intel.** An Xe-core of Xe-HPC contains 8 vector engines, each **512 bits wide** = 16 FP32 lanes with fused FMAs (128 FP64, 256 FP32, 512 FP16 ops/clk per Xe-core). The Xe subgroup is 16 threads for compute — one subgroup instruction maps cleanly onto one vector-engine pass.

The practical consequence for a GPU programmer is **vectorization within a thread**. Nothing forces a thread to touch one element at a time: loading `float4` (128 bits) per thread issues one wide memory transaction per thread instead of four narrow ones, and packing two FP16 values per 32-bit register halves instruction count for half-precision kernels. Consider saxpy `y[i] = a·x[i] + y[i]`:

```
scalar:   per 1 element: 1× LDG.32, 1× LDG.32, 1× FFMA, 1× STG.32   (4 instructions)
float4:   per 4 elements: 1× LDG.128, 1× LDG.128, 4× FFMA, 1× STG.128 (7 instructions → 1.75/element)
```

The FFMA count is irreducible (the math must happen), but the load/store and issue overhead per element drops 4×. Across a warp, 32 threads × 128 bits = 512 bytes per instruction — exactly how coalescing (GPU-4.7) turns per-thread access patterns into efficient memory transactions. Vector width is the meeting point of the execution model and the memory system.

### 1.4 Matrix and tensor execution units: datapaths restructured around reuse (GPU-3.5)

Everything so far executes *one* multiply per lane per cycle-pass. Matrix engines break that rule — and understanding *why* requires looking at what limits a SIMT datapath on dense linear algebra.

In `C += A·B`, every element of A is used N times and every element of B is used M times. On a conventional pipeline, each FMA must fetch two operands from the register file, multiply once, and write back — so the register file bandwidth and instruction issue rate, not the multipliers, become the bottleneck. A **matrix engine** rearranges the datapath so each fetched operand element fans out to *many* multipliers simultaneously (an outer-product / systolic-style array), with an accumulation tree attached. One instruction then retires hundreds or thousands of FMAs, amortizing issue and operand movement to nearly nothing. That is the entire trick — plus the willingness to accept fixed shapes, fixed layouts, and reduced precision, which is what makes the area math work.

**NVIDIA's four-plus generations.** Volta (2017) introduced the first Tensor Core: 640 of them on V100 (8 per SM), each performing `D = A×B + C` on **4×4×4 matrices** with FP16 inputs and FP32 accumulation — 64 FMAs per clock per core, ~125 tensor TFLOPS aggregate. Ampere (A100) made the third generation dramatically more general: TF32 for FP32 workloads, BF16, IEEE-compliant **FP64 tensor cores** (64 FP64 FMAs/clk/SM — real HPC support), and **fine-grained 2:4 structured sparsity** — if at most 2 of every 4 elements are nonzero (stored in a compressed format with metadata), the tensor core skips the zeros and doubles effective throughput. Hopper (H100) added FP8 and made the matrix instruction *asynchronous and warpgroup-wide* (`wgmma`). Blackwell (2024–26) is the fifth generation: FP4 and FP6 support, microscaling formats in hardware, and a restructured instruction path (`tcgen05`) that we detail in 1.5.

**AMD.** Matrix Cores sit inside each Compute Unit — 4 per CU, 1,216 on an MI300X (304 CUs). The instruction family is **MFMA** (matrix FMA), and CDNA 3 tripled FP16/BF16 throughput and multiplied INT8 throughput 6.8× versus MI250X, adding FP8, TF32, and the same 2:4 sparsity discipline (dense 4,096 INT8 ops/clk/CU doubles to 8,192 with sparsity). Crucially for HPC, AMD kept matrix FP64 at the same rate as matrix FP32 (256 FLOPs/clk/CU) — MI300X delivers 163.4 TFLOPS of *matrix* FP64, far above any current NVIDIA GPU's native FP64 tensor throughput.

**Intel.** The Xe-HPC **XMX** engine, documented in the Ponte Vecchio ISSCC paper, is an explicitly *systolic* design: each of the 8 matrix engines per Xe-core contains an 8-deep systolic array executing 4096-bit-wide operations per engine per clock — 4,096 FP16/BF16 or 8,192 INT8 ops/clk/Xe-core. It is programmed through the `DPAS` (dot-product-accumulate-systolic) instruction, exposed in SYCL as `joint_matrix` operations executed by a 16-thread subgroup.

The cross-vendor terminology map:

| Concept | NVIDIA | AMD | Intel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compute block | SM | CU | Xe-core |
| Matrix engine | Tensor Core (5th gen) | Matrix Core | XMX engine |
| Matrix instruction | `mma.sync` / `wgmma` / `tcgen05.mma` | `MFMA` | `DPAS` |
| SASS-level op | HMMA / QMMA / OMMA | — | — |
| Warp-scale group | warp (32) / warpgroup (128) | wavefront (64) | subgroup (16) |

Why do matrix engines *differ* from FP pipelines, concretely? Four reasons: (1) **macro-instructions** — one issued instruction performs thousands of FMAs over multiple cycles, so issue bandwidth stops mattering; (2) **operand sharing** — a tile element is read once and fanned out, instead of being re-read from the register file per FMA; (3) **wide, single-rounding accumulators** — FP16 inputs multiply exactly and accumulate in FP32, giving 2mnk FLOPs whose *results* are often more accurate than naive FP16 code would suggest; and (4) **fixed data layouts** — the price of the fan-out network is that operands must arrive in the exact fragment layout the hardware expects, pushing layout complexity to software (CUTLASS, Triton, rocBLAS, oneMKL).

### 1.5 Matrix instruction dataflow: tiles, fragments, and accumulators (GPU-3.6)

How does a warp actually execute a matrix instruction, and where does the data live? Take the workhorse of Ampere-era kernels, `mma.sync.aligned.m16n8k16` (FP16 inputs, FP32 accumulate):

- **Shape:** M=16, N=8, K=16 — the warp computes a 16×8 output tile, contracting over 16.
- **Fragments:** the hardware assigns each of the 32 threads a fixed slice of the operands: 8 FP16 A-elements (4× 32-bit registers), 4 FP16 B-elements (2 registers), and 4 FP32 accumulator elements (4 registers). The fragment layout — *which* thread holds *which* (row, column) — is a hardware contract, not a choice.
- **Work:** 16×8×16 = 2,048 multiply-accumulates = **4,096 FLOPs per warp instruction**, retiring over several cycles inside the tensor core.

No thread "knows" it is doing matrix math; the programmer (or a library) must place data in shared memory such that the `ldmatrix` instruction can distribute elements into exactly those fragment positions. This is the **layout contract** that makes hand-written tensor-core code hard and CUTLASS valuable.

Now zoom out to a whole GEMM tile. The canonical warp-tiled dataflow:

```
global memory :  A (M×K)                 B (K×N)
                    │   TMA (Hopper+) / cp.async (Ampere)
                    ▼                     ▼
shared memory :  A_tile (64×16)       B_tile (16×64)     ← double-buffered
                    │  ldmatrix (fragment layout)        │
                    ▼                                     ▼
registers     :  a_frag 8×fp16/thread            b_frag 4×fp16/thread
                    └──────────────┬──────────────┘
                                   ▼
                     mma.sync.m16n8k16  (×2 per 16×16 warp tile per k-step)
                                   ▼
registers     :  c_frag — 8×fp32/thread  ← ACCUMULATOR LIVES HERE ACROSS ALL K
                                   ▼  after K/16 k-steps
shared/global :  C_tile (64×64)                    ← epilogue (scale, bias, store)
```

The K-loop is where all the economics live. Follow the reuse hierarchy for one warp computing a 16×16 output tile: each k-step (16 wide) loads 16×16 A-elements and 16×16 B-elements (8+8 per thread) and performs 2×(16·8·16·2) = 8,192 FLOPs — an intensity of **16 FLOPs per loaded element**. But the A_tile in shared memory is shared by *every* warp in the block: at block-tile 64×64 the intensity rises to **64 FLOPs/element**, and at 128×128 to **128**. Each level of tiling converts more loads into reuse, and the accumulator registers — held across the entire K dimension — are the innermost level of all. This is the quantitative answer to "why tile GEMMs," and it is also why register pressure (GPU-5.3) limits tile sizes: a 128×128 FP32 accumulator tile per block is 16 KB of registers — a large fraction of a partition's file.

**Hopper's restructure: `wgmma`.** Hopper made the MMA instruction *asynchronous* and widened the issuing unit from a warp (32 threads) to a **warpgroup (128 threads)**. Operands A and B are read directly from shared memory through 64-bit **matrix descriptors** (encoded start address, leading dimension, swizzle mode) rather than through per-thread registers; the warpgroup issues `wgmma.m64nNk16` (N up to 256) and continues executing other instructions while the tensor core chews. Accumulators still live in per-thread registers.

**Blackwell's restructure: `tcgen05` and Tensor Memory.** Blackwell breaks the warp-synchronous tradition entirely. A `tcgen05.mma` is issued by a **single thread**; the hardware distributes work across the SM's tensor cores itself. Accumulators move out of the register file into **Tensor Memory (TMEM)** — a dedicated on-chip memory (hundreds of KB per SM) beside the tensor cores, allocated and managed explicitly with `tcgen05` instructions. Tiles grow (M up to 128, or 256 when two CTAs execute as a cooperating pair; N up to 256, per the PTX ISA), and a pair of CTAs can share operands over the SM-pair network to halve redundant shared-memory traffic. The direction across all five generations is one straight line: **operands move closer to the matrix engines, the issuing scope widens, and each instruction covers a larger tile** — from 4×4×4 in 2017 to 128×256-class today.

AMD's MFMA and Intel's DPAS sit at analogous points: MFMA accumulates in vector registers with operands fed from the LDS (shared memory) via fragment descriptors; DPAS accumulates in the subgroup's general registers. Every vendor walks the same path because the physics is the same: the matrix engine is only as fast as the tile-staging machinery feeding it — which is why TMA, shared-memory swizzling, and double buffering are inseparable from tensor-core performance. This staging machinery (asynchronous movement, multicast, clusters) gets its own curriculum topics in module 10; today's takeaway is the *contract*: **global → shared (tiles) → registers/TMEM (fragments and accumulators) → matrix engine**, with reuse extracted at every level.

Where you meet this in practice: cuBLAS/rocBLAS/oneMKL select tile shapes and instruction variants for you (and their autotuning is doing exactly the intensity arithmetic above); CUTLASS and Triton expose the levels explicitly (`tl.dot` lowers to `mma`/`wgmma`/`tcgen05`); HPL's DGEMM panels live or die on it; and HPL-MxP is precisely this dataflow run with FP16/BF16/INT8 fragments and an FP64 correction loop around the outside — mixed-precision iterative refinement at 10⁶-core scale.

## 2. Key Developments / News

### 2.1 Vera Rubin: 50 PFLOPS of NVFP4 per GPU — and a native-FP64 pitch for science

**What happened.** Through 2026 NVIDIA has been rolling out the Vera Rubin platform: the Rubin GPU with Vera CPU, presented at GTC 2026 (March) and, at ISC High Performance 2026, positioned explicitly as a *scientific computing* platform with system announcements from Bull, Dell Technologies, GIGABYTE, HPE, and Supermicro. Los Alamos's forthcoming "Veritas" system was announced as a Rubin-GPU machine.

**What technically changed.** The Rubin GPU's headline compute figure is a **50-petaFLOPS Transformer Engine built on fifth-generation tensor cores running NVFP4** — 4-bit elements with 16-element E4M3 block scaling (yesterday's science project, now the default unit of accelerator marketing). A Vera Rubin NVL72 rack is listed at 3.6 exaFLOPS NVFP4, 1.6 PB/s of HBM4 bandwidth, and 260 TB/s of sixth-generation NVLink. Simultaneously, NVIDIA is stressing **native FP64**: the ISC announcement emphasizes ~5 petaFLOPS of native FP64 per rack-scale system and FP64 tensor-core support for simulation workloads — a direct answer to the HPC community's unease about Blackwell's FP64 positioning (the GB200 datasheet lists 40 TFLOPS FP64 tensor-core per GPU, *below* H100's 67 TFLOPS, as transistor budget shifted to low-precision arrays).

**Why it matters.** The same architecture now serves two masters: AI factories that want the cheapest possible FLOP (FP4), and simulation users who need the most trustworthy one (FP64). That a single tensor-core generation must serve both — at opposite ends of the format table from Section 1.1 — is the clearest evidence that the format stack, not the SM count, is now the primary design axis of accelerator silicon.

**Watch next.** How much of HPL-class FP64 throughput on Rubin comes from native units versus the emulation path below; and whether NVFP4 (a NVIDIA-specific container) or OCP MX formats become the portable standard across vendors — AMD's roadmaps implement MX.

### 2.2 cuBLAS now emulates FP64 matrix multiplication on INT8 tensor cores

**What happened.** In CUDA Toolkit 13.0 Update 2 (announced October 2025), cuBLAS introduced **FP64 GEMM emulation on Blackwell's INT8 tensor cores**, complementing the earlier FP32 emulation on BF16 tensor cores.

**What technically changed.** The FP32 path, **BF16x9**, decomposes each FP32 operand into multiple BF16 pieces whose tensor-core products recombine exactly; the FP64 path uses an **Ozaki scheme** — splitting FP64 matrices into INT8-representable pieces, running many exact INT8 tensor-core products, and summing the cross-products in FP64. NVIDIA reports Quantum ESPRESSO speeding up 1.5× end-to-end (nearly 3× in a 39-mantissa-bit mode, with results matching to 12 significant digits) and ecTrans SGEMM 2.4× via BF16x9.

**Why it matters.** This is Section 1.1's area economics taken to its logical end: rather than spend scarce silicon on 52-bit multipliers, spend it on thousands of 8-bit ones and *buy precision back with more operations*. It is the same philosophical bet as HPL-MxP — low-precision bulk math plus high-precision correction — now implemented *inside* the BLAS library, transparently, with accuracy "equivalent to or better than native" claimed by default and fallback available. For HPC users it blurs the line between "the GPU has FP64 units" and "the GPU computes FP64 GEMMs fast."

**Watch next.** Emulated-FP64 DGEMM efficiency at HPL panel sizes versus the native FP64 path (the extra splits cost bandwidth and bookkeeping — the scheme only wins when the INT8 arrays are wide enough); and whether the technique appears in vendor-optimized HPL builds.

### 2.3 Independent studies put Blackwell's tensor cores — and the 4-bit formats — under the microscope

**What happened.** Two 2025–26 academic efforts examined the hardware this curriculum describes. A University of Delaware team (arXiv:2512.02189) published the first systematic microbenchmark suite for Blackwell B200, measuring the memory subsystem, `tcgen05` tensor pipeline, and every precision from FP64 down to FP4. A separate study (arXiv:2509.23202 and follow-ons) analyzed the *numerical design space* of MXFP4 versus NVFP4 quantization on real language models.

**What was demonstrated.** On the hardware side: B200 sustains roughly **96% of instruction-level peak** in dense MMA dependency chains — ~5.1 PFLOPS FP6 and ~7.7 PFLOPS FP4 measured on their unit at `m64n8k16` shapes (single-instruction dependent latency ~12.6 cycles), with ~1.27× H200 throughput on FP8/FP16/INT8 and ~1.9× on FP64 DGEMM — confirming that the new engines are issue-limited only at tiny tiles and that the FP4/FP6 datapaths are real, not paper. On the numerical side: NVFP4's 16-element blocks with E4M3 scales preserve outlier values and achieve lower round-to-nearest quantization error than MXFP4's 32-element power-of-two-scaled blocks, at the cost of 4.5 versus 4.25 bits per element and more scale-handling logic; interestingly, Hadamard-rotation preprocessing *helps* MXFP4 and INT4 but *hurts* NVFP4, whose finer scaling already captures what the rotation redistributes.

**Why it matters.** These are exactly the trade-offs Section 1.1 predicts, now quantified independently of vendor marketing: block size and scale precision are a genuine accuracy-per-bit frontier, and hardware area studies suggest E4M3 scaling costs ~13% more tensor-core area than E8M0 at equal block size. The reader now has the concepts to read such results critically — which is the entire point of this module.

**Watch next.** MI350-class and Rubin hardware results in MLPerf and independent benchmarks, where MXFP4 (AMD, portable) and NVFP4 (NVIDIA, finer) will compete on *delivered accuracy per FLOP*, not just peak.
